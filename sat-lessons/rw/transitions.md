## What this question looks like

A *transition* is a word or phrase that shows how one idea connects to another, such as *however*, *therefore* or *for example*. Transition questions give you a short text (25 to 150 words) with one blank and always ask the same question: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?"

- The blank is usually at the start of the last sentence. Sometimes it comes after a semicolon (;), or inside a sentence between commas.
- All four choices are transitions with the same punctuation. Grammar never decides the answer; logic does.
- On 2026 tests, students report longer choices: whole phrases such as "Despite this limitation," or "To address this problem," instead of single words. The method is the same, but you must check every word of the phrase.
- **Where and how many:** transitions come near the end of each Reading and Writing module, after the grammar questions and just before the notes questions. Transitions and notes questions together are 8 to 12 scored questions per test, about 20% of the section.

The sub-types are the relationships between ideas: contrast, concession, addition, similarity, cause and effect, example, clarification, emphasis, time order and summary. The table below explains each one.

## The method

1. **Cover the choices.** If you read them first, every one of them starts to "sound fine."
2. **Read the whole text once.** It is short. Know its main point.
3. **Find the two ideas the blank connects.** Usually these are the sentence before the blank (idea 1) and the sentence with the blank (idea 2). After a semicolon, they are the two halves of the same sentence.
4. **Name the relationship with a simple word.** Say "idea 1, *but* idea 2" (contrast), "idea 1, *so* idea 2" (result), "idea 1, *for example* idea 2", "idea 1, *and also* idea 2", "idea 1, *in fact* idea 2" (stronger) or "idea 1, *then* idea 2" (time). Your simple word is your prediction.
5. **Uncover the choices and sort them into families.** Cross out every choice whose family doesn't match your prediction.
6. **Cancel twins.** Only one answer can be right, so if two choices mean the same thing (such as *therefore* and *consequently*), both are wrong.
7. **Read the sentence with your answer in it.** It should make sense without any extra assumptions. For a phrase, check every word: "Despite this limitation" needs a limitation in idea 1.

> **Tip:** Your prediction doesn't need to be the exact word in the answer. "But" is enough to find *however*, *nevertheless* or *by contrast*. You only need the family.

## Rules and formulas you need

### The relationship families

| Relationship | What idea 2 does | Common transitions |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | says something opposite to idea 1, or opposite to what you expected | however, by contrast, in contrast, on the other hand, conversely, instead, rather, yet |
| Concession: admit a point | admits a fact that seems to weaken the main claim | granted, admittedly, of course, to be sure |
| Concession: despite that | keeps the main claim even though idea 1 is a problem | nevertheless, nonetheless, still, even so, that said, regardless, despite this |
| Addition (continuation) | adds another point in the same direction, about the same subject | moreover, furthermore, in addition, additionally, also, besides, what's more |
| Similarity | says a different thing works the same way | similarly, likewise, in the same way, by the same token |
| Cause and effect | is a result of idea 1, or a response to it | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, accordingly, hence, for this reason |
| Example | gives one specific case of the general idea | for example, for instance, to illustrate |
| Clarification or restatement | says idea 1 again in other words, or more exactly | in other words, that is, specifically, namely, in particular |
| Emphasis | makes idea 1 stronger, often with a surprising detail | in fact, indeed, actually |
| Time order (sequence) | happens before, after or at the same time as idea 1 | then, next, later, afterward, subsequently, previously, meanwhile, eventually |
| Conclusion or summary | sums up the text or gives the final outcome | in sum, in short, overall, ultimately, in conclusion |

### Words that trick students

- **However vs. instead.** *Instead* means one thing replaced another: "They didn't walk. Instead, they took the bus."
- **For example vs. similarly.** *For example* means idea 2 is one case inside idea 1's group. *Similarly* means idea 2 is about a different thing that behaves the same way.
- **Moreover vs. similarly.** *Moreover* adds a new point about the same subject. *Similarly* moves to a new subject with the same feature.
- **Therefore vs. in fact.** *Therefore* means idea 1 causes idea 2. *In fact* means idea 2 is a stronger version of idea 1.
- **In fact vs. in other words.** *In other words* repeats the same idea. *In fact* adds a new, stronger detail.
- **Granted vs. nevertheless.** *Granted* introduces the weak point. *Nevertheless* comes after the weak point and returns to the main claim.
- **Still.** At the start of a sentence, *still* means "despite this", not "even now".
- **Meanwhile.** It means "at the same time", often about a different place or group. It can also set two situations side by side as a light contrast.
- **Then.** It usually shows time ("after that"). On its own it does not show a cause.

### What the punctuation tells you

- **Blank after a period** (". ______ "): the transition links the new sentence to the sentence before it.
- **Blank after a semicolon** ("; ______ "): it links the two halves of the same sentence.
- **Blank inside a sentence** (", ______ "): it still links this sentence to the sentence before it. Ignore the words right around the blank.
- All four choices share the same punctuation, so punctuation tells you *where to look*, not which word is right. (Punctuation rules for transitions are tested in a different question type, Boundaries.)

### Phrase-length transitions

Read a phrase in two parts: its family and its details. Both must fit.

- "As a result of this change," is cause and effect, and idea 1 must describe a change.
- "Despite this limitation," is concession, and idea 1 must describe a limitation that idea 2 overcomes.
- "In that same year," is time, and idea 1 must mention a year in which idea 2 also happens.
- "To address this problem," is a response, and idea 1 must describe a problem that idea 2 tries to fix.

A phrase from the right family with a wrong detail is still wrong.

### Patterns from official questions (only for breaking a tie)

Tutors who counted answers in official College Board questions report three patterns: contrast words are the most common choices, restatement choices such as *in other words* are rarely correct, and *in fact* or *indeed* is often correct when it appears. Use these patterns only to break a tie between two choices. Always decide by logic first.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Octopus intelligence

Octopuses belong to the same group of animals as snails and clams, which are not known for their intelligence. Experiments, ______ have shown that octopuses can open jars, find their way through mazes and remember how they solved a problem.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

- (A) however,
- (B) for example,
- (C) similarly,
- (D) therefore,

**Solution.** The blank sits inside the second sentence, but it still links that sentence to the first one. Idea 1: octopuses are related to animals that aren't smart. Idea 2: octopuses are smart. That is "but": a contrast with what you would expect. (A) fits. (B) is wrong because the experiments are not an example of snails being unintelligent. (C) is wrong because the two ideas are not alike. (D) is wrong because being related to snails doesn't cause octopuses to be clever.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 2: Beaver dams

Beavers build dams of sticks and mud across streams, and these dams slow the moving water; ______ ponds form behind the dams, creating homes for fish, frogs and birds.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

- (A) as a result,
- (B) for instance,
- (C) nevertheless,
- (D) meanwhile,

**Solution.** After a semicolon, the transition links the two halves of one sentence. Half 1: the dams slow the water. Half 2: ponds form. Ponds form *because* the water slows down, so the relationship is "so": cause and effect. (A) fits. (B) would make the ponds an example of slow water, which makes no sense. (C) says the ponds form in spite of the dams, but the dams are the reason for the ponds. (D) only says that the two things happen at the same time and loses the cause.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 3: Plant chemicals

Many plants protect themselves by producing chemicals that taste bitter or harm the animals that eat them. ______ coffee plants make caffeine, a bitter substance that can harm insects that feed on the plants' leaves and seeds.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

- (A) Therefore,
- (B) For instance,
- (C) Consequently,
- (D) In other words,

**Solution.** Idea 1 is a general statement about many plants. Idea 2 is about one plant, the coffee plant, doing exactly that. One case of a general idea is an example. Notice that (A) and (C) mean the same thing (a result). Two choices with the same meaning can't both be right, so both are out, and neither fits anyway: the general fact doesn't cause coffee plants to make caffeine. (D) is the tempting trap: both sentences are about plant chemicals, but idea 2 doesn't repeat idea 1 in new words; it adds a specific case with new details (coffee, caffeine, insects). (B) is correct.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: The far side of the Moon

In 1959, a Soviet spacecraft took the first photographs of the far side of the Moon, the side that always faces away from Earth. By today's standards, the images were blurry and grainy. ______ they gave scientists their first look at a part of the Moon that no one had ever seen.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

- (A) Instead,
- (B) Consequently,
- (C) Still,
- (D) In other words,

**Solution.** Idea 1 (the sentence right before the blank): the photos were poor. Idea 2: the photos were valuable anyway. A problem, then a point that holds despite the problem, is a concession. (C) is right: at the start of a sentence, *still* has exactly that meaning. (A) is wrong because *instead* means one thing replaced another, and nothing was replaced. (B) is wrong because blurry pictures didn't cause the first look. (D) is wrong because idea 2 doesn't repeat idea 1; it says something new.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 5: A popular library

Planners expected the town's new library to attract a few hundred visitors in its first month. Instead, it proved far more popular. ______ so many people came on the first day that the staff ran out of library cards by noon.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

- (A) However,
- (B) In fact,
- (C) Similarly,
- (D) In other words,

**Solution.** The text already has one transition: *Instead* links sentence 1 and sentence 2. The blank links sentence 3 to sentence 2. Idea 1: the library was far more popular than expected. Idea 2: on day one, so many people came that the cards ran out. Idea 2 makes idea 1 stronger with a striking detail, which is emphasis, so (B), *in fact*, fits. (A) is the trap for students who link to sentence 1 ("they expected few; however, many came"), but sentence 2 already made that contrast, and there is no contrast between "very popular" and "a huge crowd." (C) is wrong because sentence 3 isn't about a different thing that behaves the same way. (D) is wrong because sentence 3 isn't the same idea in other words; it adds a new, more specific fact.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 6: Streetlights and insects

When a city replaced its old streetlights with bright white LED lights, residents said the streets felt much safer at night. Researchers later found a drawback: the bright light confused moths and other night-flying insects, many of which pollinate plants. ______ several neighborhoods have now switched to dimmer, yellowish LEDs that attract fewer insects.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

- (A) Despite this drawback,
- (B) Just as the researchers predicted,
- (C) To address this problem,
- (D) In addition to this drawback,

**Solution.** All four phrases point back to the drawback, so you must check both the family and the details. Idea 1: bright lights harm insects (a problem). Idea 2: neighborhoods switched to lights that attract fewer insects (an action that responds to the problem). That is cause and effect, in the form of a response. (C) fits, and its details match: idea 1 states a problem and idea 2 tries to fix it. (A) says the switch happened in spite of the drawback, but the drawback is the reason for the switch. (B) adds a prediction that the text never mentions. (D) treats the switch as a second drawback, but it is a solution.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **Choosing by sound.** Every choice can sound smooth. Decide the relationship first, then look at the choices.
- **Linking to the wrong sentence.** The blank usually connects to the sentence right before it. Don't jump back to an earlier sentence that already has its own transition (Example 5).
- **A contrast inside the sentence.** A *but* or *although* inside the blank's sentence doesn't make the transition a contrast word. Compare the whole sentence with the one before it.
- **The "moreover" default.** Addition words fit loosely almost anywhere. Before choosing *moreover* or *in addition*, make sure there isn't a sharper relationship: result, example, contrast or emphasis.
- **The restatement trap.** *In other words* and *that is* need idea 2 to say the same thing as idea 1. If idea 2 adds a new detail, they are wrong.
- **Example vs. similarity.** *For example* introduces a case inside the same group; *similarly* introduces a different thing that acts the same way.
- **Time is not cause.** *Then*, *later* and *meanwhile* only show time. If idea 2 happens *because of* idea 1, you need a cause-and-effect word.
- **Backward concession.** "Despite this…" is wrong when idea 2 happens *because of* idea 1 (Example 6).
- **Phrase details.** A phrase from the right family can still add something false or missing, such as a prediction, a year or a second problem.
- **Synonym twins.** Two choices with the same meaning cancel each other out.
- **Rejecting a word you don't know.** Words like *conversely*, *accordingly* and *still* are often right answers. Learn the table so an unfamiliar word doesn't scare you away from the correct choice.

## Quick checklist

- I covered the choices and named the relationship with my own simple word (but, so, for example, also, in fact, then).
- I linked the right two ideas: the sentence before the blank, or the two halves around a semicolon.
- I crossed out every choice from the wrong family, and any synonym twins.
- For a phrase, every word of it fits the text.
- I read the sentence with my answer in it, and it needs no extra assumptions.
