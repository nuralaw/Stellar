# Main purpose
Say what the whole text does, not what it says.

```visual
text-structure-purpose--turn
```

## Key ideas
A **main purpose** question asks *why* the author wrote the text. The answer is a verb plus an object: *to explain…, to argue…, to correct…, to describe a study…*

1. Give each sentence a job label: claim, example, result, reaction.
2. Find the **turn**: *but*, *however*, *yet*, *actually*, *in reality*. The author's own point usually comes after it.
3. Say the purpose in your own words. Then pick the choice that fits the **whole** text.

*In fact* and *indeed* only add emphasis. They signal a correction only when the sentence before states a common belief.

```example
Most people think the tongue has separate zones for sweet and sour. Actually, researchers have shown that every part of the tongue can sense every taste.
Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?
Choices: To correct a common belief about taste / To explain how sweetness is measured / To compare two studies of taste
1. Sentence 1: a common belief :: Label the job, not the topic.
2. *Actually* turns the text :: The author's own point comes after the turn.
3. Sentence 2: evidence against the belief :: It corrects sentence 1.
4. Answer: To correct a common belief about taste :: A verb that fits the whole text.
```

```theory
**Purpose vs. main idea.** The main idea is *what* the text says. The purpose is *what the text does* for the reader. "Goldfish can remember for months" is an idea. "To correct a belief about goldfish memory" is a purpose.

**What the choices look like.** Most start with *To* plus a verb ("To argue that…"); some start with *It* plus a verb. The verb must describe the whole text, not one sentence, and the words after the verb must match the text too.

**Common jobs.** To explain how or why something happens; to describe a person, place or thing; to argue for a view; to correct a mistaken view; to present a study and its results; to compare two things. In fiction and poetry the verbs are softer: *to convey* a feeling, *to depict* a scene, *to portray* a character.

**Where the point hides.** If the text has a turn, the point sits after it. If it has none, the point is often in the first or last sentence and the rest supports it.

**Tone.** Notice whether the author is neutral, admiring or critical. A choice with the opposite tone is wrong, even if its verb sounds fine.

**Stuck?** You do not have to invent your own wording. Cross out choices whose verb clearly does not fit (a text that only reports results does not "argue"), then compare what is left.
```

# One part's job
Ask what the part does for its neighbors.

```visual
text-structure-purpose--neighbors
```

## Key ideas
A **function** question asks why the author put one sentence or phrase (underlined) in the text. It does not ask what the part says.

Read the sentence **before** and the sentence **after** it. Then ask two things:
- What does the part do to the sentence before it: answer it, give a reason, give an example, push back?
- What does the next sentence do with it: explain it, build on it, correct it?

Clues inside the part: a **colon** brings an explanation or example; **brackets and dashes** usually define or add detail; *could*, *may* and *might* mark a guess; a **question** sets up an answer.

```example
Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor. **Yet about a quarter of all sea species live on them.** Biologists therefore call reefs the rainforests of the sea.
Which choice best describes the function of the underlined (bold) sentence in the text as a whole?
1. Before: reefs are tiny :: Label the neighbor before.
2. Bold: *Yet* + a huge share of species :: A contrast.
3. After: *therefore* a nickname :: The bold fact is the reason.
4. Answer: It gives a contrasting fact that explains the nickname :: The job, not the topic.
```

```theory
**Function** means the job a part does for the whole text. The SAT words it as "the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole". The part can be a whole sentence, a clause, or a short phrase.

**Typical jobs.** It introduces the topic or the claim. It gives background the reader needs. It defines a term. It gives an example or a piece of evidence. It states a reason or cause. It contrasts with what came before. It sets up something that comes later. It answers a question that was just asked.

**Why neighbors matter.** The same sentence can do different jobs in different texts. "Many scientists believed the sea floor was flat" is a belief to be disproved when a correction follows, but it would be background if the text then only described the sea floor. Only the sentences around it tell you which.

**Harder questions** add a second half: "It describes X, which the text goes on to show is Y." Both halves must be true. Check the later part of the text for the second half.

**Phrases.** If the underlined part is a phrase, ask what it adds to the sentence it sits in. A phrase beginning *particularly at a time when…* usually explains why something happened.

**Trap.** Choosing the answer that repeats what the part says. The right answer says what the part *does*.
```

# Job verbs
Learn the verbs that answer choices use.

```visual
text-structure-purpose--verbs
```

## Key ideas
Choices name a part's job with a verb. Learn what each verb asks for:

| Verb | The part… | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| defines | says what a term means | *is*, *refers to*, dashes, brackets |
| illustrates | gives an example | *for example*, *such as*, a named case |
| supports | gives evidence | numbers, results, *because* |
| concedes | admits a point to the other side | *admittedly*, *although*, *while* |
| qualifies | limits a claim | *usually*, *only*, *though* |
| challenges | pushes back | *but*, *however*, *yet* |

Read the part, say in plain words what it does, then pick the verb that says the same. A verb is only half the choice: the words after it must also match the text.

```example
Wind farms are often criticized as noisy. **Admittedly, a turbine close to a house can hum.** But at 500 meters, the sound is quieter than a refrigerator.
Which choice best describes the function of the underlined (bold) sentence?
Choices: It concedes a point to critics. / It refutes the claim that wind farms are noisy. / It illustrates how quiet turbines are.
1. Sentence 1: critics say turbines are noisy :: The claim being tested.
2. Bold: *Admittedly* + a small agreement :: It gives critics a point.
3. After: *But* + quiet at 500 m :: The author pushes back.
4. Answer: It concedes a point to critics :: It admits a point.
```

```theory
**More jobs you will meet.**
- *Provides background*: context a reader needs before the argument starts.
- *Introduces* or *sets up*: opens a topic or prepares what comes next.
- *Elaborates*: adds detail to the sentence before.
- *Describes a method*: says how a study was done. *Presents a finding*: says what it showed. *States a hypothesis*: gives a guess the study will test.
- *Poses a question*: usually answered in the next lines. *Proposes a solution*: answers a problem.
- *Draws a contrast*: sets two things against each other.

**Fiction and poetry** use gentler verbs: *conveys* a mood, *depicts* a scene, *portrays* a character, *reveals* a feeling.

**Matching verb to part.** Read the part, say in plain words what it does, then find the verb that says the same. Cross out verbs whose job is absent: if nothing in the part gives an example, *illustrates* is wrong. A verb is only half the choice, so the words after it (what is being conceded, defined or supported) must also match the text.

**Near neighbors.** *Illustrates* and *supports* both involve examples, but *supports* needs evidence for a claim, while *illustrates* just shows a case. *Defines* needs a term; if no term is explained, it is wrong. *Concedes* needs the author to admit something the other side would like, usually before pushing back.
```

# Verb strength and tone
Go only as far as the text goes.

```visual
text-structure-purpose--strength
```

## Key ideas
Pick the verb whose **strength** matches the text.
- The text says *usually*, *partly*, *may*: use *qualifies*, *notes*, *suggests*.
- The text says *wrong*, *no evidence*: use *challenges*, *refutes*.
- *Proves*, *disproves* and *discredits* are very strong. They are right only when the text itself is that forceful.

Also check **tone**. Is the author neutral, admiring or critical? A choice with the opposite tone is out.

*Qualify* = true, but only with limits. *Refute* = shown to be wrong. Most SAT texts hedge, so the milder verb is often the right one.

```example
Cats are said to always land on their feet. They usually do, twisting in midair. But in very short falls, they lack the time to turn.
Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?
Choices: To refute a common claim about cats / To qualify a common claim about cats
1. Sentence 1: a common claim :: Find the claim.
2. *Usually do*, then *But* in short falls :: Mostly true, with an exception.
3. Answer: To qualify a common claim about cats :: Not *refute*: the claim mostly holds.
```

```theory
**A rough ladder (our own tool, not a College Board list).** From agreeing to pushing back: *supports → concedes → qualifies → challenges → refutes*. The SAT does not use this exact order, but it helps you ask, "How far does the text really go?"

**Concede** means to admit a point the other side would like ("Admittedly, the lanes are narrow"). **Qualify** means to add a limit ("true, but only in some cases"). Both are mild. **Challenge** means to question or push back without proving anything. **Refute** means to show that something is wrong. The right answer is often the *milder* verb, because most SAT texts hedge: *may*, *usually*, *one study*.

**Words that give the strength away.** *Usually, mostly, partly, in some cases, may* → mild. *Wrong, no evidence, disproved, impossible* → strong.

**Extreme verbs.** *Prove, disprove, discredit* are usually too strong. If you are unsure, ask: does the text say the claim is false, or only that it is limited?

**Tone.** Read the adjectives and adverbs. "Remarkable," "elegant" show admiration; "flawed," "weak" show criticism. A text that just reports a study is neutral, so a choice with a strong opinion is suspect.

**Trap.** A choice that gets the direction right but the strength wrong. *Refute* when the author only qualifies is a common wrong answer.
```

# Text structure
List the jobs in order: first this, then that.

```visual
text-structure-purpose--structure
```

## Key ideas
A **structure** question asks how the text is built: "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"

1. Number the jobs: first, then, finally.
2. Read each choice **part by part, in order**.
3. One false part, or a wrong order, means the choice is out.

Common patterns: a question and its answer; a claim and its evidence; an old view and a new finding; a problem and a solution; a hypothesis, a test and a result; a general idea and a specific case.

```example
The old squire had always laughed at ghosts. Yet when the clock in the empty hall struck thirteen, he lit every candle in the house.
Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?
Choices: It states a belief, then shows behavior that contradicts it. / It describes a house, then explains why it is empty.
1. Sentence 1: he laughs at ghosts :: Job 1: a belief.
2. Sentence 2: *Yet* + he lights every candle :: Job 2: behavior against it.
3. Answer: It states a belief, then shows behavior that contradicts it :: Both parts true, in order.
```

```theory
**Structure answers describe roles, not topics.** Words like *introduces a problem*, *presents a hypothesis*, *gives an example*, *notes an exception* name the job of each stage. A choice that only restates the topic ("The text is about a house") is not about structure.

**Reading a long choice.** Most structure choices have two or three parts joined by *then*, *and then*, or a comma. Read the first part and find it in the text; if it is missing, stop. Then check the second part, and make sure it comes **after** the first. A choice can have the right parts in the wrong order.

**Match the specific words.** *Hypothesis* means a guess to be tested, so it needs a researcher testing something. *Finding* means a result. *Example* means one case of a general point. *Contradicts* needs something that conflicts. If the choice uses a word the text does not support, it is wrong.

**Poems and stories.** Structure can also mean a shift: a speaker who begins in one mood and moves to another, or a scene that changes from the past to the present. Look for words like *now*, *then*, *but*, or a change in time or place.

**Trap.** A choice that gets the first part right and invents a second part (for example, "then explains why it is empty") is wrong. The text must actually do each thing.
```

# Trap: true, wrong job
A true detail can still miss the whole job.

```visual
text-structure-purpose--traps
```

## Key ideas
Wrong choices on purpose and function questions are often **true** but miss the job. Four common traps:
- **Too narrow**: copies a detail from one sentence.
- **Too broad**: claims more than the text covers.
- **Recycled language**: uses words from the text, but changes what the text does with them.
- **Wrong half**: the first part is right, the second part is invented.

Test every choice: does it cover the **whole** text, including the part after the turn? If you cannot point to a sentence that proves each part of a choice, cross it out.

```example
Saffron is known as an expensive spice. But what makes it costly is the harvest: each crocus flower gives only three red threads, and every one is picked by hand.
Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?
Choices: To state that saffron is a spice. / To explain why saffron is expensive.
1. Trap: "saffron is a spice" :: True, but it is only part of sentence 1.
2. *But* + the harvest is the reason :: The point follows the turn.
3. Answer: To explain why saffron is expensive :: It covers the whole text.
```

```theory
**Why wrong answers sound good.** Test writers build wrong choices from real pieces of the passage. They are true statements about the passage, just not answers to *this* question. Always ask whether the choice says what the text **does**.

**Too narrow vs. too broad.** A narrow choice zooms in on one example or number. A broad choice zooms out ("To discuss mural painting in cities") when the text covers one alley. The right answer is the right size: about as wide as the text.

**General wording is fine.** The correct answer often uses more general words than the text ("an invention" instead of "a computer mouse"). Do not reject a choice just because it avoids the text's specific names.

**Recycled language.** Seeing exact words from the passage in a choice is not a reason to pick it. Check what the choice *says the text does* with those words. Often the passage mentions a concept only in passing, and the choice treats it as the main topic.
```
