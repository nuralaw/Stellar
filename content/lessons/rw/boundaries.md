# Two full sentences
Period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS.

## Full sentences and FANBOYS

A **full sentence** stands alone. Join two with a period, a semicolon, or a comma + **FANBOYS**. Never a comma alone.

```visual
boundaries--fanboys
```

```example
The rain ______ the game began.
Choices: stopped, / stopped; / stopped
1. Before: "The rain stopped" :: A full sentence.
2. After: "the game began" :: Also a full sentence.
3. Answer: stopped; :: A semicolon joins two full sentences.
```

```more
A **clause** is a group of words with a subject (who or what) and a verb (what happens). A **full sentence** is a clause that makes sense alone: *The rain stopped.* A **fragment** looks like a sentence but cannot stand alone: *After the rain stopped.* **FANBOYS** are seven joining words: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So. The comma goes before the FANBOYS word.
```

## Test both sides

```quiz
The trail was ______ we reached the summit by noon.
- steep, :: A comma alone between two sentences is a comma splice.
* steep, but
- steep but, :: The comma goes before *but*, not after it.
> "The trail was steep" and "we reached the summit by noon" are full sentences. Comma + *but* joins them.
```

## Choose the FANBOYS word

```quiz
Maya did not answer the ______ did she reply to my message.
* phone, nor
- phone nor :: A comma is missing before *nor*.
- phone, or :: *Or* gives a choice. After *not*, the matching word is *nor*.
> *Nor* means "and not". It flips the next verb: *nor did she reply*. A comma goes before *nor*.
```

# However and therefore
However isn't a FANBOYS word. Semicolon between sentences.

## However can't join sentences

*However* and *therefore* are not FANBOYS. Between two sentences, put a semicolon or period before them and a comma after.

```example
The store was ______ we waited outside.
Choices: closed, however, / closed; however, / closed however
1. "The store was closed" + "we waited outside" :: Two full sentences.
2. closed, however, :: Only commas: a comma splice.
3. Answer: closed; however, :: Semicolon before *however*, comma after.
```

```more
Other words like *however*: *therefore*, *moreover*, *instead*. A semicolon or period goes between the two full sentences. Commas set the word off wherever it sits. It usually opens the second sentence (*; however,*). Sometimes it ends the first, when it contrasts with something earlier.
```

## However inside a sentence

*However* in the middle of one sentence takes two commas.

```quiz
The first flight ended in a crash. The second ______ went perfectly.
- flight; however, :: "Went perfectly" has no subject, so it is not a full sentence.
- flight, however :: One comma is missing after *however*.
* flight, however,
> *However* interrupts one sentence: "The second flight went perfectly." An interruption takes two commas.
```

## Which side?

Ask what *however* contrasts with. The semicolon still sits between the sentences.

```quiz
Critics expected the film to fail. It sold ______ within a year it had earned back its cost twice.
* well, however;
- well; however, :: That makes *however* contrast the two clauses, but they agree.
- well, however, :: Commas alone cannot join two sentences.
> *It sold well* contrasts with the critics, so *however* ends that clause. *Within a year…* agrees with it, so the semicolon comes after *however*.
```

# Colon or dash
Full sentence first, then a list or explanation.

## Full sentence, then the rest

Before a colon or a single dash: a full sentence. After it: a list, a phrase, or an explanation.

```example
She packed two ______ a map and a compass.
Choices: things: / things; / things.
1. Before: "She packed two things" :: A full sentence.
2. After: "a map and a compass" :: A list, not a sentence.
3. Answer: things: :: The colon introduces the list.
```

```more
A **colon** (:) and a **single dash** (—) both mean "here comes the detail". What comes before must be a full sentence. What follows can be a list, a phrase, or a full sentence that explains the first one. A semicolon also separates list items that already contain commas: *Paris, France; Rome, Italy.*
```

## Introduce the list

```quiz
The recipe needs only three ______ flour, water, and salt.
* ingredients:
- ingredients; :: A semicolon needs a full sentence after it too.
- ingredients. :: "Flour, water, and salt." has no verb: a fragment.
- ingredients :: Nothing introduces the list, so it runs into the sentence.
> The left side is a full sentence, and the colon hands over the list it promised.
```

## One dash like a colon

```quiz
The mayor made only one ______ no more traffic jams.
* promise—
- promise; :: "No more traffic jams" is not a full sentence.
- promise. :: That leaves "No more traffic jams" as a fragment.
> A full sentence comes first. One dash then introduces the explanation, just like a colon.
```

# Matching pairs
Extra detail in the middle: two commas or two dashes.

## Close what you opened

Extra detail in the middle takes a matching pair: two commas or two dashes. Remove it: a full sentence must remain.

```example
My sister, a nurse in ______ works nights.
Choices: Boston / Boston, / Boston—
1. Remove the detail: "My sister works nights." :: Still a full sentence.
2. The detail opened with a comma :: Close it the same way.
3. Answer: Boston, :: Two commas make the pair.
```

```more
The extra detail (*a nurse in Boston*) can be removed, and the sentence still works. Mark both ends the same way: comma and comma, or dash and dash. Never mix a comma and a dash. Never mark only one end.
```

## Find the opening mark

```quiz
The axolotl—a salamander that keeps its gills as an ______ can regrow lost legs.
- adult, :: A dash opened the detail. A comma cannot close it.
- adult :: Nothing closes the detail, so it runs into "can regrow."
* adult—
> Remove the detail: "The axolotl can regrow lost legs." It opened with a dash, so it closes with a dash.
```

# Phrase at the end
One comma before an added phrase.

## A phrase renames the idea

After a full sentence, a phrase that renames the idea needs one comma. It is not a sentence.

```example
Engineers measured the sway at three times the safe ______ result that forced the city to close the bridge.
Choices: limit, a / limit; a / limit. A / limit a
1. Before: "…three times the safe limit" :: A full sentence.
2. After: "a result that forced…" :: It renames the idea. Not a sentence.
3. Answer: limit, a :: One comma attaches the phrase.
```

```more
This phrase starts with a noun (*a result*, *a finding*, *a discovery*) and names the whole idea before it. It has no sentence of its own, so a period or semicolon cannot come before it. The comma joins it to the full sentence.
```

## Comma or semicolon?

```quiz
Researchers recorded wolves howling at dusk in every ______ pattern that surprised them.
* season, a
- season; a :: A semicolon needs a full sentence after it. This is a phrase.
- season. A :: A period would leave "A pattern…" as a fragment.
> "A pattern that surprised them" renames the idea. It cannot stand alone, so it takes one comma.
```

# Although openers
Opening clause? Comma, then the main sentence.

## Opener, comma, sentence

*Although*, *because*, *when* and *if* start a clause that cannot stand alone. Add a comma, then the main sentence.

```example
Although it ______ we played.
Choices: rained / rained, / rained;
1. "Although it rained" :: It cannot stand alone.
2. "we played" :: A full sentence.
3. Answer: rained, :: Opener, comma, full sentence.
```

```more
Words that begin a clause that cannot stand alone: *although*, *because*, *when*, *if*, *while*, *since*, *after*, *before*. Compare *It rained.* (a full sentence) with *Although it rained* (not finished). A semicolon or period after *Although it rained* leaves a broken piece.
```

## Fix the opener

```quiz
Because the road was ______ the bus ran late.
* icy,
- icy; :: "Because the road was icy" is not a full sentence.
- icy. :: A period leaves "Because the road was icy" as a fragment.
> "Because the road was icy" needs the main sentence after it. A comma joins them.
```

# Trap: needless marks
No mark between words that belong together.

## Don't split a pair

Some words belong together: subject and verb, verb and object, preposition and object. Put no mark between them.

```visual
boundaries--tree
```

```example
The books on the top ______ belong to Maya.
Choices: shelf, / shelf: / shelf
1. Subject: "The books on the top shelf" :: Long, but one subject.
2. Verb: "belong" :: It comes right after the subject.
3. Answer: shelf :: No mark between subject and verb.
```

```more
A **preposition** is a word like *in*, *of*, *on* or *to*. An **object** is the noun after a verb (*collected ferns*) or after a preposition (*in the forest*). Put no mark there, or between a general title and a name (*the botanist Lena Marsh*). A direct question ends with a question mark, not a period.
```

## Title before a name

```quiz
The ______ Lena Marsh led the team.
* botanist
- botanist, :: A comma would make the name extra, but it is needed.
- botanist: :: "The botanist" is not a full sentence before a colon.
> *The botanist* alone does not say who. The name is needed, so no marks.
```

## Verb and its object

```quiz
In 1998, a team of botanists led by Lena Marsh climbed into the cloud forests of Peru. After two months of fieldwork, the team had ______ more than four hundred species of fern, many of them new to science.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?
* collected
- collected, :: A comma splits the verb from its object.
- collected; :: "More than four hundred species…" is not a full sentence.
- collected: :: A colon needs a full sentence before it.
> "Had collected" needs its object, "more than four hundred species." Nothing goes between them.
```
