Research sweep for the two skills in the conventions domain, done in November 2026. The strongest single source turned out to be College Board's own answer explanations: we pulled every conventions explanation from SAT Practice Tests 4 to 11 (125 questions) and tallied what each one tests. Reddit could not be reached from our tools (reddit.com blocked page fetches, the search tool, and direct requests), so no Reddit threads were used. YouTube watch pages were readable, but auto-captions were not, so for most videos we used the title, description and chapter list only. The one full transcript we read came from a transcript site.

Tally of the 125 official explanations (Practice Tests 4 to 11): 61 Boundaries and 64 Form, Structure, and Sense. Boundaries: punctuation within a sentence (supplements, transition words, colons and semicolons) 24, joining two clauses or two sentences 13, no-punctuation cases (subject-verb, verb-object, preposition-object, title-name, restrictive appositives, integrated *that* clauses) 12, end punctuation 5, complex series 4, colons 2, opening dependent clause 1. Form, Structure, and Sense: verb forms (finite or nonfinite) 17, subject-verb agreement 17, tense 10, subject-modifier placement 9, pronoun-antecedent 5, plurals and possessive nouns 3, possessive determiners and determiners 3.

## Boundaries

### Best sources

- [College Board: Assessment Framework for the Digital SAT Suite](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/assessment-framework-for-digital-sat-suite.pdf) — the official definition (between sentences; within sentences: coordination, series, supplements, no punctuation), the 26% / 11 to 15 question share, and the rule that conventions questions are ordered by difficulty regardless of skill

### What the best teachers agree on

- Decide by structure, never by sound. Every serious source starts with the same move: test whether each side of the blank could stand alone as a sentence (Khan's before-and-after test, Test Ninjas' diagnose step, Open Exam Prep's cover test, our period test).
- Two independent clauses take a period, a semicolon, a comma plus FANBOYS, or a colon when the second explains the first. A comma alone (splice) and no mark (run-on) are always wrong.
- "Semicolon = period" is the single most useful rule; the only exception is the complex list.
- Transition words like *however* can't join clauses. Semicolon or period before, comma after; commas on both sides when they interrupt one clause. College Board's hardest items decide the semicolon's side by meaning.
- A colon needs a complete sentence before it and never follows *such as*, *including* or a verb that still needs its object.
- Extra information takes a matching pair, and the closing mark is what most items test. Essential information, including a title or description directly before a name, takes no marks.
- No single mark between subject and verb, verb and object, preposition and object. Official explanations repeat this more than any other ruling.
- Two choices that do the same job (period vs semicolon) cancel each other out.

### Disagreements and choices made

- **Can one dash join two sentences?** Khan's punctuation guide says dashes only set off nonessential elements; Just Locked In, Erica Meltzer and PrepScholar treat a single dash like a colon. College Board's Practice Test 4 explanation implies a dash can mark a boundary, but not when the sentence already contains a dash. We teach the single dash as colon-like (complete sentence first) plus College Board's "no second dash" ruling.
- **Comma before FANBOYS for short clauses.** Chicago lets writers drop it for very short, closely linked clauses; College Board's explanations call the missing comma unconventional for longer clauses. We teach the comma as required and only show long clauses, so no example rests on the disputed case.
- **Semicolon plus "and".** Khan calls "; and" unnecessary and unconventional; Chicago allows it when long clauses already contain commas. We tell students not to choose it, and we never use it as a wrong choice in our examples.
- **Comma before a trailing dependent clause.** Vibrant Publishers says "no comma" for every dependent clause after the main clause. The Editor's Blog and standard usage split it: no comma before *because/if/when*, a comma before contrast words like *although* and *whereas*. We teach the split.
- **Question marks.** The SAT Gamified video suggests a question mark only when the sentence starts with a question word. College Board's items test indirect questions (period plus statement word order) and direct questions at the end of a longer sentence, often after a colon. We teach "does the sentence ask, or report a question?"
- **Comma before a quotation.** Many guides say a comma introduces any quotation. Standard usage and College Board's verb-complement rulings point the other way when the quote is part of the sentence's grammar. We teach: integrated quote, no mark; after a complete sentence, a colon; after *said* or *wrote*, a comma.
- **Mixed comma/dash pairs.** Khan and most guides say never mix. One Practice Test 11 item has a comma-opened supplement closed by the closing dash of a nested dash pair, because a comma and a dash are not stacked. We keep "match the marks" as the rule and mention this case once as a rare exception.
- **Serial comma and short introductory commas.** PrepScholar says the serial comma isn't tested; official texts always use it. Both it and the comma after a short opener are optional in real writing, so we tell students no question can hinge on them.
- **Heuristics we left out.** Test Innovators' "different subjects usually means a period" and SAT Gamified's "only the first verb matters" didn't hold up against official items.

- [College Board: Assessment Framework for the Digital SAT Suite](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/assessment-framework-for-digital-sat-suite.pdf) — the six official testing points: subject-verb agreement, pronoun-antecedent agreement, verb finiteness, verb tense and aspect, subject-modifier placement, genitives and plurals (including *its/it's*, *their/they're/there*)
- [College Board: Skills Insight for the Digital SAT Suite](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/skills-insight-digital-sat-suite.pdf) — agreement difficulty grows with the number of nouns and verbs between subject and verb; introductory modifiers sit near the top band

### What the best teachers agree on

- Read the choices first: what changes tells you the rule (verb number, verb form, tense, pronoun, apostrophe, word order). Khan, Test Innovators, Test Ninjas and Princeton Review all lead with this.
- Every rule has one deciding word elsewhere in the text: the true subject, the antecedent, a time clue, the owner, or the noun an opening phrase describes.
- Strip out prepositional phrases and interrupters to find the subject; watch inverted sentences, compound subjects, *each/every*, and group nouns (singular in American English).
- Every clause needs a finite verb; if the clause already has one, the blank needs an *-ing*, *-ed* or *to* form; *to* + verb signals purpose; modals take the base form.
- Tense follows time clues and the surrounding verbs; general truths and literature take the present; "by" + past time takes the past perfect.
- Pronouns agree in number with a clear antecedent; organizations take *it*; no shift to *you*.
- Apostrophes: judge each noun on its own (owner or not, one or many); expand contractions to test them.
- The noun right after an opening modifier must be the thing it describes; possessives are the classic trap.

### Disagreements and choices made

- **Is pronoun case, who/whom, parallelism or comparison tested?** PrepScholar's complete guide and Princeton Review list them. College Board's six testing points don't, Erica Meltzer marks pronoun case as ACT-only, and none appeared in the 125 official conventions items we tallied. We tell students to skip *who/whom*, *I/me*, idioms and spelling pairs, and we handle parallel verbs only inside verb-form questions (two verbs after one helper or subject match).
- **Which sub-types are most common?** The SCORE video says agreement, pronouns and possessives make up most grammar questions. The official data says verb forms and subject-verb agreement lead (about 27% each), then tense (16%), modifiers (14%), and pronouns and possessives (about 8 to 9% each). We follow the official count.
- **Singular "their".** LearnQ says the SAT accepts *their* for a singular antecedent in most contexts. Khan says the test never makes students choose between *he or she* and singular *they*, and every official item pairs *their* with a plural antecedent. We teach number agreement and add Khan's note.
- **Group nouns.** British usage allows plural verbs; College Board items and Test Ninjas treat companies, cities and committees as singular. We teach singular and flag the British habit for international students.
- **Gerunds.** Test Innovators says no released verb-form item tests gerunds, but official agreement items use *-ing* subjects (*using*, *landing*, *writing*) with singular verbs. We teach gerund subjects as singular.
- **Mixed verb questions.** Older guides treat agreement, tense and form as separate question types. 3-4-5 Tutoring's 2026 video and a Practice Test 11 agreement item with a nonfinite distractor show mixing. Our method checks every difference between the choices, and one worked example mixes number and form.
- **Possessive of singular nouns ending in s.** Khan writes *rhinoceros's*; AP style varies for names. The test doesn't hinge on this, so we teach *'s* for singular nouns and don't test it.
- **"Simple tense is usually right."** Erica Meltzer's tip is useful but not a rule, so we present it as a tie-breaker only.
- **Absolute phrases.** Few guides mention them, but 3-4-5 Tutoring models an item on an official question, and Practice Test 9 has a nonfinite participle phrase after a main clause. We cover them in both lessons: a noun plus an *-ing* or *-ed* form after a comma stays nonfinite, and a finite verb there makes a comma splice.
