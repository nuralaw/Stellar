# Who the results fit
A random sample fits its own group, no bigger.

## Sample fits its group

**Random sample**: people picked by chance from a group. Its results fit that whole group, no bigger.

```visual
statistical-claims--sample
```

```example
A researcher randomly selects 60 of a hospital's 400 nurses. 70% of the sample nap on breaks. Who do the results describe?
1. Sampled: 60 nurses, picked at random :: Find who was picked, and how.
2. Picked from: this hospital's 400 nurses :: Find the group they came from.
3. About 70% of all 400 nurses nap :: Not nurses at other hospitals.
```

```more
The **population** is the whole group the sample was picked from. Picking by chance makes the sample a small copy of the population, so its results can be extended to all of it. The results cannot be extended to people outside that group, who had no chance of being picked.
```

## Find the largest group

```quiz
A researcher randomly selects 80 of the 500 ninth graders at one school and asks how long they sleep. What is the largest group the results describe?
- The 80 students surveyed :: Too small. A random sample speaks for its whole group.
* All 500 ninth graders at the school
- All students at the school :: Other grades had no chance of being picked.
- All ninth graders in the state :: Only this school was sampled.
> The sample was random from this school's ninth graders, so it describes exactly them.
```

## Count the group

```quiz
A company has 2,000 employees. A researcher randomly selects 100 of them and surveys them about commuting. To how many employees can the results be extended?
answer: 2000
- 100 :: That is the sample. The results fit the whole group.
- 1900 :: Those 1,900 were not picked, but they are in the group.
> The sample is random from all 2,000 employees, so the results fit all 2,000.
```

# Biased samples
Volunteers and hand-picked groups speak only for themselves.

## Not random, no generalizing

Volunteers or a hand-picked group may differ from everyone else. A bigger sample does not fix that.

```example
A town has 9,000 residents. A librarian surveys 300 people inside the library: 90% want longer library hours. Do about 90% of all residents?
1. Sampled: people already in the library :: Not random: non-visitors had no chance.
2. Library visitors likely want longer hours more than others :: The sample leans one way.
3. No. It describes only those 300 :: Biased sample: no generalizing.
```

```more
A sample is **biased** when it leans one way, because some people were more likely to be picked than others. People who chose to answer (volunteers) are often the ones with strong opinions. To **generalize** is to apply the results to the whole population. Without random selection, the results fit only the people who answered.
```

## Spot the bias

```quiz
A fitness app asks its users to rate a new plan. Of the 6,000 who choose to answer, 85% love it. Why might this not represent all users?
- 6,000 users are too few. :: Size is fine. Who answered is the problem.
* Users chose whether to answer.
- 85% is too high to be true. :: A fair sample could give 85% too.
> Volunteers with strong opinions answer more often, so they may not match all users.
```

# Cause needs random assignment
Chance picks the groups. Then you may say "causes".

## Random assignment allows cause

**Random assignment**: chance, like a coin flip, puts people into groups. One group gets the **treatment**: the thing being tested.

```visual
statistical-claims--design
```

```example
A coach randomly assigns 40 of a team's 80 swimmers to a new warm-up. They race faster, on average, than the other 40. Did the warm-up cause it?
1. Who chose the groups? Chance :: Random assignment: groups start out alike.
2. Only difference between the groups: the warm-up :: So it explains the faster times.
3. Yes: the warm-up likely made these swimmers faster :: Cause, for this team only.
```

```more
When chance decides who gets the **treatment** (here, the new warm-up), the two groups start out alike in everything else. So if the groups end up different, the treatment is the likely reason. This is why an experiment can show a cause, and a survey cannot.
```

## Pick the conclusion

```quiz
A researcher randomly selects 120 of a company's 2,000 employees and randomly assigns 60 of them to walk at lunch. The walkers report less tiredness, on average. Which conclusion is best?
* Lunch walks likely reduce tiredness for this company's employees.
- There is a relationship between walks and less tiredness, but no cause is shown. :: Too weak. Random assignment allows a cause.
- Lunch walks likely reduce tiredness for all office workers. :: Too big. Only this company was sampled.
> Random assignment: cause. Random sample of this company: its employees, no further.
```

# Sample or assignment?
Two separate questions: who it fits, and cause.

## Set them side by side

Random **sample** → who the results fit. Random **assignment** → whether a cause is shown.

```visual
statistical-claims--grid
```

```example
A school asks for volunteers for a study. It randomly assigns 100 volunteers: 50 try a new study method, 50 do not. The method group scores higher. What can be concluded?
1. Volunteers, not a random sample :: Results fit only these volunteers.
2. Random assignment to two groups :: A cause is possible.
3. The method likely helped these volunteers :: Cause, but only for them.
```

```more
Check each question alone. "Were people picked at random from the group?" If yes, the results fit the group. "Were people put into groups at random?" If yes, a cause can be shown. Volunteers with random assignment can show a cause, but only for the volunteers.
```

## Neither one

```quiz
A website lets each visitor choose layout A or layout B. Visitors who chose B spent more money, on average. Which conclusion is best?
- Layout B causes visitors to spend more money. :: Visitors chose their layout, so no cause is shown.
- Layout B causes all shoppers to spend more. :: No random assignment and no random sample.
* There is a relationship between choosing B and spending more for these visitors.
> Visitors chose, so no cause. They were not a random sample, so it fits only these visitors.
```

# Trap: big survey, no cause
A survey shows a relationship, never a cause.

## Surveys only watch

A survey only watches. However big or random, it shows a **relationship between** two things, never a cause.

```example
A random sample of 2,000 of a city's teens is surveyed. Those who eat breakfast have higher grades, on average. Does breakfast raise grades?
1. Was breakfast assigned at random? No, teens chose :: A survey only watches.
2. Breakfast eaters may also sleep more :: Something else could raise grades.
3. Not shown. There is a relationship for the city's teens :: Relationship, not cause.
```

```more
A **relationship between** two things means they go together, such as breakfast and grades. It does not mean one causes the other. Something else, like sleep, could cause both. Only random assignment removes this problem. Random sampling only tells you who the relationship fits.
```

## Relationship or cause?

```quiz
A researcher selected 5,000 adults at random from all adults in a state and surveyed them about their habits. Those who read before bed report falling asleep faster, on average. Which conclusion is best supported?
- Reading before bed makes the state's adults fall asleep faster. :: Trap: nobody was assigned to read. No cause.
* For the state's adults, there is a relationship between reading before bed and falling asleep faster.
- Nothing can be concluded. :: Too weak. A random sample still shows a relationship.
> No assignment, so no cause. Random sample of the state, so the relationship fits its adults.
```
