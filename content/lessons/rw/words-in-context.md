# Clue, predict, match
Find the clue. Predict a word. Match a choice.

```visual
words-in-context--clue-predict
```

## Key ideas
A blank question asks: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?" The text **proves** the answer. Somewhere there is a **clue**, a word or group of words that shows what the blank means.

1. Cover the choices and read the whole text.
2. Find the clue. It often follows a colon or dash, sits in the next sentence, or lists examples.
3. Predict your own simple word.
4. Pick the choice that matches your word. Cross out the rest.

The right word matches the clue's **meaning**. It does not have to sound natural or familiar, and a fancy word is not a better answer. The clue decides.

```example
The new principal is ______: she answers every email within an hour and never forgets a name.
Choices: attentive / distant / hasty
1. Clue: "answers every email", "never forgets a name" :: It follows the colon.
2. Predict: pays close attention :: Your own word, before the choices.
3. distant = far away; hasty = rushed :: Neither matches. Cross out.
4. Answer: attentive :: It matches the clue exactly.
```

```theory
Words in context questions test whether you can read a sentence closely and work out which word fits. All four choices are real English words, and several may be possible in a general sentence. Only one is **proved** by the text.

A **clue** is any piece of text that tells you the blank's meaning. The usual types are:
- **A restatement or explanation** after a colon or dash ("wave power isn't ______: it varies unpredictably").
- **Examples** ("his ______ influences: European portraits, pop art and flatstyle").
- **A cause or result** in the next sentence.
- **A contrast** (covered in the next part).

Why predict first? The choices are bait. If you read them first, a plausible word will seduce you before you have checked the text. A prediction ("pays attention", "hard to understand") is a net that catches only the right meaning.

**Do not judge by sound.** "Does it sound right?" is not the test. A correct word may sound unusual, especially in a less common meaning. A wrong word may sound smooth because it is common or because it relates to the topic. Test meaning against the clue, not rhythm.

**Precise** means the word fits the clue's meaning and strength. "Most logical" means nothing in the text contradicts it.

Typical wrong choices are an opposite, a word related to the topic but unsupported, a word with the right direction but the wrong strength, and a hard word that is simply wrong.

If you cannot predict a word, name the clue's direction (good, bad, mild, strong) and use it to cross out choices.
```

# Same side or opposite
Spot the signal. Does the meaning continue or flip?

```visual
words-in-context--contrast-side
```

## Key ideas
Signal words show the direction of the blank.

- **Opposite**: *but*, *although*, *though*, *however*, *yet*, *despite*, *unlike*, *while*, *whereas*, *rather than*, *diverged from*.
- **Same direction**: *and*, *also*, *because*, *so*, *indeed*, *in fact*, *for example*, *such as*, a colon, a dash.

A contrast word flips the meaning only **across** it. If the blank and the clue sit on opposite sides of *but*, they are opposites. If both are on the same side, they agree.

Work through each signal one at a time. Find the clue, note which side it is on, then set the meaning of the blank.

```example
Although the museum looks ______ from the street, its galleries take hours to explore.
Choices: compact / sprawling / ancient
1. Clue: "galleries take hours to explore" :: The museum is big inside.
2. Blank and clue: opposite sides of *Although* :: So the blank is the opposite.
3. Predict: small :: The opposite of big.
4. Answer: compact :: It looks small from outside.
```

```theory
The SAT hides most blank clues behind a signal word. The signal tells you whether to copy the clue's meaning or reverse it.

**Contrast signals** reverse the direction. These include *but*, *yet*, *however*, *although*, *though*, *despite*, *in spite of*, *while*, *whereas*, *unlike*, *rather than*, *instead of*, *on the other hand*, and verbs such as *diverged*, *challenged*, *disproved*.

**Continuation signals** keep the direction. These include *and*, *also*, *because*, *since*, *so*, *thus*, *indeed*, *in fact*, *for example*, *such as*, *like*, *similarly*, and punctuation that introduces an explanation: a colon, a dash.

The "across the wall" rule: imagine a wall at the contrast word. If the blank and the clue are on opposite sides of the wall, they are opposites. If they are on the same side, they agree. In "Even though the audience praised the talk, the speaker felt ______: her hands shook," the blank and *hands shook* are on the same side of *though* (after the comma), so they agree: nervous.

**Multiple signals.** A sentence can have a contrast word *and* a colon. Walk through them in order, from the clue to the blank, flipping once for each contrast.

**A signal in the text can be a word, not a conjunction.** "The twin's style *diverged from* her sister's, which was more ______" tells you the blank is the opposite of what the twin did. A name or label that sets two things against each other has the same effect.

Common traps: reversing when the clue is on the same side; missing a quiet signal such as *unlike*; and choosing the word that matches the clue when the sentence needed its opposite.
```

# Negation flips the charge
Not, far from, rarely: decide what is true, then flip.

```visual
words-in-context--negation
```

## Key ideas
A word has a **charge**: positive (+), negative (−) or neutral. Decide the charge of the **blank**, after every flip, and not just the charge of the clue.

Negative signals flip the meaning of what follows: *not*, *never*, *far from*, *by no means*, *hardly*, *rarely*, *little*, *unlikely*, *without*, *lack of*, *less*.

1. Find what is true in the text. Is it + or −?
2. Apply the negation. "Far from ___" and "not ___" make the blank the opposite.
3. Pick the choice with that charge.

Two negatives make a positive: *by no means unimportant* means important.

```example
Far from being ______, the new schedule actually helped: students slept longer and arrived on time.
Choices: harmful / helpful / unfamiliar
1. Clue: "helped", "slept longer", "arrived on time" :: True: the schedule is good (+).
2. "Far from" flips the blank :: The blank must be negative (−).
3. helpful repeats the true idea :: It would only work without "far from". Cross out.
4. Answer: harmful :: The opposite of helped.
```

```theory
Negation is a favorite trap because students copy the clue's charge into the blank. The text says the schedule helped, so a student picks *helpful*, and the sentence now says "far from being helpful, the schedule actually helped," which makes no sense.

The fix is to ask what the **blank** must be. If the text says *far from X* and the truth is positive, X is negative: *far from being harmful, it helped*. If the text says *not X* and the truth is negative, X is positive: *not generous, it cost them a fortune*.

**Hidden negations.** Some words carry a negative without saying *not*: *rarely*, *seldom*, *hardly*, *scarcely*, *barely*, *unlikely*, *little more than*, *a paucity of* (a shortage), *lack of*. In "Visitors were surprised: the lake ______ freezes before January," the surprise tells you the blank means *rarely*.

**A negative word can describe a positive idea.** *Less ______* turns a negative word into a good idea: "The new schedule had a less *harmful* effect than critics feared." The word *harmful* is negative, but the idea is positive. This is why you decide the blank's charge from the logic, not from the feel of the surrounding words.

**Tricky expressions.** *For all* means *despite* ("for all its flaws, the film succeeded"). *All but* means *almost*, not *all except* ("all but forgotten" = nearly forgotten).

**Method.** Say the text in plain words: "The schedule helped. The writer says it was *far from* ___. So the blank is the opposite of 'helpful.'" Then put your choice back into the sentence and check that the whole sentence is true.
```

# Match the strength
Mild clue, mild word. Strong clue, strong word.

```visual
words-in-context--strength
```

## Key ideas
Words in the same family differ in **strength**: *reduces* / *eliminates*, *unlikely* / *impossible*, *concerned* / *terrified*. The blank must match the strength of the clue as well as its direction.

- **Mild signals**: *slightly*, *some*, *may*, *often*, *in part*, *almost*, *tend to*, *seems*, *little*.
- **Strong signals**: *always*, *never*, *entirely*, *only*, *every*, *no*, *completely*, *considerable*, *proves*.

Cross out words that are too strong, such as *eliminates* when "traces remain," and words that are too weak, such as *acceptable* when the text says "considerable." Check the direction first, then the size.

```example
The new filter ______ lead in tap water: tests found 20 percent less than before, though traces remain.
Choices: removes / reduces / ignores
1. Clue: "20 percent less", "traces remain" :: A real but partial drop.
2. removes = takes out fully :: Too strong: traces remain.
3. ignores = has no effect :: Wrong direction.
4. Answer: reduces :: Same size as the clue.
```

```theory
Direction is not enough. The SAT often offers two choices in the same direction and expects you to pick the one with the right intensity.

Think of a scale. For pain: *worsens*, *no effect*, *eases*, *eliminates*. For likelihood: *impossible*, *unlikely*, *possible*, *likely*, *certain*. For opinion: *dislikes*, *criticizes*, *condemns*. For size: *negligible*, *modest*, *considerable*, *enormous*.

Read the clue and find its place on the scale.
- "Almost no rain fell" is very low but not zero, so *scant* fits and *none* overshoots.
- "A sharp rise" means a large change, so *dramatic* fits and *slight* does not.
- "More quietly than before" points to *discreetly*, not *loudly*.

**Absolute words** (*never*, *always*, *entirely*, *proves*, *conclusively*) are right only when the text itself is absolute. A single small study "lends some support" to a theory but does not "conclusively settle" it.

**The clue can be a number.** A 20 percent drop is a reduction, not an elimination. Three failures out of ten is not "rare" or "frequent" in an absolute sense, so look at the wording around the number.

**Tone words** work the same way: *mild criticism* is not *scorn*.

Method: write your prediction as a short phrase with an adverb ("quite large", "only slightly"), and then check each choice against it.
```

# Unknown words
Cross out what you know. Use the charge.

```visual
words-in-context--charge
```

## Key ideas
When you do not know a choice, use the clue, not the choice.

1. Predict the blank and decide its **charge** (+, −, neutral), after any flips.
2. Cross out the choices you **do** know that clash with it.
3. Use word parts as hints: *bene-* good, *mal-* bad, *un-*, *in-*, *non-* not. Roots can mislead: *invaluable* means extremely useful.
4. The unfamiliar word that is left is probably right. Hard words are often the correct answers on the SAT, so do not drop a word just because you have never seen it.

Do not choose a word because it is fancy. A strange word needs the same proof as any other.

```example
The neighbors found Mr. Dunn ______: he shoveled their driveways each winter and never accepted a cent.
Choices: munificent / churlish / languid
1. Clue: "shoveled their driveways", "never accepted a cent" :: A kind act with no payment. Charge: +.
2. churlish = rude (−); languid = slow (neutral) :: You know these. They clash.
3. munificent: unknown, but it is what is left :: Trust the clue.
4. Answer: munificent :: It means very generous (+).
```

```theory
Some words are rare, and the SAT knows it. The test is built so that the clue, not the dictionary, gives the answer. With a rare word, you can still win by working from the clue.

**Charge first.** After you have found the clue and applied any flips (*not*, *far from*, *unlikely*), decide whether the blank is positive, negative or neutral. Praise, success and care are positive. Criticism, failure and danger are negative. This is the charge of the **blank**, not a copy of the clue's charge: "by no means careless" has a negative word in the clue and a positive blank.

**Eliminate known mismatches.** Even a student who knows only two of the four words can often cross them out. If three choices clash with the clue, the fourth wins.

**Word parts.** Prefixes and roots give charge or direction: *bene-* and *bon-* good, *mal-* and *mis-* bad, *pre-* before, *post-* after, *un-*, *in-*, *im-*, *il-*, *non-* not, *anti-* against, *-phobia* fear. Roots can fool you: *invaluable* has *in-* but means extremely valuable. *Discerning* is a positive word. Treat word parts as hints, not proofs.

**Strangeness is not an answer.** Do not choose a long or unusual word merely because it looks "academic," and do not reject a word merely because you have never met it. Only the clue decides.

**Practice habit.** Every time you meet an unknown choice, note its meaning from the explanation. Frequent SAT words recur.
```

# Trap: topic words
Topic-related is not proved by the text.

```visual
words-in-context--topic-trap
```

## Key ideas
The most tempting wrong choice is often true in the real world, or it is a word that sounds right for the topic, but **the text never says it**. A word is right only if the clue proves it.

Test every choice with one question: **which words in the text prove this?** Point to them. If you cannot, cross the choice out.

- A choice that is *true* but unrelated to the clue is wrong.
- A choice that is *positive* or *interesting* about the subject, but not supported, is wrong.
- A choice that is a *topic word* (a bridge as *historic*, an artist as *celebrated*) is wrong unless the text says it.

```example
Because the old bridge was narrow and its stone was cracked, engineers judged it ______ for heavy trucks.
Choices: historic / unsuitable / scenic
1. Clue: "narrow", "cracked" :: Both are problems for trucks.
2. Predict: not safe enough :: Your own word.
3. historic, scenic: bridge words :: The text says neither.
4. Answer: unsuitable :: Only this one is proved by the clue.
```

```theory
This trap comes in different disguises, but the defense is always the same: go back to the text and find the proof.

**Topic words.** If the passage is about museums, a choice like *celebrated* or *historic* feels right. If the passage is about an experiment, *innovative* feels right. The question writers know that students choose words that "go with" the subject. They want you to ask whether the text supports the word.

**True but off-target.** Choices can state facts that are true of the thing and still not answer the blank. If a text says a museum roof cracked under one snowfall, the museum may be famous, but the clue proves only that the roof is fragile.

**Tempting but partial.** A word may match part of the clue. If the text says a rule has "very little effect," *irrelevant* sounds close, but it overshoots, because the text does not say there is no effect at all.

**Unrelated meaning.** A choice can look related because it shares a word with the passage, but its meaning does not fit the logic.

How to defend yourself:
1. Predict from the clue before you read the choices.
2. For each choice you like, underline the words in the text that prove it.
3. If the only reason you like the word is "it sounds right here," treat it as a trap.

Also watch for a flip. A word can be proved by the text and still be the opposite of what the blank needs after a signal word. Check the sign of the blank, too.
```

# Trap: the meaning you know
Cover the word. Read the sentence as a blank.

```visual
words-in-context--second-meaning
```

## Key ideas
"As used in the text, what does the word X most nearly mean?" is a blank question in disguise. Words have many meanings, and the first meaning you think of is often the trap.

1. Cover the underlined word. Read the sentence as if it had a blank.
2. Find the clue. Predict your own word.
3. Match the choice that means the same **in this sentence**.

Wrong choices are other real meanings of the same word: *dispute* = argue, *support* = defend or encourage, *collect* = increase. They are tempting because you know them.

These questions often use older novels, plays and poems. Read the line before the passage. It tells you who is speaking and what is happening. A word may also be used figuratively, not literally.

```example
As used in the text, what does the word "carry" most nearly mean?
The mayor's plan failed to carry the council: only two of nine members voted for it.
Choices: lift / persuade / transport
1. Blank it: "failed to ______ the council" :: Forget the meaning you know.
2. Clue: "only two of nine voted for it" :: The plan did not win votes.
3. lift, transport: physical meanings :: They do not fit a vote.
4. Answer: persuade :: To win over.
```

```theory
Most "most nearly means" questions use a common word in a less common sense. The question tests whether you let the sentence, rather than your memory, decide.

**Why they trap.** The wrong choices are usually *other genuine definitions* of the word. In "the speaker *struck* a chord with the crowd," the choice *hit* is a real meaning of *struck*, but a chord with a crowd is not hit. Choices that match another sense are always wrong for this sentence.

**The blank method.** Replace the word with a blank, then predict. A plain prediction ("held up", "win over", "block") is more reliable than judging the choices one by one.

**A clue that the word is used in a second sense.** A simple word placed in an odd role: a noun used as a verb, an abstract word with a physical object, a person word with a thing. If a very ordinary word looks strange in the sentence, expect a less common meaning.

**Context before the passage.** The italic line tells you the author, year and situation. Old novels and plays use words in older senses. For example, *tender* may mean offer, and *engage* may mean hire.

**Sound is not the test.** The correct choice may sound strange when substituted. A phrase like "take into account and follow" is clumsy, but if it fits the clue it is the right answer.

**Figurative language.** A word may be a metaphor ("dry lectures"). Read the logic of the sentence, not the dictionary's first entry.

Common traps: the everyday meaning, a different part of speech, and an answer that would be right in a different sentence.
```
