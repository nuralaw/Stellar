> **Key idea:** This is a reading question more than a vocabulary quiz. The passage almost always holds a clue that tells you what the blank must mean, so find the clue before you look at the choices.

## What this question looks like

You get a short passage (often two or three sentences) and one of two question stems. These questions usually come first in each Reading and Writing module.

- **Fill the blank** (most common): "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?"
- **Meaning of a word:** "As used in the text, what does the word *X* most nearly mean?" This shows up more in older literary passages.

All four choices are real words that usually fit the grammar. So the question isn't "which word sounds okay?" but "which word does the passage *prove* belongs here?"

## The method

Use this routine on every question, aiming for about 30–45 seconds each.

1. **Hide the choices.** If you read them first, the fanciest-sounding word tends to grab you.
2. **Find the clue.** Read the whole passage, not just the blank's sentence. Look for words that describe the blank and for signal words like *however*, *because*, *for example* or a colon.
3. **Predict your own word.** A plain word is fine: "small," "not important," "made worse." If none comes, at least decide the direction: positive or negative, more or less.
4. **Match and eliminate.** Cross out every choice that doesn't match your prediction. If two are left, compare their tone (positive, negative, neutral) and strength (mild or extreme) with the passage.
5. **Check it.** Reread the passage with your answer in the blank. It should agree with the clue and the tone.

### "Most nearly means" questions

Mentally cross out the quoted word and treat it as a blank. Predict a meaning from the clues, *then* read the choices, because the test loves **second meanings**.

### When you don't know a word in the choices

- Eliminate the words you *do* know. If three clearly fail, the unknown word is the answer.
- Don't pick a word *because* it's hard. In one count of official practice tests, the hardest-looking choice was right only about a quarter of the time, no better than guessing.
- Use word parts as a hint: *in-*, *un-*, *a-* usually mean "not" (*atypical* = not typical); *-able* / *-ible* mean "can be" (*perceptible* = can be noticed).
- Never leave it blank. There's no penalty for guessing.

## Rules and formulas you need

### The four main clue types

| Clue type | Signal words | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | however, but, although, yet, despite, while, unlike, rather than | The blank goes the **opposite** way |
| Cause / result | because, since, so, therefore, as a result, thus | The blank is a cause or effect of the other idea |
| Restatement | colon (:), dash (—), that is, in other words, *this* / *such* + noun | The passage repeats the blank's meaning nearby |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, including | The examples show the quality the blank names |

**Continuation** words (*also*, *similarly*, *in fact*, *moreover*) keep going in the same direction as the idea before them.

> **Tip:** Restatement is one of the most common clues. Look right after the blank for a colon, a dash, or a phrase starting with "so," "such that" or "meaning"; the passage often explains the blank in plain words there.

### Everyday words with test meanings

These look easy, but the SAT tests a second meaning.

| Word | Test meaning | Example of that sense |
|---|---|---|
| qualify | limit or soften a claim | "She qualified her praise." |
| novel | new, original | "a novel method" |
| temper | moderate, tone down | "temper expectations" |
| check | stop or slow | "check the spread of a disease" |
| yield | produce | "the study yielded data" |
| account for | explain | "account for the results" |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A contrast clue

For decades, many historians treated letters written by nineteenth-century factory workers as ______ sources, useful mainly for a colorful quote or two. Recent studies, however, have shown that these letters hold detailed records of wages, hours and working conditions that appear in no official document.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) indispensable
- (B) peripheral
- (C) authoritative
- (D) contentious

**Solution.** Hide the choices and find the clue. Two clues work together. First, a restatement: the letters were seen as "useful mainly for a colorful quote or two." Second, a contrast: "however" tells us the recent view (the letters hold unique, detailed records) is the *opposite* of the old view. So the old view was that the letters were minor. Prediction: "minor" or "not central."

- (A) *indispensable* means "absolutely necessary." That's the *new* view, so it's the reversal trap.
- (B) *peripheral* means "on the edge, not central." It matches "minor."
- (C) *authoritative* means "reliable, trusted." That's positive, but the old view was dismissive.
- (D) *contentious* means "causing arguments." Nothing in the passage says historians argued about the letters, so it's a topic-related word with no support.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: Cause plus examples

Because the island has been cut off from the mainland for thousands of years, its lizards have developed traits that are ______ elsewhere: a blue-tinted tongue, unusually long toes and a diet made up mostly of flower nectar.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) widespread
- (B) absent
- (C) detrimental
- (D) celebrated

**Solution.** The cause clue is "because the island has been cut off." Long isolation makes a population *different* from others. The colon then gives examples of odd, unusual traits. So the traits are ones you *don't* find in other places. Prediction: "not found."

- (A) *widespread* means "found in many places." That's the opposite of what isolation produces.
- (B) *absent* means "not present." "Traits that are absent elsewhere" means "found only here," which matches.
- (C) *detrimental* means "harmful." The passage never says the traits hurt anything.
- (D) *celebrated* means "praised." The passage gives no one's opinion of the traits.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 3: A second meaning ("most nearly means")

In her review, the critic praised the novel's vivid setting and sharp dialogue. She was careful, though, to qualify her enthusiasm, pointing out that the final chapters felt rushed and left several plot threads unresolved.

As used in the text, what does the word "qualify" most nearly mean?

- (A) earn
- (B) limit
- (C) certify
- (D) describe

**Solution.** Cross out "qualify" and treat it as a blank: "She was careful, though, to ______ her enthusiasm." "Though" is a contrast clue: after the praise comes a step back. The restatement follows: she pointed out flaws. So she held back some of her excitement. Prediction: "hold back" or "tone down."

- (A) *earn* matches "qualify for a prize," which is the everyday meaning. It makes no sense to earn your own enthusiasm.
- (B) *limit* means "hold back or restrict." That matches the prediction.
- (C) *certify* matches "qualified" as in "a qualified nurse." That's a real meaning of the word, but the wrong one here.
- (D) *describe* is too vague. It misses the idea of pulling back that "though" and the flaws signal.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: Getting the strength right

A survey of 40 coastal towns found that towns near large seagrass meadows also tended to report bigger fish catches. The study's authors were careful not to overstate this result. They argued only that the pattern ______ a link between seagrass and fish populations, since other factors, such as water temperature, could also explain it.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) confirms
- (B) hints at
- (C) obscures
- (D) rules out

**Solution.** First the direction: the pattern (more seagrass, more fish) *supports* a link, so the blank is positive toward the link. Then the strength: the authors were "careful not to overstate," "argued only," and admitted that other factors could explain the result. So the support is weak. Prediction: "suggests."

- (A) *confirms* is the right direction but far too strong. It's exactly the overstatement the authors avoided.
- (B) *hints at* means "suggests a little." Right direction, right strength.
- (C) *obscures* means "hides or makes unclear." That's the wrong direction, because the pattern points *toward* a link.
- (D) *rules out* means "proves false." That's the opposite.

**Answer:** (B)

## Traps to avoid

Most wrong choices fall into one of these patterns.

- **The reversal.** The opposite of what the passage needs, usually from a missed *however* or *although*. It's the most common wrong answer, so cross these out first.
- **The everyday meaning.** A real definition, just not the one used here (*qualify* = "earn" in Example 3). Test the meaning against the passage, not your memory.
- **Right direction, wrong strength.** "Proves" when the passage only "suggests," *furious* when someone is just *annoyed*. Match the passage's hedging words (*may*, *some*, *only*, *tended to*).
- **Right meaning, wrong tone.** *Frugal* (careful with money, positive) and *stingy* (negative) mean nearly the same thing. Pick the feeling the passage has.
- **On topic but unsupported.** A word that fits the subject but nothing in the passage backs up, like *contentious* in Example 1.
- **The fancy-word magnet.** Picking the most impressive word because it "sounds like the SAT." Simple answers like *show*, *reduce* or *absent* win all the time.
- **"It sounds fine."** Every choice usually fits the grammar, so "it flows" proves nothing. Ask which word the clue *demands*.

> **Trap:** Don't skip what comes *after* the blank. On the Digital SAT the clue is often in the next clause or the next sentence.

## Quick checklist

- Did I read the whole passage before looking at the choices?
- Can I point to the exact clue (contrast, cause, restatement or example)?
- Did I predict a word, or at least a direction?
- Does my answer match the passage's tone *and* strength, not just its topic?
- For "most nearly means," did I pick the meaning the passage uses, not the everyday one?
- Did I reread the passage with my answer in place?
