## What this question looks like

These questions ask about the *job* of a text, or of one part of it. In each module they come right after the Words in Context questions. Expect about 3–5 per test.

There are three question stems:

- **Main purpose:** "Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?"
- **Overall structure:** "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?"
- **Function of a part:** "Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?" Sometimes only a phrase or part of a sentence is underlined.

The text is short (science, history, social science, the arts or literature, including short poems). The choices usually start with a verb: "It introduces…", "It challenges…", "It illustrates…". Structure choices come in two or three parts: "The text describes X, then explains Y."

> **Remember:** On the test, the part you are asked about is underlined. In this lesson it is shown in **bold**.

> **Key idea:** The question is not "What does the text say?" but "What is the text, or this sentence, *doing*?" A choice can describe the content correctly and still be wrong because it names the wrong job.

## The method

1. **Read the question first.** Decide the scope: the whole text (purpose or structure) or one part (function).
2. **Read the whole text and give each sentence a job label** of two to four words: "old belief", "new study", "example", "reason", "result", "limit", "author's point". On scratch paper, a few words per sentence is enough.
3. **Find the turn.** Words like *but, however, yet, still, in fact, actually* and *recently* mark where the text changes direction. The sentence after the turn, and often the last sentence, usually carries the author's real point.
4. **Predict in your own words** before you look at the choices. Use the shapes in the table below. For a function question, also ask: "What would be lost if this sentence were deleted?"
5. **Test every choice in two pieces: the verb and its object.** Does the text really do that action (illustrate, challenge, concede…)? Does it do it to that thing? For structure choices, every part must be true and in the right order. One wrong word makes the whole choice wrong.
6. **Choose the accurate, modest answer.** Right answers describe the text fairly and plainly. Wrong answers often add a debate, critics, a solution or a verb that is stronger than anything the text does.

| Question | Predict in this shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | "To (verb) (what)." | "To show that an old belief about bats is wrong." |
| Overall structure | "First X, then Y (then Z)." | "First a common assumption, then a study that tests it, then a result against it." |
| Function | "It (verb) the idea in the sentence before or after." | "It gives an example of the claim in the first sentence." |

For a function question, read the whole text anyway (it is short), but look hardest at the sentences just before and after the underlined part. A sentence's job only makes sense next to its neighbors.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Purpose, structure and function

- **Main purpose** is the author's reason for writing the whole text: to inform, explain, correct, argue, describe a discovery, portray a character, and so on.
- **Overall structure** is the order of the text's moves. It is the purpose broken into steps.
- **Function** is the job one part does for the whole: introduce, support, give an example, contrast, limit, conclude.

### Verbs in the answer choices

The verb carries the answer, so know exactly what each one requires.

| Verb | What the text must actually do |
|---|---|
| introduce, set up | bring in a topic, idea or character that the text then develops |
| present, describe | state or show something in a neutral way |
| explain | tell how or why something happens |
| illustrate, exemplify | give a specific example of a general idea |
| elaborate on | add more detail to an idea already stated |
| support, substantiate | give evidence or reasons for a claim |
| argue, contend, claim | take a position and try to convince the reader |
| emphasize, underscore | stress that something is important |
| challenge, question, dispute | say that an idea may be wrong |
| refute, disprove | show that an idea is definitely wrong (much stronger than challenge) |
| qualify | limit a claim: true, but only partly or only in some cases |
| concede, acknowledge | admit a point that seems to work against the author's own view |
| contrast | show how two things differ |
| compare | show how two things are alike, and sometimes how they differ |
| summarize | restate briefly what was already said |
| propose, recommend | suggest a plan or a solution |
| speculate | guess about something not yet known |
| criticize, critique | point out problems or weaknesses |
| evaluate, assess | judge how good, useful or strong something is |
| trace, recount | follow events or a development through time |
| characterize, portray | describe what someone or something is like |
| reveal | show something hidden, often a character's feeling |
| define, clarify | make the meaning of a term clear |
| rule out | show that one possible explanation doesn't work |

> **Trap:** Three pairs cause the most mistakes. *Challenge* (an idea may be wrong) versus *refute* (it is definitely wrong). *Qualify* (limit a claim) versus *reject* (throw it out). *Illustrate* (give an example) versus *elaborate* (add detail). When the text only hedges, the milder verb is the right one.

### Common structures

| Pattern | Typical signals |
|---|---|
| Old view, then new evidence that challenges it | "long believed", "assumed", "traditionally", then "but", "however", "a recent study" |
| Claim, then example or evidence | "for example", "one study", a named case |
| Problem, then a solution (and sometimes its limits) | "difficult", "a challenge", then "to address this", "but only" |
| Question, then investigation, then answer | "why", "how", "to find out", "found that" |
| Observation, then explanation | "noticed", then "because", "this may be due to" |
| General statement, then a specific case (or the reverse) | "in general", "many", then a named example |
| Description, then reflection (literature and poems) | a scene or object, then the speaker's thoughts or feelings about it |
| Two views side by side | "some scholars… others", "while", "whereas" |

The most common pattern in science and history texts is the first one: "People used to think X. But new evidence shows Y."

### Literature and poems

Literary texts get the same three questions. Purpose answers often use verbs like *portray*, *convey*, *reveal* and *characterize* ("to reveal a character's mixed feelings about a gift"). For structure, look for a **shift**: from past to present, from description to reflection, from one mood to another, or from what the speaker expected to what happened. You don't need deep poetry analysis. Summarize each half in a few words and match.

## Worked examples

The texts below were written for this lesson. Some of the people and studies in them are invented for practice.

### Example 1: Main purpose of a science text

The phrase "blind as a bat" is still common in English, and many people believe that bats cannot see at all. In fact, no bat species is blind. Many fruit bats have large eyes and find food mainly by sight and smell, and even the insect-eating bats that hunt by echolocation (making calls and listening for the echoes) use their eyes too, for example to find their way over long distances.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

- (A) To explain how echolocation allows some bats to hunt insects in the dark
- (B) To compare how well fruit bats and insect-eating bats find food
- (C) To argue that scientists have overstated the importance of echolocation
- (D) To correct a widespread but mistaken belief about bats

**Solution.** Labels: popular belief (bats can't see) → "In fact" (the turn) → no bat is blind → examples of bats using their eyes. Prediction: "to show that the belief that bats are blind is wrong."

- (A) Echolocation appears only in a short side note in parentheses. The text never explains how it works, so this is too narrow.
- (B) Both kinds of bats are mentioned, but the text never says which finds food better. This comparison is invented.
- (C) No scientists' views appear, and the text never says echolocation matters less than people think.
- (D) Matches. The belief is widespread ("still common"), and the text shows it is mistaken.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 2: Overall structure of a social science text

Many low-income neighborhoods have no full-service grocery store nearby, and city leaders have often assumed that opening a supermarket in such an area would improve what residents eat. To test this idea, a team of economists tracked the purchases of thousands of households before and after new supermarkets opened near their homes. Families did start shopping at the new stores, but the foods they bought barely changed. The team concluded that long-standing habits and preferences shape diets more than the distance to a store does.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

- (A) It describes a problem, compares two competing solutions to it, and recommends the less expensive one.
- (B) It presents a common assumption about how to solve a problem, describes a study that tested the assumption, and reports results that did not support it.
- (C) It presents a common assumption about a problem, explains why city leaders came to hold it, and gives examples of cities where it proved correct.
- (D) It reports a surprising finding, explains the method behind it, and argues that the finding applies only to large cities.

**Solution.** Labels: problem and common assumption (a supermarket will improve diets) → a study that tested it → result: purchases barely changed → conclusion: other things matter more. Prediction: "assumption, then a test, then a result against the assumption."

- (A) Only one solution appears, opening a supermarket, and the text recommends nothing.
- (B) Every part matches, in the right order.
- (C) The first part is right, but the text never explains why leaders held the belief, and its evidence goes against the belief. A typical half-right trap.
- (D) The order is wrong: the assumption and the method come before the result. Nothing limits the finding to large cities either.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 3: Function of a sentence in a history text

The libraries that opened in many British mining and mill towns in the late 1800s are often remembered as gifts from wealthy owners to their workers. **Owners did pay for many of the buildings, and a few took a real interest in their workers' education.** Yet the libraries' own records tell a fuller story: workers paid for most of the books through small weekly contributions, chose new titles through elected committees, and kept many libraries running long after the owners had lost interest.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

- (A) It provides the main evidence for the text's claim that the libraries were chiefly the owners' achievement.
- (B) It introduces the library records that the rest of the text examines.
- (C) It admits that part of a popular view is accurate before the text shows what that view leaves out.
- (D) It explains why many owners eventually lost interest in the libraries.

**Solution.** Labels: popular view (gifts from owners) → bold sentence: yes, owners did pay → "Yet" (the turn) → the records show the workers' large role. The words "did pay" signal that the author is granting a point before turning against the popular view. Prediction: "It concedes part of the popular view."

- (A) The text's claim is the opposite: workers played a bigger role than people remember. This choice mistakes a concession for the main point.
- (B) The records first appear in the next sentence, not this one. This describes the wrong part.
- (C) Matches. "Did pay" admits the true part, and the next sentence shows the missing part, the workers' role.
- (D) Losing interest comes up only at the end, and no reason for it is given.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 4: Overall structure of a poem

The following text is from a poem.

All winter the river lay under its lid of ice, / gray and still as a road no one travels, / and every morning I walked along its bank / believing it had given up. / Then, one March day, kneeling to tie my boot, / I heard it: under the ice, a low and steady talking, / the water going on without me, / busy with its long errand to the sea.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

- (A) The speaker describes a scene that seems lifeless and then notices something showing it was active all along.
- (B) The speaker recalls a happy memory of a river and then explains why the memory now brings sadness.
- (C) The speaker describes a frozen river and then warns the reader about the dangers of walking on ice.
- (D) The speaker describes a lively, noisy river and then explains how winter made it fall silent.

**Solution.** First half (up to "given up"): the frozen river looks dead, and the speaker thinks it has stopped. The turn: "Then, one March day". Second half: the speaker hears water moving under the ice, "going on" the whole time. Prediction: "it looks dead, then the speaker finds out it was alive."

- (A) Matches both halves, in order.
- (B) There is no happy memory and no sadness. The change goes from a wrong impression to a discovery.
- (C) The first part fits, but nothing warns about walking on ice. Half right.
- (D) This reverses the order. The poem moves from silence to sound, not from sound to silence.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 5: Main purpose of a text about music history

Louis Armstrong is often credited with inventing scat singing (improvising with nonsense syllables instead of words) on his 1926 recording of "Heebie Jeebies." Older recordings show, however, that other performers, including the vaudeville singer Gene Greene as early as 1911, had already scatted on record. Still, Armstrong's recording became a hit and inspired countless singers to try the technique, so his reputation as the musician who brought scat to a wide audience is well deserved.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

- (A) To refute the claim that Armstrong played an important part in the history of scat singing
- (B) To qualify a common claim about Armstrong by separating inventing scat singing from making it popular
- (C) To defend Armstrong against critics who say his recordings lacked originality
- (D) To describe the 1926 recording that first introduced scat singing to the world

**Solution.** Labels: common claim (Armstrong invented scat) → "however": others did it earlier → "Still": he made it popular, so his reputation is deserved. The text neither accepts the claim fully nor throws it out. It narrows it. Prediction: "to correct part of a claim: Armstrong didn't invent scat, but he did make it popular."

- (A) Refute is far too strong. The last sentence says his reputation is "well deserved".
- (B) Qualify means to limit a claim, which is exactly what the text does.
- (C) No critics appear, and it is the text itself that says he didn't invent scat. An invented element.
- (D) The text says the opposite: other singers had scatted on record years earlier. It also never describes the recording itself. This trap catches students who read only the first sentence.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 6: Function of a sentence in a study report

Invasive rats eat the eggs and chicks of many island seabirds. In 2015, conservationists removed all the rats from a group of small islands and then counted seabird nests there for five years; the number of nests rose by about 40 percent. **On nearby islands where the rats were left in place, nest counts over the same five years stayed almost flat.** The team concluded that removing the rats, rather than a change in ocean conditions across the region, was the main reason the colonies grew.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

- (A) It reports a second result that contradicts the team's main finding.
- (B) It describes the method the conservationists used to remove the rats.
- (C) It presents a comparison that helps rule out another possible explanation for the rise in nests.
- (D) It qualifies the team's conclusion by pointing out that nest numbers did not rise on every island.

**Solution.** Labels: problem (rats eat eggs) → action and result (rats removed, nests up 40%) → bold sentence: islands that kept their rats, no change → conclusion (the rats, not the ocean). Ask what would be lost without the bold sentence. If ocean conditions had improved across the whole region, the islands with rats would have gained nests too. They didn't, so the comparison removes that other explanation. Prediction: "It shows the rise wasn't caused by something that affected the whole region."

- (A) The islands that kept their rats support the main finding; they don't contradict it.
- (B) The text never says how the rats were removed.
- (C) Matches. "Comparison" and "rule out another explanation" are both accurate, and the last sentence names the explanation that is ruled out.
- (D) The sentence is true to the facts, but it doesn't limit the conclusion; it makes the conclusion stronger. Qualify is the wrong job.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **Says, not does.** The choice summarizes the content correctly but names the wrong job. The question asks what the text *does*.
- **Half right.** In structure questions, the first part matches and a later part doesn't, or the parts are in the wrong order (Examples 2 and 4). Check every piece.
- **Wrong part.** The choice correctly describes a different sentence, often the next one (Example 3, choice B). Stay inside the underlined part.
- **Too strong.** *Refute* when the text only *challenges*, *reject* when it only *qualifies*, *argue* when it only *describes* (Example 5).
- **Invented element.** A debate, critics, a comparison, a recommendation or a solution that never appears in the text.
- **Detail as purpose.** A choice built on one detail, often something in parentheses or a single image, presented as the main purpose or the whole structure.
- **Opinion the text doesn't hold.** Words like *admirable*, *misguided* or *unfortunate* in a choice when the text is neutral.

> **Tip:** When two choices are left, compare their verbs. Ask which verb you could defend by pointing at a specific word or sentence in the text. The one you can point to is the answer.

## Quick checklist

- Did I check the scope: the whole text or one part?
- Did I give each sentence a job label and find the turn?
- Did I predict a verb and an object before reading the choices?
- Is every word of my choice true, including how strong the verb is?
- For structure: are all the parts right, and in the right order?
- For function: does my choice describe the underlined part, not its neighbor?
