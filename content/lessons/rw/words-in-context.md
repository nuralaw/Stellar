## A blank the text proves

These questions open every Reading and Writing module. They come in two forms:

- **Fill the blank:** "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?"
- **Word meaning:** "As used in the text, what does the word *X* most nearly mean?"

All four choices are real words that usually fit the grammar, so "it sounds fine" proves nothing. The text always holds a **clue** that proves which word belongs.

```quiz
Lena's houseplants are remarkably ______: she forgets to water them for weeks at a time, yet they stay green and healthy.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
- fragile :: The reverse. Plants that survive weeks without water are tough, not delicate.
- decorative :: Maybe true of houseplants, but the clue is about surviving neglect, not looks.
* hardy
- expensive :: Nothing after the colon is about price. On topic, but unsupported.
> The colon introduces the clue: the plants survive weeks without water. Predict "tough." *Hardy* means able to survive hard conditions.
```

## Find the clue, then predict

1. **Cover the choices.** Read the whole text first.
2. **Find the clue:** the words that tell you what the blank must mean.
3. **Predict** a plain word of your own: "necessary", "doubtful", "made worse".
4. **Match.** Cut choices that don't fit your prediction, then reread the text with your pick in place.

> **Tip:** The clue is often **after** the blank: past a colon or dash, in the next sentence, or in a phrase like "this caution" that names the blank's idea again.

```quiz
For the mill town of Brenton, the railway was ______. When the line closed in 1962, the mill lost its only way to ship cloth, and half the town's families moved away within ten years.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
- harmful :: The reverse. Losing the railway hurt the town, so the railway helped it.
- outdated :: Maybe true of an old line, but the clue shows how much the town needed it, not how old it was.
- noisy :: On topic for a railway, but nothing in the text is about sound.
* vital
> The clue is the whole next sentence: when the railway closed, the town fell apart. So the town depended on it. Predict "necessary." *Vital* means exactly that.
```

## Know the clue signals

Most clues come with a signal word.

| Clue | Signal words | The blank… |
|---|---|---|
| Restatement | colon, dash, *that is*, *this* + noun | is said again in other words |
| Contrast | but, however, although, yet | goes the opposite way |
| Cause | because, so, as a result | fits as a cause or a result |
| Example | for example, such as | names what the examples share |

```quiz
Critics first dismissed the singer's debut album as ______. Ten years later, however, many of the same critics ranked it among the most original records of its decade.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
- groundbreaking :: That's the critics' later view. *However* says their first view was the opposite.
* unremarkable
- controversial :: Nothing says critics argued about the album. They simply dismissed it.
- popular :: *Popular* is about how many people liked it. The contrast is with "most original," which is about quality.
> *However* sets the first view against the later one: "among the most original." So the first view was the opposite, and *dismissed* agrees. Predict "nothing special": *unremarkable*.
```

## Unknown words? Use the charge

You won't know every choice, and you don't need to.

- Cut the words you **do** know that fail. If three fail, the fourth is right, even if it's new to you.
- Check the **charge**, the feeling a word carries. Does the blank need a positive or a negative word? That alone can settle it.

> **Remember:** Don't pick a word because it sounds hard. One count of the official practice tests found the hardest-looking choice was right about as often as a blind guess.

```quiz
Reviewers expected the remake to be a dull copy of the original film. Instead, they found it ______, praising its fresh jokes and fast pace.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
- tedious :: *Tedious* means boring, a negative word. *Instead* and *praising* call for a positive one.
- derivative :: *Derivative* means copied from something else. That's the dull copy reviewers expected, not what they found.
* exhilarating
- ponderous :: *Ponderous* means slow and heavy. The text praises the fast pace.
> *Instead* and *praising* make the blank positive. The other three words are all negative, so even if *exhilarating* (thrilling) were new to you, it's the only one left.
```

## Match the strength

Words with similar meanings can differ in how strong they are. Pick the strength the text supports.

| Idea | Weaker → stronger |
|---|---|
| showing it's true | suggest → support → prove |
| disagreeing | question → challenge → refute |
| being small | slight → marginal → negligible |

Hedges in the text (*may*, *some*, *only*, *might*) call for a mild word.

```quiz
The team called its finding ______: the sample was small, and the effect might disappear in a larger study. Still, the result was big enough to deserve a closer look.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
- conclusive :: Far too strong. A small sample and an effect that "might disappear" are reasons for doubt.
- negligible :: It sounds cautious, but it means "too small to matter." The text says the result was big enough to deserve a closer look.
- fabricated :: Much too harsh. Nothing suggests anyone made the data up.
* tentative
> The colon gives the reasons behind the blank: a small sample, an effect that might vanish. Those are reasons for doubt, not reasons to call the result small. Predict "not certain yet." *Tentative* means exactly that.
```

## Fit the word's partners

A word can match the idea and still not fit the words around it. Ask: **who does what to what?** A survey can *reveal* a trend, but it can't *cause* one.

In phrases, the small word is part of the meaning: *attest to* (be evidence of), *account for* (explain), *at odds with* (in conflict with), *give rise to* (cause).

```quiz
Botanists long believed a rare orchid grew on only one mountain in Peru. Then they found healthy patches of it in three countries, showing that the plant is not ______ a single peak.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
* confined to
- comparable to :: A plant isn't being compared with a mountain. "Not similar to a single peak" makes no sense.
- opposed to :: A plant can't oppose a mountain. The phrase doesn't fit its partners.
- derived from :: The finding is about where the plant grows now, not where it came from.
> Old view: one mountain. New finding: three countries. So the plant isn't limited to one place. *Confined to* means "found only in," and it's the only phrase that fits both the plant and the peak.
```

## Flip the negatives

Words like *not*, *far from*, *by no means* and *hardly* reverse the blank. Work in two moves:

1. Decide what is **true**, using the clue.
2. Pick the word for the **opposite**, because the negative flips it.

If the truth is "real," then "far from ______" needs a word meaning "fake."

```quiz
Some critics called the director's early films careless, but her notebooks show that she planned every shot in detail, sometimes for weeks. Her method was far from ______.
Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
- meticulous :: That describes what's true: she planned every detail. *Far from* flips it, so the blank needs the opposite.
- lengthy :: *Far from lengthy* would mean quick, but she planned some shots for weeks.
* haphazard
- costly :: Nothing in the text is about money.
> What's true: she planned every shot. *Far from* means "not at all," so the blank needs the opposite of careful planning. *Haphazard* means random or unplanned, close to the critics' word *careless*, which the notebooks prove wrong.
```

## The trap: the meaning you know

> **Trap:** In "most nearly means" questions, the wrong choices are often real meanings of the word, just not the one used here. Treat the quoted word as a blank, predict from the clue, then match. Never pick a meaning because it's the one you know best.

```quiz
The board refused even to entertain the proposal: members voted it down without a single minute of discussion.
As used in the text, what does the word "entertain" most nearly mean?
- amuse :: The everyday meaning. A board doesn't amuse a proposal.
* consider
- host :: A real meaning (*entertain guests*), but you host people, not proposals.
> Treat it as a blank: they refused even to ______ the proposal and voted it down with no discussion. They wouldn't think about it at all. Predict "think about": *consider*.
```

## Recap

- Read the whole text before the choices.
- Find the clue: a restatement, contrast, cause or example.
- Predict a plain word, or at least whether it's positive or negative.
- With *not*, *far from* or *by no means*, decide what's true, then flip.
- Match meaning, charge and strength, and make sure the word fits its partners.
- For "most nearly means," pick the meaning used here, not the everyday one.
