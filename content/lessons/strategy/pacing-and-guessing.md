# Your time budget
About 71 seconds in R&W, 95 in Math.

```visual
pacing-and-guessing--budget
```

## Key ideas

Each module has its own clock, so your budget is the module time divided by its questions:

\[\text{budget} = \frac{\text{module minutes} \cdot 60}{\text{number of questions}}\]

Reading and Writing (R&W): \(\frac{32 \cdot 60}{27} \approx 71\) seconds. Math: \(\frac{35 \cdot 60}{22} \approx 95\) seconds.

These are averages. Easy questions should take less, and hard ones more. In Math the questions climb from easy to hard, so go fast at the start and bank time. In R&W, Conventions questions are usually the quickest and Information and Ideas the slowest.

**Check your pace** at any moment: divide the seconds you have left by the questions you have left (counting the one you are on). If the answer is below your budget, you are behind.

```example
Math module. 20 minutes left, and you are starting question 8 of 22. Are you on pace?
1. \(22 - 7 = 15\) questions left :: Count the question you are on.
2. \(20 \cdot 60 = 1200\) seconds left :: Minutes to seconds.
3. \(1200 \div 15 = 80\) seconds each :: Below the 95-second budget.
4. Slightly behind: speed up :: Bank time on the easier ones.
```

```theory
The **budget** is only an average. A Words in Context question can take 20 seconds, and a paired-text question can take two minutes. The point of knowing 71 and 95 seconds is to notice when you are far above them for no reason.

Here are two checkpoints, based on those averages. In Math, when you start question 12, about 17 minutes should be left. In R&W, when you start question 14, about 16 or 17 minutes should be left. If you have much less, you are behind and should move faster, using guess and flag on questions that stall.

Question types take different amounts of time. College Board says Information and Ideas questions are usually the longest, Standard English Conventions usually the shortest, and Craft and Structure and Expression of Ideas in between. Math questions set in a word-problem context usually take longer than pure equations. A quick pass through the fast ones leaves more time for the slow ones.

Many students do two passes. On the first pass, answer what you can within your budget and flag the rest, always after choosing an answer. On the second pass, return to the flagged ones with the time that is left. This works because you never pour a long time into one question before seeing the others.

Module 2 can be harder if module 1 went well, so questions may take longer there. Keep your budget, and check your pace more often. Spare time does not carry over to the next module.
```

# Stuck? Guess, flag, move on
A stuck question steals time from easy ones.

```visual
pacing-and-guessing
```

## Key ideas

Give every question a deadline: about your budget (71 seconds in R&W, 95 in Math). When the deadline passes and you have **no plan**, stop. Then:

1. Cross out what you can.
2. Pick a choice.
3. Flag the question.
4. Move on.

Come back only if time is left. A question you cannot crack is worth the same as an easy question you never reach, so two minutes on one hard question can cost you two easy ones.

The deadline is for questions with **no plan**. If you have found the method and need 20 more seconds, finish.

**Trap:** re-reading the same question again and again, hoping it will click. Move on. You often see the answer when you return with fresh eyes.

```example
R&W question 6. After 80 seconds you have narrowed it to B and D, and you still cannot decide.
1. 80 seconds is over the 71-second budget :: Time to stop.
2. Pick B or D :: One in two beats one in four.
3. Flag it :: A bookmark to return to if time is left.
4. Go to question 7 :: Fresh start.
```

```theory
Why move on? Every question is worth about the same to you, but they do not cost the same time. College Board says it plainly: it is not necessary to answer every question correctly to score well, and grinding on a question you cannot solve is probably not worth it, because of what else you could have answered in that time. This idea is called **opportunity cost**: the cost of a choice is the best thing you gave up for it.

In Math, the questions climb from easy to hard, so a stuck question in the first half is unusual. Check that you read it correctly, and then guess and flag. In R&W, questions are not strictly ordered by difficulty, so the next question may well be easier. Moving on pays.

Flag sparingly. Flag only questions where you made a real guess and think a second look may help. If you flag half the module, the review screen is useless. Keep your flags few.

One more rule: do not decide that a question is unscored and skip it. You cannot tell, and no question is marked.

Finally, a guess is not a failure. A two-choice guess is right half the time, and returning later often turns it into a sure answer. Moving on is a plan, and giving up is not.
```

# Cross out, then guess
Fewer choices, better odds.

```visual
pacing-and-guessing--cross-out
```

## Key ideas

Each choice you cross out raises your chance of a correct guess:

- 4 choices left: 1 in 4
- 3 choices left: 1 in 3
- 2 choices left: 1 in 2

So before you guess, spend a few seconds crossing out. Use the cross-out tool in Bluebook.

**Math:** cross out values that cannot happen: a probability above 1, a negative length, a result that must be bigger than a number in the problem, or the wrong sign. **R&W:** cross out choices that contradict the passage, go off topic, or say more than the passage supports.

**Trap:** cross out only what you can justify. Removing the right answer by accident turns a good guess into a certain miss.

```example
A case is discounted 25 percent, then a 10 percent fee is added. The final price is $82.50. What was the original price?
Choices: $55 / $100 / $110 / $150
1. \(0.75 \cdot 1.10 = 0.825\) :: The net change is below 1, so the price shrinks.
2. Original is above $82.50: cross out $55 :: Three choices left: 1 in 3.
3. Test $100: \(100 \cdot 0.825 = 82.50\) :: It matches.
```

```theory
Wrong choices on the SAT are not random: they are built from common mistakes. So if your own work lands on one of the choices, think about whether you made a typical error: forgetting to square, adding instead of subtracting, answering a different question. A choice that you reach by a mistake is probably a distractor.

Math eliminations are quick, because they use properties that a correct answer must have. A probability lies between 0 and 1. A length, an area or a count is not negative. A percent change that is a discount followed by a smaller tax lowers the price. A quantity that must be a whole number cannot be 2.5. If your estimate puts the answer near 40, choices like 4 or 400 can go.

In R&W, a choice is out if it contradicts the text, is about something the text does not say, or goes further than the text supports. A choice with a correct idea but an unsupported extra claim is also out.

Elimination should take seconds, not minutes. If you cannot rule anything out after one pass, guess among the four and move on.

For a blind guess, pick one letter and use it every time. No letter is more likely to be correct, so a fixed letter simply saves you the effort of deciding.

The odds are easy to compute: with 2 choices left, 6 guesses give about 3 correct answers. With 4 choices left, they give about 1.5.
```

# Out of time: fill blanks
Any answer beats a blank.

```visual
pacing-and-guessing--fill-blanks
```

## Key ideas

A blank is a sure zero. A guess is a chance. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so **every question must have an answer when time ends**.

When the clock gets short and several questions are still blank:

1. **Stop solving.** About a minute from the end, or sooner if many are blank.
2. **Fill every blank.** Multiple choice: click a choice. Typed answer: enter a number. Use one letter for all blind guesses.
3. **Improve one at a time.** With the seconds left, solve a single question and change its guess only if you solve it.

```example
50 seconds left. Multiple-choice questions 24, 25 and 26 are blank. Each needs about 80 seconds.
1. 50 seconds is less than 80 :: You cannot solve even one.
2. Click a choice on all three :: About 10 seconds in total.
3. Three 1-in-4 chances :: Not three zeros.
4. Work on question 24 with the rest :: Change the guess only if you solve it.
```

```theory
Why is a guess worth it? Take one blank multiple-choice question. Blank: 0 chance. Guess: a 1-in-4 chance of one point. For three blanks, guessing gives about 0.75 of a point, and leaving them blank gives 0. With no penalty, the guess is always at least as good.

Timing matters. If you wait until the last seconds, there may not be time to click all the blanks. A good habit is to look at the review screen (Check Your Work) while a minute or two remain. It lists unanswered questions, and you can fill them before the clock ends. Bluebook lets you hide the timer, but it reappears when 5 minutes remain: treat that as your signal to plan the finish.

Typed-answer questions cannot be crossed out, so a guess is a long shot. But it costs only a few seconds and cannot lower your score. If you have to choose between a multiple-choice blank and a typed blank in the last seconds, take the multiple-choice one first: the chance is 1 in 4 instead of a small one.

Do not try to work out which questions are scored. You cannot, so fill them all.

Blind guesses are best done with one fixed letter. No letter is correct more often than another, so a fixed letter is as good as any and saves time.
```

# Trap: flag, not answer
A flag is a bookmark, not a guess.

```visual
pacing-and-guessing--flag-blank
```

## Key ideas

The **flag** (Mark for Review) puts a bookmark on a question, so you can find it again in the review screen. It does **not** save an answer. The test scores the answer you selected, never the flag.

A question that is flagged and blank scores zero if time runs out before you return.

The safe order is always:

1. Choose an answer, even a guess.
2. Flag the question.
3. Move on.

At the end, use the review screen to revisit the flagged questions with the time you have left.

**Trap:** flagging a hard question and leaving it empty "for later". If the module ends first, you lose the chance for a point.

```example
You flag question 16 without choosing an answer, planning to return. The timer reaches 0:00 while you are on question 24.
1. Question 16: flagged, still blank :: A flag holds no answer.
2. 0:00: the module closes :: You never got back.
3. Blank scores zero :: A guess had a 1-in-4 chance.
4. Fix: choose a guess first, then flag :: The flag only marks it.
```

```theory
Think of two separate things on every question. The **answer** is what the test scores. The **flag** is a note to yourself. They do not affect each other. You can have an answer without a flag, a flag without an answer, both, or neither. Only the answer earns points.

The review screen shows flagged and unanswered questions, so a flagged blank appears twice in its own way. But if the clock runs out, the module closes and you cannot go back, so everything on that screen that is still blank stays blank.

Why do students flag blanks? They plan to return and answer properly, which feels safe. But planning to return is exactly what fails when time is short. A guess entered first costs a second and removes the risk.

Flags are for reminders about quality: "I guessed this, check it", "I want another look at the second sentence". Flag too many, and the screen shows you nothing. Keep them few.

Notes on scratch paper are also not answers: writing "14" on paper does not enter anything in Bluebook. If you want to remember a question, flag it, after you choose an answer.
```

# Check Your Work
Spare minutes: blanks first, flags second.

```visual
pacing-and-guessing--check-screen
```

## Key ideas

When you reach the end of a module and time remains, Bluebook shows the **Check Your Work** screen. It lists your **flagged** and **unanswered** questions, and you can jump to any question in the module.

Use the spare minutes in this order:

1. **Blanks first.** Solve or guess every blank.
2. **Flagged questions with an answer.** Recheck the ones you guessed.
3. **Everything else**, only if time is left. Change an answer only when you have a specific reason.

Each module has its own clock. Spare minutes never carry over to the next module, so there is nothing to save. Prep books disagree on whether you can end a module early, so do not count on it.

**Trap:** rushing module 1. It decides how hard module 2 is, so accuracy there matters most.

```example
R&W module 1: 4 minutes left. The review screen shows question 12 blank, and question 26 flagged with an answer.
1. Spare time does not carry over :: Use it in this module.
2. Question 12 first :: A blank is a sure zero. Guess or solve.
```

```theory
The review screen appears when you finish the last question while time remains, or when you open it from the question menu. It is the place to turn a flag into an answer. You can leave it and go back into any question, and you can return to the screen as often as you like until the clock ends.

Why blanks before flags? A blank has a chance of zero. A flagged question that already has an answer has some chance, and a quick recheck adds only a little. So filling a blank gains more than rechecking a flag.

Changing answers: research finds that changed answers go from wrong to right more often than the other way, so do not be afraid to change one. Change an answer only when you have a concrete reason, such as a misread number or a rule you now remember. A vague "maybe" is not a reason.

The timer can be hidden, but it comes back at 5 minutes remaining. That is a good moment to start thinking about the end of the module. Many students plan to reach the review screen with one or two minutes left, in case more than a few blanks remain.

Each module is timed separately and closes automatically when time expires. Once it closes, you cannot go back to it, and you cannot return to Reading and Writing after Math begins. Official College Board materials say the module ends when time expires. Some prep books describe ending a module early from the review screen, so the safest plan is to treat your time as belonging to the current module only and to use all of it.

Finally, remember that module 1 routes you. Spend your spare minutes there with extra care.
```
