## What this question looks like

You get a short passage with one blank, and the question is always the same: *"Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"*

That same question is used for punctuation questions too. Here's how to tell them apart:

- **Punctuation (Boundaries):** the words in all four choices are the same and only the commas, periods, colons, and so on change.
- **Form, Structure, and Sense:** the *words themselves* change. You might see four versions of one verb (*reveal / reveals / revealing / to reveal*), four pronouns (*it / its / their / they're*), a word with and without an apostrophe (*scientists / scientist's / scientists'*), or the same idea in four different word orders.

These questions show up in the Standard English Conventions group, which is about a quarter of the Reading and Writing section. Form, Structure, and Sense is usually the bigger half of that group, so it's worth several points on every test. The good news: there are only about six rules, and they repeat constantly.

> **Key idea:** These questions are not about what "sounds right." Your ear is easy to fool when there's a long phrase in the way. They're about finding one controlling word (the subject, the noun a pronoun points back to, a time clue) and matching it.

## The method

1. **Ask: what's different between the choices?** Don't read the passage yet. Glance at the four options and name the change. This tells you which rule is being tested (see the triage table below). It takes three seconds and keeps you from checking things that don't matter.
2. **Find the controlling word.** Each rule has a word that decides the answer. For a verb, it's the subject. For a pronoun, it's the noun it replaces. For tense, it's a time clue or the other verbs. For a modifier, it's the noun right after the comma.
3. **Strip the sentence down.** Cross out (in your head, or with the Bluebook highlighter) everything between the subject and the verb: phrases starting with *of, in, with, along with, such as*, clauses starting with *who, which, that*, and anything set off by commas or dashes. What's left is the bare skeleton: *subject + verb*.
4. **Predict the form.** Before you look back at the choices, decide what you need: "singular verb," "a main verb," "plural possessive," "the person who did the action."
5. **Eliminate, then read the winner in the full sentence.** Make sure your choice didn't create a new problem, like a second main verb or a fragment.

> **Tip:** If three choices share a feature and one doesn't (three plural verbs and one singular), the odd one out is often correct. Use this to double-check, not as a replacement for finding the subject.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Triage: what changes tells you what's tested

| What changes in the choices | Rule being tested | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| *is / are*, *shows / show* | Subject–verb agreement | The real subject: one or more than one? |
| *showed / shows / will show / had shown* | Verb tense | Time clues and the tense of nearby verbs |
| *shows / showing / to show / having shown* | Verb form (finite vs. nonfinite) | Does the sentence already have a main verb? |
| *it / its / they / their* | Pronoun agreement | The noun the pronoun replaces |
| *its / it's*, *their / they're*, apostrophes | Plurals and possessives | Does something belong to something? How many owners? |
| Same words, different order | Modifier placement | Who or what is the opening phrase describing? |
| *to study / studying / studied* inside a list | Parallel structure | The form of the other items in the list |

### Subject–verb agreement

A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. Remember the flip: for verbs in the present tense, the *singular* one usually ends in -s (*the bird sings*, *the birds sing*).

| Tricky subject | Treat it as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + *of* phrase | Match the first noun | The *box* of old photos **is** heavy. |
| Group words (*team, committee, collection, series, flock*) | Singular | The *committee* **has** voted. |
| *each, every, either, neither, one of the ___* | Singular | *One* of the bridges **was** closed. |
| Two nouns joined by *and* | Plural | The *map and the diary* **were** found. |
| *or / nor* | Match the nearer noun | Neither the coach nor the *players* **were** ready. |
| An *-ing* activity as subject | Singular | *Measuring* glaciers **takes** years. |
| *The number of* vs. *a number of* | Singular vs. plural | The number of visitors **has** grown. A number of visitors **have** left. |
| Verb before subject (inverted) | Flip it back to subject-first | Along the wall **hang** three *paintings*. |

> **Trap:** The test loves to park a noun of the *opposite* number right next to the blank. In "The results of the study ___," the nearest noun is *study*, but the subject is *results*.

### Verb form: does the sentence need a main verb?

Every sentence needs at least one **finite verb**, meaning a real working verb that has a tense and a subject (*reports, reported, has reported, is reporting*). **Nonfinite** forms can't run a sentence on their own: *reporting* (with no helper like *is*), *to report*, and *having reported*.

| Situation | What the blank needs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The sentence has a subject but no main verb yet | A finite verb | The researchers, after years of fieldwork, **published** their findings. |
| The sentence already has a main verb, and the blank adds detail after a comma | A nonfinite form, usually *-ing* | The river flooded the valley, **forcing** families to move. |
| A noun *receives* the action | Past participle (-ed / -en form) | a bridge **built** in 1890 |
| A noun *does* the action | Present participle (-ing) | a bridge **connecting** two towns |

After *has / have / had*, use the past participle: *had begun*, not *had began*; *has written*, not *has wrote*.

### Verb tense

Match the timeline the passage has already set. Look for time clues first; if there are none, copy the tense of the other verbs nearby.

| Clue | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *in 1850, last year, ago* | Simple past | She **discovered** the fossil in 2004. |
| *since, for the past decade, so far, recently* | Present perfect (*has/have* + participle) | Prices **have risen** since 2020. |
| An action that happened before another past action | Past perfect (*had* + participle) | By the time help arrived, the fire **had spread**. |
| A general fact, or what a book or study says | Simple present | The novel **follows** two sisters. |

### Pronoun agreement

A pronoun must match the noun it replaces in number. Find that noun, label it singular or plural, then pick.

| Noun it replaces | Use | Not |
|---|---|---|
| One thing, company, team, museum, city | *it, its, this, that* | *they, their, these, those* |
| Two or more things or people | *they, their, these, those* | *it, its* |

Watch comparisons too: "Her scores were higher than **those** of her classmates" (*those* = scores, plural).

Pronoun case (*he* vs. *him*, *who* vs. *whom*) is rare on the digital test. If it shows up, drop the other person: "The award went to Maya and ___" becomes "went to ___," and you'd say *me*, not *I*.

### Plurals, possessives, and look-alike words

Apostrophes on nouns show ownership. They never make a plain plural.

| Meaning | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| More than one, nothing owned | -s, no apostrophe | the *scientists* agreed |
| One owner | -'s | one *scientist's* notebook |
| Several owners (plural ending in s) | -s' | three *scientists'* notebooks |
| Plural not ending in s | -'s | the *children's* drawings |

Possessive pronouns never use apostrophes. The apostrophe versions are contractions.

| Possessive (owns something) | Contraction | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| *its* | *it's* = it is / it has | Say "it is." If it sounds wrong, use *its*. |
| *their* | *they're* = they are | Say "they are." |
| *whose* | *who's* = who is | Say "who is." |
| *your* | *you're* = you are | Say "you are." |

> **Tip:** To decide on an apostrophe, look at the word right *after* the noun. If it's a thing being owned (*the bees' hive*), you need a possessive. If it's a verb (*the bees swarm*), you need a plain plural.

### Modifier placement

When a sentence opens with a describing phrase and a comma ("Raised in Lagos, ___"), the very next noun must be the one that phrase describes. Ask: *who* was raised in Lagos? That person must come right after the comma.

### Parallel structure

Items in a list or a pair must have the same form: *to plan, to build, and to test* or *planning, building, and testing*, never *planning, to build, and tested*. Pairs like *not only… but also* and *either… or* need matching forms on both sides.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Agreement with a long interrupter

The collection of handwritten letters, maps, and diaries that historian Ana Ruiz donated to the city archive ___ new details about how the town was founded.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) reveal
- (B) have revealed
- (C) reveals
- (D) are revealing

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices change between singular and plural, so this is subject–verb agreement. Step 3: strip the sentence. Cross out "of handwritten letters, maps, and diaries" and "that historian Ana Ruiz donated to the city archive." What's left: "The collection ___ new details." *Collection* is one group, so it's singular and needs a singular verb.

- (A) *reveal* is plural. It matches *diaries* or *letters*, the trap nouns.
- (B) *have revealed* is plural (singular would be *has revealed*).
- (C) *reveals* is singular. Correct.
- (D) *are revealing* is plural.

Notice that three choices are plural and one is singular, which fits the odd-one-out tip.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: Missing main verb

In 2019, a team of marine biologists studying coral reefs off the coast of Belize ___ that some corals recover from bleaching faster when parrotfish are present.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) reporting
- (B) to report
- (C) having reported
- (D) reported

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices are four forms of *report*, mixing real verbs with *-ing* and *to* forms. That's a verb-form question, so ask: does this sentence have a main verb yet? The subject is "a team." The word *studying* only describes the biologists; it isn't the main verb. Nothing tells us what the team *did*, so the blank must be a finite verb. The clue "In 2019" also points to the past.

- (A) *reporting* with no helper can't be a main verb, so the sentence would be a fragment.
- (B) *to report* can't be a main verb either. Fragment.
- (C) *having reported* only describes; it can't run a sentence. Fragment.
- (D) *reported* is a finite past-tense verb, and it works for a singular subject. Correct.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: Pronoun plus possessive

The Kessler Botanical Garden recently reopened ___ glass conservatory, which had been closed for repairs after a winter storm cracked several of the panes.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) it's
- (B) its
- (C) their
- (D) they're

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices mix pronouns and apostrophes, so check two things: number and possession. Step 2: the pronoun replaces "The Kessler Botanical Garden," which is one place, so it's singular. The conservatory *belongs to* the garden, so we need a possessive.

- (A) *it's* means "it is." "Reopened it is glass conservatory" makes no sense.
- (B) *its* is singular and possessive. Correct.
- (C) *their* is possessive but plural. The garden is one place, so it doesn't match.
- (D) *they're* means "they are," and it's plural. Wrong on both counts.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: Who is doing the action?

Having spent three summers recording bird calls in the Andes, ___

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) the recordings that Priya Nair made were eventually turned into a free online library.
- (B) Priya Nair's recordings were eventually turned into a free online library.
- (C) Priya Nair eventually turned her recordings into a free online library.
- (D) a free online library was eventually built from Priya Nair's recordings.

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices say roughly the same thing in a different order, so this is modifier placement. Step 2: ask who "spent three summers recording bird calls." A person did, Priya Nair. So her name, as the subject, must come right after the comma.

- (A) puts *the recordings* right after the comma, which says the recordings spent three summers recording. Illogical.
- (B) is the sneaky one. *Priya Nair's* is there, but it's only describing the real subject, which is *recordings*. Recordings still didn't do the recording.
- (C) puts *Priya Nair* right after the comma. Correct.
- (D) says a library spent three summers recording bird calls. Illogical.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **Matching the nearest noun.** The noun right before the blank is often a decoy inside an *of* phrase or a *which/that* clause. Always strip back to the real subject.
- **Picking the verb that "flows."** An *-ing* form often sounds smooth, but if the sentence has no other main verb, choosing it leaves you with a fragment. Ask "what did the subject *do*?" and make sure some word answers that.
- **Adding a second main verb.** The flip side: if the sentence already has its main verb, a finite verb after a comma creates a run-on. You usually need an *-ing* form there instead.
- **Using *their* for one company, team, or group.** In everyday speech people say "the company changed their policy," but on the SAT a single organization is *it/its*.
- **The possessive in a modifier answer.** "Born in Ohio, the *author's* first novel…" looks close, but the subject is *novel*, not the author. The person has to be the subject itself, not an owner tucked in front of it.
- **Apostrophes on plain plurals.** *The 1990's*, *the scientist's agreed*, *it's wings*: if nothing is owned, or if it's a pronoun, no apostrophe.
- **Shifting tense for no reason.** If the passage is in the past and nothing signals a change, a present or future choice is wrong even though it's grammatically "a verb."
- **Wrong form after *had*.** *Had began, has went, had wrote* are wrong. Use *begun, gone, written*.
- **Judging by length or fanciness.** The correct form can be the shortest choice (*reveals*) or a longer one (*had been completed*). Only the controlling word decides.

## Quick checklist

- What changes between the four choices? Name the rule.
- Verb blank: who or what is the real subject, once you cross out the extras?
- Does the sentence already have a main verb, or does it still need one?
- Is there a time clue, or a tense set by nearby verbs?
- Pronoun: what exact noun does it replace, and is that one thing or many?
- Apostrophe: is something actually owned, and by one owner or several?
- Opening phrase: is the noun right after the comma the one doing that action?
