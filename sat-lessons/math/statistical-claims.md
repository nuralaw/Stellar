## What this question looks like

A paragraph describes a study or a survey. The key facts are hidden in the description: how the people (or animals, plants, products) were chosen, and whether the researchers assigned a treatment at random. There is nothing to calculate.

Typical question stems:

- "Which of the following is the most appropriate conclusion based on the study?"
- "Based on the design of the study, to which population can the results be extended?"
- "Which of the following best explains why the results may not represent all ...?"
- "Which of the following methods of choosing a sample is most likely to give results that represent all ...?"

How often: rare. Expect at most one per test, and many tests have none. The College Board lists this skill for the SAT only, not for the PSAT. It is still worth learning, because two rules answer almost every version, and it takes under a minute.

## The method

Ask two questions about every study, then build the conclusion from the answers.

1. **How were the subjects chosen?** At random from a population: the results apply to that population, and only to that population. Not at random (volunteers, people in one place, people who chose to answer): the results apply only to people like those in the study.
2. **How were the groups formed?** The researchers assigned the treatment at random: a cause-and-effect conclusion is allowed. The subjects chose for themselves, or the researchers only watched or asked: only an association (a link) can be concluded.
3. **Build the answer:** [causes / is associated with] + [for which group].
4. **Eliminate** choices that claim more than the design allows (a cause without random assignment, or a bigger population than the one sampled) and choices that claim less ("no conclusion can be drawn" when one can).
5. **Check the verbs and the size words:** "causes", "leads to" and "improves" claim a cause; "is associated with" claims only a link; "all", "every" and "exactly" often go too far.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Random sampling versus random assignment

| | Treatment assigned at random | Treatment not assigned at random |
|---|---|---|
| Subjects chosen at random from a population | Cause and effect, for that whole population | Association only, for that whole population |
| Subjects not chosen at random | Cause and effect, only for subjects like those in the study | Association only, only for subjects like those in the study |

> **Key idea:** Random sampling answers "who can we talk about?" Random assignment answers "can we say it causes?" They are separate questions, so check them separately.

### Experiments and observational studies

- In an **experiment**, the researchers decide who gets the treatment. When they decide at random (like flipping a coin for each person), the groups end up alike in every other way, on average. So a difference in the results can be blamed on the treatment.
- A **control group** is the group that doesn't get the treatment, so there is something to compare with.
- In an **observational study**, the researchers only watch or ask. People are already in their groups because of their own choices or situation. Other differences between the groups could explain the results, so only an association can be concluded.
- A **confounding variable** is one of those other differences. For example, people who eat breakfast every day might also sleep more, and the extra sleep could be what keeps them healthy.

### Problems with how a sample is chosen (bias)

- **Voluntary response:** people decide for themselves whether to answer (online polls, call-in shows). People with strong opinions answer more often.
- **Convenience sample:** people who are easy to reach (one class, one store, one street, one entrance).
- **Undercoverage:** part of the population can't be chosen at all (a phone survey misses people without phones).
- **Nonresponse:** many of the chosen people don't answer, and they may differ from those who do.
- A bigger sample doesn't fix any of these. 5,000 volunteers are still volunteers.

### Words that make a claim

| Claims a cause | Claims only a link |
|---|---|
| causes, leads to, results in, improves, "X increases Y", "X reduces Y", has an effect on | is associated with, is linked to, is related to, tends to, is correlated with |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Choosing a good sample

A school has 1,200 students. The student council wants to estimate the proportion of all students at the school who would use a new bike rack. Which of the following methods of choosing 100 students to survey is most likely to give an estimate that represents all students at the school?

- (A) Survey the first 100 students who walk through the school's main door one morning.
- (B) Post an online form for all students and use the first 100 responses.
- (C) Select 100 students at random from a list of all students at the school.
- (D) Survey 100 members of the school's cycling club.

**Solution.** Only choice (C) gives every student the same chance of being chosen, so it is a random sample of the whole school. Choice (A) is a convenience sample: it reaches only students who use that door at that time, and students who ride bikes might use a different entrance. Choice (B) lets students decide whether to answer, and students who care about bikes are more likely to reply. Choice (D) surveys a group that is much more likely than other students to use a bike rack.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: Random sample and random assignment

A researcher selected 200 students at random from the 3,000 students at a university. She then randomly assigned 100 of them to use a flashcard app for 15 minutes a day, and the other 100 did not use the app. After six weeks, the students who used the app had a much higher mean score on a vocabulary test than the students who didn't. Which of the following is the most appropriate conclusion?

- (A) Using the app is likely to cause higher vocabulary scores for students at this university.
- (B) Using the app is likely to cause higher vocabulary scores for all college students.
- (C) Using the app is associated with higher vocabulary scores for students at this university, but no cause-and-effect conclusion can be drawn.
- (D) Students with higher vocabulary scores are more likely to choose to use the app.

**Solution.** Question 1: the students were chosen at random from this university, so the results apply to students at this university. Question 2: the app was assigned at random, so a cause-and-effect conclusion is allowed. Together: the app is likely to cause higher scores for students at this university, which is choice (A). Choice (B) stretches the result to all college students, but only this university was sampled. Choice (C) claims too little: random assignment is exactly what allows a cause-and-effect conclusion. Choice (D) reverses the direction, and the students didn't choose anyway, because the researcher assigned the app.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 3: An observational study

Researchers selected 600 adults at random from all the adults in a state and asked them about their daily habits. On average, the adults who said they eat breakfast every day reported fewer sick days in the past year than the adults who said they don't. Which of the following is the most appropriate conclusion?

- (A) Eating breakfast every day causes adults in the state to take fewer sick days.
- (B) For adults in the state, eating breakfast every day is associated with taking fewer sick days.
- (C) For all adults in the country, eating breakfast every day is associated with taking fewer sick days.
- (D) No conclusion can be drawn, because the adults weren't randomly assigned to eat breakfast.

**Solution.** Question 1: the adults were a random sample from the state, so the results apply to adults in the state. Question 2: nobody was assigned to eat breakfast; the adults chose their own habits, so this is an observational study and only an association can be concluded. That gives choice (B). Choice (A) claims a cause, but breakfast eaters may differ in other ways, such as sleep or exercise. Choice (C) uses the wrong population. Choice (D) goes too far: without random assignment you can't conclude a cause, but you can still conclude an association.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: A poll where people choose to answer

A music streaming service posted a poll on its home page asking listeners whether they would pay $2 more per month for a version with no ads. Of the 4,800 listeners who chose to answer the poll, 71% said yes. Which of the following best explains why this result may not represent all of the service's listeners?

- (A) A sample of 4,800 listeners is too small to represent all of the service's listeners.
- (B) The listeners chose whether to answer, so the listeners who answered may be different from the listeners who didn't.
- (C) The listeners weren't randomly assigned to pay $2 more per month.
- (D) The poll didn't report a margin of error.

**Solution.** This is a voluntary response poll: people decided for themselves whether to answer. Listeners with strong feelings about ads, or the most loyal fans, are more likely to answer, so the 71% may not match all listeners. That is choice (B). Choice (A) blames the size, but 4,800 is a large sample; the problem is how the people were chosen, and a bigger self-chosen sample would have the same problem. Choice (C) mixes up two ideas: random assignment is for experiments that test a cause, and this is a survey. Choice (D) misses the point: a margin of error only measures random sampling error, so it couldn't fix a sample in which people chose themselves.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 5: Volunteers with random assignment

A sleep researcher placed an ad in a newspaper in one city and recruited 80 adult volunteers. The researcher randomly assigned 40 of the volunteers to use a white-noise machine at night and the other 40 to sleep without one. Over one month, the volunteers who used the machine slept 25 minutes longer per night, on average, than the others. Which of the following is the most appropriate conclusion?

- (A) Using a white-noise machine is likely to cause longer sleep for all adults in the city.
- (B) Using a white-noise machine is likely to cause longer sleep for people similar to the volunteers in the study.
- (C) Because the participants were volunteers, the study can't show that the white-noise machine caused the longer sleep.
- (D) Using a white-noise machine causes every person to sleep exactly 25 minutes longer per night.

**Solution.** Question 1: the subjects were volunteers who answered a newspaper ad, not a random sample of the city, so the results apply only to people like the volunteers. Question 2: the machine was assigned at random, so a cause-and-effect conclusion is allowed. Together that gives choice (B). Choice (A) stretches the result to all adults in the city, but volunteers who answer an ad aren't a random sample of the city. Choice (C) mixes up the two questions: how people were recruited limits who the results apply to, while random assignment is what allows the cause-and-effect conclusion. Choice (D) turns an average into a promise about every person.

**Answer:** (B)

## Traps to avoid

- **A cause without random assignment.** In a survey or observational study, choices with "causes", "leads to" or "improves" claim too much.
- **Too big a population.** Results from a random sample of one university, city or state don't extend to all students, all adults or the whole country.
- **"No conclusion can be drawn."** This is usually too strong. Without random assignment you can still conclude an association; with volunteers you can still conclude a cause for people like the volunteers.
- **Mixing up the two kinds of randomness.** Volunteers don't block a cause-and-effect conclusion, and a random sample doesn't turn a survey into an experiment.
- **Groups chosen by a person, not by chance.** If a coach or teacher decides who gets the treatment (for example, the strongest players), that isn't random assignment, so the groups may differ in other ways and no cause can be concluded.
- **Big sample means good sample.** A large sample chosen in a biased way is still biased.
- **Reversed direction.** "People with higher scores choose the app" turns the study around; check who chose what.
- **Average versus every person.** A difference in averages doesn't mean every person changed by that amount.

## Quick checklist

- How were the subjects chosen? Random sample: the results apply to that population only.
- How were the groups formed? Random assignment: cause is allowed. Otherwise: only an association.
- Build the answer: cause or association + which group.
- Cross out choices that claim more, or less, than the design allows.
- Watch the verbs ("causes" versus "is associated with") and words like "all", "every" and "exactly".
