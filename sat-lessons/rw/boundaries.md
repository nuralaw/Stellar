## What this question looks like

A short text, usually one or two sentences long, has one blank. Below it is the same question every time:

*Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?*

Look at the choices before anything else. In a Boundaries question they repeat the same word or words and differ in punctuation: `river,` `river;` `river:` `river`. On some questions a small word changes as well (*and*, *but*, *however*, *which*, *that*), or one mark slides to a new position.

The Form, Structure, and Sense skill uses the identical question. The difference shows up in the choices: there, the words themselves change shape (*is / are*, *its / their*, *shows / showing*). Here, the words stay put and the marks move.

**How many.** College Board says Standard English Conventions makes up about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, which is 11 to 15 questions over the two modules. It gives no separate figure for Boundaries, but in its official practice tests roughly half of the conventions questions are about punctuation. Plan on meeting about 5 to 8 of them per test.

**Where.** Each module opens with reading questions, then the conventions block, then Transitions and notes questions at the end. Inside the conventions block, punctuation and grammar questions are shuffled together and get harder as you go.

**What College Board lists for this skill.**

- **Ending a sentence:** a period or a question mark, and where one sentence stops and the next starts.
- **Linking clauses inside a sentence:** semicolons, colons, a comma with a word like *and* or *but*, and transition words like *however*.
- **Add-ons:** describing words set off by commas, dashes or parentheses.
- **Series:** commas between list items, and semicolons when the items have commas of their own.
- **Leaving marks out:** knowing when a sentence part must not be separated at all.

> **Key idea:** Each punctuation mark has a short job description, and these questions check whether a mark is doing its job. Sound plays no part. If you can't say which rule allows a mark in that spot, don't pick it.

## The method

1. **Read past the blank, all the way to the final period.** The deciding clue is often after the blank: a dash that closes an add-on, or a semicolon further along a list.
2. **Note what varies.** Which marks are on offer? Does a word such as *however*, *and* or *which* appear in some choices? The table below turns that into the rule being tested.
3. **Run the period test on both halves.** Imagine a period exactly at the blank. Would the words before it make a sentence on their own? Now imagine a capital letter after the blank. Would the words after it make a sentence on their own? Mark each half **YES** or **NO**.
4. **Let the pattern choose the mark.** YES–YES needs a sentence joiner: a period, a semicolon, a comma plus a FANBOYS word, or a colon when the second half explains the first. YES–NO can take a comma, a colon, a dash or no mark, but never a period or a semicolon. NO–YES (an opening phrase or clause) takes a comma or no mark.
5. **Check for an open add-on.** Did a comma, dash or parenthesis earlier in the sentence start a describing add-on that ends at the blank? Then the blank has to close it with the same kind of mark.
6. **Check for glued neighbors.** A verb and its object, a subject and its verb, a preposition and its object, a title and the name it belongs to: these are glued together. A blank between glued neighbors takes no mark at all.
7. **Remove look-alikes.** A period and a semicolon between the same two sentences are equally correct, so if both are offered, neither is the answer.
8. **Say the rule out loud.** Before you click, name the rule that makes your choice correct.

**What the choices are testing.** Match the pattern you noticed in step 2.

| If the choices offer... | the question is about... | so first ask... |
|---|---|---|
| period, semicolon, comma, no mark | joining or splitting clauses | Is each half a sentence by itself? |
| comma, dash or parenthesis after a describing phrase | closing an add-on | What mark opened the add-on? |
| commas or no commas around a name | necessary or extra information | Does the name tell me *which one*? |
| *however* (or *though*, *for example*) on either side of a semicolon | a transition word between clauses | What is the transition word reacting to? |
| colon, semicolon or period before a list or explanation | the colon | Does the first half make a sentence? |
| comma or semicolon between items | series | Do the items carry commas inside them? |
| question mark or period | the end of the sentence | Is the sentence asking, or reporting a question? |
| *and*, *but*, *which*, *that* or *so* in some choices | joining words | Does the word stop one half from standing alone? |

> **Tip:** The choice with no mark at all is a genuine option and often the right one. When it's on the list, test for glued neighbors before anything else.

## Rules and formulas you need

### The grammar words you need

- **Subject:** the person or thing the sentence is about. In "The river flooded," the subject is *the river*.
- **Main verb:** the verb that teams up with the subject and shows time: *floods*, *flooded*, *will flood*, *has flooded*. A word ending in *-ing* or a *to* + verb form can't do this job alone, so "The river flooding the valley" has no main verb.
- **Clause:** any group of words that has its own subject and main verb.
- **Independent clause:** a clause that works as a sentence by itself. "The river flooded" passes the period test.
- **Dependent clause:** a clause that leans on another one, because it opens with a word like *because*, *when* or *which*. "Because the river flooded" fails the period test.
- **Phrase:** a group of words without its own subject and main verb: *after the storm*, *a slow, muddy river*, *covering the fields*.
- **Fragment:** a phrase or dependent clause written as if it were a sentence. Starting with a capital letter and ending with a period doesn't make a piece complete.

These words make a clause dependent:

| Kind | Words |
|---|---|
| Time, cause, contrast, condition | *although, though, even though, because, since, as, when, whenever, while, whereas, if, unless, until, before, after, once, so that* |
| Relative words, which describe a noun | *who, whom, whose, which, that, where, when* |

> **Tip:** Judge the whole half, not its first word. "Although the bridge was old, it held" passes the period test, because the main clause "it held" is inside it.

> **Trap:** *It*, *this*, *these*, *they*, *there* and *such* never make a clause dependent. "This raised the water level" is a sentence. So a comma alone can't attach it ("The rain fell for days, this raised the water level"), while *which* can ("The rain fell for days, which raised the water level").

### Linking two sentences

When both halves pass the period test, only a few links are legal. Questions on this point show up in almost every test.

| Link between two YES halves | Verdict | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Period | Correct | The dam broke. Water flooded the valley. |
| Semicolon | Correct | The dam broke; water flooded the valley. |
| Comma + FANBOYS word | Correct | The dam broke, and water flooded the valley. |
| Colon | Correct when the second half explains the first | The dam failed for one reason: its gate had rusted shut. |
| A single dash | Correct like a colon, when the sentence has no other dash | The dam failed for one reason—its gate had rusted shut. |
| Comma by itself | Wrong: a **comma splice** | The dam broke, water flooded the valley. |
| No mark | Wrong: a **run-on**, or fused sentence | The dam broke water flooded the valley. |
| FANBOYS word without the comma | Wrong when the clauses are long | The old dam finally broke after weeks of rain and water flooded the valley below the town. |
| Comma + *however*, *therefore* or *then* | Wrong: still a comma splice | The dam broke, however, the town stayed dry. |

**FANBOYS** stands for *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*. These seven words, and no others, can link two sentences with just a comma, and the comma sits in front of the word. College Board's own answer explanations reject *and* or *but* without the comma when it links two longer clauses, so look for that comma.

> **Tip:** A semicolon is legal only where a period would be legal. Picture a period in its place. If you get two real sentences, keep the semicolon. If either side turns into a broken piece, drop it.

Because a semicolon already links the two sentences, adding *and* or *but* right after it does the same job twice. Don't choose "; and" or "; but."

### Transition words: however and the rest

*However, therefore, thus, moreover, furthermore, consequently, nevertheless, nonetheless, instead, rather, meanwhile, otherwise, also, then, later, still, indeed, in fact, as a result, for example, for instance, in addition, on the other hand.*

Grammar books call these conjunctive adverbs. They tell the reader how two ideas relate, but grammatically they are only adverbs, so they have no power to connect two sentences. Three placements are correct:

- **Linking two sentences:** period or semicolon in front, comma behind. *The dam held; however, the road washed away.*
- **Interrupting one clause:** commas on both sides. *The road, however, washed away.*
- **Opening a sentence:** comma behind. *However, the road washed away.*

> **Tip:** *However* can slide to a new spot in its clause ("The road washed away, however.") and the sentence still works. *But* can't slide anywhere. A word that slides is a transition word, and it never links sentences by itself.

**Semicolon before or after the transition word?** On harder questions both versions are offered, and only one makes sense.

- "*first clause*; however, *second clause*" tells the reader the second clause pushes against the first.
- "*first clause*, however; *second clause*" tells the reader the first clause pushes against the sentence **before** it, and the second clause backs the first one up.

So find what the transition word is reacting to. Imagine the earlier sentence was "Most visitors expect the old bridge to be fragile."

- "It is surprisingly strong, however; it once carried army trucks." Correct. *Strong* pushes against *fragile*, and the trucks are proof of strength.
- "It is surprisingly strong; however, it once carried army trucks." Wrong. It treats carrying trucks as the opposite of being strong.

The same reasoning works for *though*, *for example*, *in fact*, *therefore* and the others. The semicolon marks the real break between the two clauses, and the transition word stays with the clause whose meaning it describes.

### The colon

- **In front of a colon:** a group of words that passes the period test. No exceptions.
- **Behind a colon:** whatever explains, lists, names or illustrates something the first part set up: one word, a phrase, a list or a whole sentence. The first part makes a promise and the part after the colon pays it off.
- **Not right after words that leave the thought hanging:** *such as*, *including*, *like*, a verb such as *is*, *were* or *include*, or a preposition such as *of* or *from*.

| Wrong | Correct |
|---|---|
| The festival's goals were: to raise money and to honor local artists. | The festival's goals were simple: to raise money and to honor local artists. |
| The kit includes: a map, a compass, and a whistle. | The kit includes three tools: a map, a compass, and a whistle. |
| The kit has tools such as: a map and a compass. | The kit has tools such as a map and a compass. |

**Colon or semicolon between two sentences?** Grammar allows both, so meaning decides.

- **Colon:** the second sentence explains, proves or zooms in on the first. *The plan had one serious flaw: nobody had checked the budget.*
- **Semicolon:** the second sentence is a separate statement that adds something, contrasts or moves on. *Rosa checked the plan's design; Tomás checked its budget.* A colon here would falsely announce an explanation.

### Dashes

- **Two dashes** set off an add-on, the same way two commas do: *The tower—finished in 1889—was meant to be temporary.*
- **One dash** acts like a colon. A sentence comes before it, and an explanation, a list or a final punch comes after: *The tower was meant to be temporary—it was supposed to come down after twenty years.*
- An add-on that opens with a dash closes with a dash, never with a comma.
- When a sentence already contains a dash, don't use another dash to link two clauses. College Board calls that confusing and expects a period, semicolon or colon there.

### Add-ons and matching marks

An **add-on** is a word group that gives extra detail the sentence could live without. College Board calls it a supplement; you may also see "nonessential" or "nonrestrictive." **The delete test** tells you whether you have one: delete the words in your head. If what remains is still a correct sentence with the same basic message, they were an add-on.

| Kind of add-on | Example |
|---|---|
| A noun phrase that renames the noun before it (an **appositive**) | Lagos, Nigeria's largest city, sits on the Atlantic coast. |
| A clause starting with *which* or *who* | The violin, which belonged to her grandfather, needed new strings. |
| A describing phrase built on an *-ing* or *-ed* verb | The bridge, built in 1890, still carries traffic. |
| A transition word inside a clause | The second trial, however, failed. |
| A short aside or abbreviation | The World Health Organization (WHO) issued a warning. |

How to punctuate one:

1. **Inside the sentence:** a mark before the add-on and a mark after it, of the same kind. Comma with comma, dash with dash, parenthesis with parenthesis.
2. **At the very start or very end:** a single mark, since the sentence's own beginning or end takes the place of the other one. *Built in 1890, the bridge still carries traffic.* / *The town loves its bridge, which was built in 1890.*
3. **When the blank is the closing spot,** scan backward for the opening mark and copy its kind. That is exactly what most of these questions test.
4. Semicolons and colons never open or close an add-on.
5. Parentheses separate on their own, so no comma goes directly in front of an opening parenthesis.
6. Commas, dashes and parentheses are equally correct around an add-on. No question asks you to choose between them on taste, so when two matched versions differ only that way, look for another difference.

An add-on inside dashes may contain commas: *The prizes—a ribbon, a trophy, and a small check—were given out at noon.* Those inner commas don't close the dashes.

> **Remember:** One official practice question shows a rare exception. When an add-on that began with a comma finishes with its own pair of dashes, the second dash closes both, because Standard English doesn't put a comma right next to a dash: *The robot, designed by students from three countries—Peru, Chile, and Brazil—won first prize.* Everywhere else, the closing mark copies the opening mark.

### Necessary information: no marks

When the delete test fails, because the sentence stops making sense or you can no longer tell which one is meant, the words are **necessary** (restrictive), and they take no commas, dashes or parentheses.

- A clause that starts with *that* is necessary: *The bridge that collapsed was rebuilt.* No comma goes before this *that*.
- A clause that starts with *which* is almost always an add-on on this test, so it comes after a comma: *The bridge, which collapsed in 1990, was rebuilt.*
- A clause that starts with *who* can go either way. In *The doctor who treated me has retired*, the clause tells you which doctor, so no commas. In *Dr. Lee, who treated me, has retired*, the name already did that, so commas.
- A describing phrase can be necessary too: *The paintings stolen in 1990 have never been found.* The phrase tells you which paintings.
- A describing clause with its own verb needs its linking word: *a company that makes maps*, not *a company makes maps*, when it sits inside a longer sentence.

### Names, titles and descriptions

This pattern comes up often, and the version with no marks wins more often than students expect.

| Pattern | Marks | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A job title or description directly in front of a name | none | Marine biologist Ana Ruiz studies coral. |
| The same, with *the* in front | none | The marine biologist Ana Ruiz studies coral. |
| A name after a noun that already points to one person or thing | commas | The lab's director, Ana Ruiz, studies coral. |
| A description that follows a name | commas | Ana Ruiz, a marine biologist, studies coral. |
| A general word in front of a title | none | The novel *Things Fall Apart* came out in 1958. |
| A title after a one-of-a-kind noun | commas | Achebe's first novel, *Things Fall Apart*, came out in 1958. |

The test in one question: does the name pick out *which one*? Then leave it bare. Has the sentence already narrowed things down to exactly one (the lab's only director, a writer's first novel, a mountain's highest point)? Then the name is an add-on and takes commas.

> **Trap:** A comma between a job title and a name ("biologist, Ana Ruiz") is wrong, and so is a lone comma after the name ("biologist Ana Ruiz, studies coral"), which cuts the subject off from its verb.

### Openers

- A dependent clause at the start takes a comma where it ends: *Because the road was closed, we walked.*
- A long opening phrase takes a comma where it ends: *After three days of heavy rain, the river rose.* / *Built in 1890, the bridge still stands.* / *To protect the town, engineers built a wall.*
- The comma goes once, at the point where the opener ends and the main subject begins. No extra commas inside the opener.
- A period, semicolon or colon after an opener is wrong, since the opener can't stand alone.
- After a very short opener such as *In 2019* or *Today*, a comma is optional in ordinary writing, which means a question can't hinge on it.
- One link per join. "Although the road was closed, but we walked" links the same two clauses twice. Keep *although* or *but*, never both.

### When the main clause comes first

- In front of *because*, *if*, *when*, *until*, *before*, *after*, *since* or *unless*: usually no mark. *We walked because the road was closed.*
- In front of contrast words like *although*, *though*, *whereas*, or *while* meaning "whereas": usually a comma. *The north is dry, whereas the south gets heavy rain.*
- In front of *which*: a comma. *The road closed, which forced us to walk.*
- In front of an *-ing* phrase that adds a result or a detail: a comma. *The river flooded, covering the fields in mud.*
- In front of a noun followed by an *-ing* or *-ed* form (grammar books call this an absolute phrase): a comma. *The ship left at dawn, its sails filled with wind.* It looks like a sentence, but *filled* is not a main verb, so it fails the period test.
- In front of any of these: never a period or a semicolon, since the part that follows can't be a sentence by itself.

### Series

- Three or more items: commas between them, including one before the final *and*, as SAT texts do: *The kit holds a map, a compass, and a whistle.*
- Exactly two items joined by *and*: no comma. *a map and a compass*
- No mark before the first item, and no mark right after *such as*, *including* or *like*. A comma may come before *such as* when the examples are an add-on, but never after it.
- Items that contain their own commas are separated by **semicolons**: *The tour stops in Kyoto, Japan; Busan, South Korea; and Hanoi, Vietnam.* All the separators in such a list must match, so one semicolon later in the list means the blank needs one too.
- A colon may introduce a series, but only after words that pass the period test, and a colon never sits between two items.

### Glued neighbors: no mark allowed

| Glued neighbors | Wrong | Correct |
|---|---|---|
| A subject and its verb, however long the subject | The bridge across the river, was closed. | The bridge across the river was closed. |
| A verb and its object | The study found, that sleep helps memory. | The study found that sleep helps memory. |
| A verb like *is* or *is called* and the word that completes it | This shade of blue is called, indigo. | This shade of blue is called indigo. |
| A preposition and its object | The wall is a mix of, sand and clay. | The wall is a mix of sand and clay. |
| A job title and a name | Painter, Frida Kahlo was born in 1907. | Painter Frida Kahlo was born in 1907. |
| Two verbs or two nouns linked by *and* | Ana sanded, and painted the shelf. | Ana sanded and painted the shelf. |
| *such as* or *including* and the examples | big cats such as, lions and tigers | big cats such as lions and tigers |
| A FANBOYS word and the clause it begins | It rained, but, the game went on. | It rained, but the game went on. |
| A noun and the necessary clause that describes it | The team, that scored most, won. | The team that scored most won. |

> **Remember:** Two matching commas can sit between a subject and its verb, because together they wrap an add-on: *The bridge, built in 1890, was closed.* One mark on its own can never sit there.

### Question mark or period

- A **direct question** asks something. Its helping verb usually comes before the subject, and it ends with a question mark: *How do bats find insects in the dark?*
- An **indirect question** is a question reported inside a statement. It keeps statement order, subject then verb, and ends with a period: *Scientists wanted to know how bats find insects in the dark.*
- Check two things: the final mark and the word order inside the reported question. "Scientists wanted to know how do bats find insects?" breaks both.
- A direct question can finish a longer sentence, often after a colon: *The team faced one question: could the seeds survive the winter?*

### Quotations

- When quoted words fit the grammar of your sentence, for example after *that*, *as* or *called*, or as the object of a verb, put no mark before them: *Critics called the film "a quiet masterpiece."*
- After words that pass the period test, a colon can introduce a quotation: *The sign had one message: "Keep out."*
- After *said*, *wrote* or *asked*, a comma usually comes before a quoted sentence: *She said, "We start at dawn."*

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Two sentences, one blank

Ostriches cannot ______ are the fastest runners among living birds, reaching speeds of about 70 kilometers per hour.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) fly, they
- (B) fly they
- (C) fly; they
- (D) fly, however, they

**Solution.** Period test on the first half: "Ostriches cannot fly." YES. On the second half: "They are the fastest runners among living birds, reaching speeds of about 70 kilometers per hour." Also YES; the *-ing* phrase at its end is only extra detail. A YES–YES blank needs a sentence joiner.

(A) uses a comma by itself, which is a comma splice. (B) uses no mark, which is a run-on. (D) brings in *however*, but a transition word can't link sentences, so this is still a comma splice. (C) uses a semicolon, which is one of the legal joiners.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: A job title, a name and a closing mark

*The Two Fridas*, a 1939 double self-portrait by Mexican ______ hangs in the Museum of Modern Art in Mexico City.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) painter, Frida Kahlo,
- (B) painter Frida Kahlo—
- (C) painter Frida Kahlo
- (D) painter Frida Kahlo,

**Solution.** Read to the end, then scan backward. The comma right after the title starts an add-on: "a 1939 double self-portrait by Mexican painter Frida Kahlo." Delete it: "*The Two Fridas* hangs in the Museum of Modern Art in Mexico City." That's still a correct sentence, so the add-on needs a closing mark, and since it opened with a comma, it must close with one.

Inside the add-on, "Mexican painter" is a description directly in front of a name, and the name tells you which painter. Those words are glued neighbors.

(A) separates the description from the name with a comma. (B) closes a comma-opened add-on with a dash. (C) never closes the add-on, which leaves one lonely comma between the subject, *The Two Fridas*, and its verb, *hangs*. (D) leaves "painter Frida Kahlo" unbroken and closes the add-on with a comma.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: A colon after a full thought

Garum, a salty fish sauce that ancient Romans added to many dishes, was usually made from just two ______ fish and salt, left to ferment in the sun for weeks or even months.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) ingredients:
- (B) ingredients;
- (C) ingredients, these were
- (D) ingredients. These being

**Solution.** The first half, "Garum ... was usually made from just two ingredients," passes the period test. The commas around "a salty fish sauce that ancient Romans added to many dishes" only wrap an add-on. The second half, "fish and salt, left to ferment in the sun for weeks or even months," has no main verb, so it fails. It names the two ingredients that the first half promised.

(A) A colon follows a complete thought and pays off its promise, so it fits. (B) A semicolon needs a sentence on both sides, and the second side isn't one. (C) "These were fish and salt" is a sentence, so a comma alone between it and the first half makes a comma splice. (D) The period leaves "These being fish and salt, left to ferment in the sun for weeks or even months" standing alone, and *being* is not a main verb, so that piece is a fragment.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 4: Parentheses inside a pair of commas

Mount Kilimanjaro's highest point, Uhuru Peak (5,895 meters above sea ______ was first climbed in 1889.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) level)
- (B) level),
- (C) level)—
- (D) level);

**Solution.** Start with the comma after "point." A mountain has just one highest point, so the name "Uhuru Peak" doesn't tell you which one; it simply names a place the sentence already identified. That makes the name and its parentheses an add-on, running from "Uhuru Peak" to the closing parenthesis. It opened with a comma, so it has to close with a comma. The parentheses around the height are a smaller pair nested inside it, which is allowed.

(A) never closes the comma pair, so a single comma ends up between the subject, "Mount Kilimanjaro's highest point," and the verb, "was first climbed." (C) closes a comma-opened add-on with a dash. (D) puts a semicolon after words that fail the period test. (B) closes the parentheses and then the comma pair.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 5: Which side of however?

Deserts are often pictured as nearly empty places. The Sonoran Desert of North America is crowded with ______ supports more than 2,000 species of native plants, including the giant saguaro cactus.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) life, however; it
- (B) life; however, it
- (C) life, however, it
- (D) life however it

**Solution.** Both halves pass the period test: "The Sonoran Desert of North America is crowded with life" and "It supports more than 2,000 species of native plants." So the blank needs a semicolon, plus a comma on the other side of *however*. (C) is a comma splice and (D) is a run-on, so they're out.

To choose between (A) and (B), ask what *however* is reacting to. "Crowded with life" contradicts the sentence before it, which pictured deserts as empty. "Supports more than 2,000 species" doesn't contradict "crowded with life"; it's the evidence for it. So *however* belongs to the first clause, and the semicolon comes after it.

(B) puts the semicolon in front of *however*, which claims that having 2,000 plant species is the opposite of being crowded with life. That's backward. (A) attaches the contrast to the clause that really contrasts with the earlier sentence.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 6: A series with commas inside the items

Three airplanes mark turning points in the history of flight: the Wright Flyer, which made the first successful powered airplane flight in ______ *Spirit of St. Louis*, which Charles Lindbergh flew alone across the Atlantic in 1927; and the Bell X-1, which first flew faster than sound in 1947.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) 1903, the
- (B) 1903: the
- (C) 1903; the
- (D) 1903 the

**Solution.** The colon after "flight" opens a series of three airplanes. Keep reading: the second and third items are divided by a semicolon, "in 1927; and the Bell X-1." The list needs semicolons because every item carries a comma of its own ("the Wright Flyer, which made..."). In a series like that, every divider has to be a semicolon.

(A) uses a comma between the first two items, which clashes with the semicolon later and hides where the first item stops. (B) places a colon between two items, but a colon can only open a series. (D) puts no divider between the items at all. (C) uses a semicolon, matching the rest of the series.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **The breath trap.** A long subject makes you want to pause before the verb. Pausing has nothing to do with punctuation: one comma never separates a subject from its verb.
- **The pronoun disguise.** A second clause that begins with *it*, *this*, *they*, *these* or *there* is still a sentence of its own, so a comma alone can't attach it. A word like *which* or *who* would change that.
- **However posing as a joiner.** "Tickets sold out, however, the show was canceled" is a comma splice. Between two sentences, a transition word needs a period or semicolon in front of it.
- **Semicolon plus phrase.** A semicolon needs a sentence after it. Words that begin with *which*, *because*, *such as*, an *-ing* form or a bare noun never qualify.
- **The period that strands a piece.** A "sentence" that opens with *Because...*, *Which...*, *Hoping...* or a lone noun phrase is a fragment.
- **The clause that isn't one.** "Its sails filled with wind" or "the crowd cheering loudly" has a noun and a verb-like word, but the *-ed* or *-ing* form can't run a sentence. After a full sentence, it takes a comma, not a semicolon or a period.
- **The colon in mid-thought.** When the words before a colon stop in the middle of an idea (*such as*, *including*, *were*, *of*, *from*), the colon is wrong.
- **The colon with nothing to explain.** If the second clause contrasts with the first or only adds a separate fact, a colon falsely promises an explanation. A semicolon or period fits.
- **The mismatched or missing closer.** An add-on that starts with a dash needs a dash at its end too, and no add-on may be left open. Scan backward for the opener.
- **Commas that cut off a needed name.** "Novelist, Toni Morrison, won the Nobel Prize" splits a description from the name it requires. If the name picks out *which one*, leave it bare.
- **The FANBOYS word with no comma.** Two long sentences linked by *and*, *but* or *so* need a comma in front of the linking word.
- **The comma on the wrong side.** "but, the" and "such as, lions" put the comma after the word instead of before it.
- **Look-alike choices.** A period and a semicolon between the same two sentences are equally correct, so when both appear with nothing else changed, the answer is a different choice.
- **Stopping at the blank.** The deciding clue often comes later: a closing dash, a semicolon further down a list, or the real end of a long subject.
- **Asking when you should be reporting.** Reported questions take a period and keep statement order: "asked how bats see," not "asked how do bats see?"

## Quick checklist

- Have I read to the final period, beyond the blank?
- Did each half get a YES or NO from the period test?
- YES–YES: is my link a period, a semicolon, a comma plus FANBOYS, or (for an explanation) a colon?
- Did an earlier comma, dash or parenthesis open an add-on that my choice has to close?
- Does the blank sit between glued neighbors? Then no mark.
- If my choice is a colon or a semicolon, do the words in front of it pass the period test?
- Can I name the rule that makes my choice right?
