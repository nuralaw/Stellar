# Claim and job
Hunt the claim. Read the verb.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--claim-job
```

## Key ideas
Every question has two parts you must find before you read the choices.

- **The claim**: the idea someone states. Look for *claims, argues, concludes, hypothesizes, proposes, asserts*. Test only this claim, not the whole passage.
- **The job**: the verb in the question. **Support** makes the claim more likely. **Weaken** (or *undermine*) makes it less likely. **Illustrate** shows the claim happening in a quotation.

In an *illustrate* question the claim sits right before the colon and the blank.

The right answer for *support* and *weaken* is **new information that is not in the passage**. You do not need to prove anything. Ask: if this were true, would the claim look more likely, or less?

The classic trap is the right idea for the wrong job. A choice that supports the claim is wrong when the verb is *weaken*. Underline the verb first.

```example
Ecologist Ravi Sen claims that rooftop gardens cool the buildings under them. Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken his claim?
Choices: Roofs with gardens were 4°C cooler inside than bare roofs. / Roofs with gardens were just as warm inside as bare roofs.
1. Claim: gardens cool the building :: Find the claim first.
2. Job: weaken :: The verb is not "support".
3. (A) shows cooling :: That supports the claim. Wrong job.
4. Answer: (B) :: No cooling at all makes the claim less likely.
```

```theory
A **claim** is an idea that someone puts forward: a hypothesis (an idea that scientists test), a conclusion, an argument, an assertion. A **finding** is a result of a study or observation. **Evidence** is a finding or a quotation that bears on the claim. The SAT calls these questions "Command of Evidence: Textual", and they come in three forms: *illustrate* (a quotation from a poem, story or article shows the claim), *support* (a finding makes the claim more likely), and *weaken* (a finding makes it less likely).

Where is the claim? In science and history passages it is usually one sentence near the end, started by a word such as *hypothesizes* or *concludes*. The passage may also contain background facts and a second person's view. Those are not the claim, and a choice that fits the background but not the claim is a trap.

The words "most directly" appear in almost every support or weaken question. They are standard wording, not a hint about which choice to pick. Likewise "if true" only asks you to imagine that the finding is real.

Why does new information decide the answer? The passage already gave you the claim. The question asks what else would have to be seen. That is why you predict first: say in your own words what a world where the claim is true would look like, then match.

The usual wrong choices are: the opposite job, a finding on the same topic that has no link to the claim, a finding about a different group or place, and a choice that supports only half the claim.
```

# Support: predict, then match
Say what the claim predicts. Find that.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--predict-match
```

## Key ideas
To **support** a claim, a finding must make it more likely. You do not need to prove it. Use three moves.

1. **Shrink the claim** to one link: *this causes that*, or *group A differs from group B*.
2. **Predict** what you would see if it were true. Many questions describe an experiment with two groups. If the factor matters, the group that has it should behave differently from the group that does not.
3. **Match.** Cross out choices that are on the same topic but do not test the link.

Stay with the same group and the same place as the claim. A finding about a different species or region does not support a claim about this one.

```example
Researchers propose that lizards on one island lose their tails more often because a hunting bird lives there. Which finding would most directly support this?
Choices: On the island, lizards in areas the bird hunts lost more tails than lizards in areas the bird avoids. / On the island, lizards ate more insects in summer than in winter.
1. Claim: bird → more lost tails :: One cause, one effect.
2. Predict: more lost tails where the bird lives :: If true, what should we see?
3. (B) is about food :: Same topic, wrong link. Cross it out.
4. Answer: (A) :: Same island, more lost tails where the bird hunts.
```

```theory
A good prediction is specific about the **comparison**. A hypothesis such as "loud noise keeps birds from nesting" predicts that birds in a noisy grove build fewer nests, while birds in a quiet grove build the usual number. If the hypothesis were false, both groups would build the same number of nests. The finding that supports the claim is the one that would look different if the claim were false.

There are three recurring wrong choices. First, **same topic, wrong link**: the finding mentions the same animals or the same experiment but measures something else, such as speed, size or food. Second, **the reverse result**: the finding shows the opposite pattern, so it weakens the claim. Third, **the wrong group**: a finding about a different species or a different place. A claim about one population is not supported by news about another.

Also watch for choices that only show that something was *possible*. The fact that prey lived in the area does not show that the animals ate it. The fact that an idea sounds sensible is not evidence for it. A supporting finding must connect the cause to the effect in the real data.

When the claim is about a difference between two groups, look for the choice that states a difference between exactly those two groups, in the right direction. If the claim says one group has an advantage, the right finding often shows that group present where the other group is rare.

On hard questions the claim has more detail, such as the word *adaptation* or *because*. Read it twice and predict a result that includes that detail.
```

# Weaken: flip the prediction
Find what the claim says you should not see.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--weaken-flip
```

## Key ideas
To **weaken** a claim, pick the finding that makes it less likely. It does not have to disprove it.

Start as with support: predict what the claim says you should see. Then look for the finding that **breaks the prediction**. Four patterns repeat:

- **The opposite result.** The claim says X leads to Y, and the data show X without Y.
- **No difference.** The group with the factor does no better than the group without it.
- **Another cause.** A different explanation gives the same result. The claim is no longer needed.
- **The setup does not hold.** A step the claim depends on turns out to be missing.

A finding that supports the claim, or merely adds an interesting fact, is wrong. Reread the verb before you choose.

```example
A coach claims that a new shoe made her team faster: the team's mile times dropped by 8 seconds. Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken her claim?
Choices: Teams in other leagues wore the same shoe. / Runners who kept their old shoes also dropped 8 seconds.
1. Claim: new shoe → faster :: Find the claim.
2. Should not see: the same gain without the shoe :: Flip the prediction.
3. (A) is about other leagues :: Same topic, no link to the gain. Cross it out.
4. Answer: (B) :: Same gain without the shoe: another cause.
```

```theory
To **weaken** and to **undermine** mean the same thing: make the claim less likely to be true. A good weakener is a finding that the claim would be surprised by.

The first pattern, the opposite result, is the easiest to spot. If a student claims that a medicine lowers fever, a finding that fever stayed high in the treated group is a weakener. The second pattern is often tested with experiments: the claim says red light helps seedlings, and the finding shows that seedlings grow just as well in white light. Nothing is opposite, but nothing is different either, so the claim has lost its evidence.

The third pattern is an **alternative explanation**: someone else's reason for the same result. If the team's time dropped for another reason, such as a change in the track or the weather, the shoe may not be needed to explain it. The best weakeners often show that the control group changed too. Unequal conditions work the same way: if the treated group was tested in warmer rooms, the room could explain the difference.

The fourth pattern attacks the logic. A hypothesis can rest on an assumption, such as "people remember typed notes worse than handwritten notes". A finding that most students recopied their typed notes by hand before the test shows that the two groups studied the same way, so the hypothesis loses its support.

Wrong choices are of three kinds: findings that support the claim (the opposite job), findings that only change the subject, and findings about a different group. A tempting wrong choice often sounds scientific but only describes the study, not its result.
```

# Chains and rivals
Test the end of a chain, or compare two rivals.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--chain
```

## Key ideas
Some hypotheses are **chains**: A leads to B, B leads to C, C leads to D. To weaken or support a chain:

- **Shrink it to its end result.** Say it in one short line: *A causes D*.
- **Test the end.** If a choice shows D does not happen when A does, the whole chain fails. Breaking any single link weakens it too.
- **On a *weaken* question, a finding that only confirms a middle link is a trap.** It agrees with the chain but does not test the end result. (It supports, but does not weaken.)

Other questions give **two rivals**: two explanations for the same event. To support one over the other, pick the finding that fits your explanation and **cannot be explained by the rival**, or that breaks the rival. A fact that supports both ideas, or only the rival, does not help.

```example
Researcher Hana Ito proposes that city lights confuse moths, so moths circle street lamps and burn energy, which shortens their lives. Which finding would most directly weaken her proposal?
Choices: Moths near lamps flew more circles than moths in dark areas. / Moths near lamps lived no shorter than moths in dark areas.
1. Chain: lights → circling → energy loss → shorter life :: Four links.
2. End result: lights shorten moth lives :: Shrink the chain.
3. (A) fits the circling link :: It breaks nothing. It supports.
4. Answer: (B) :: No shorter lives breaks the end result.
```

```theory
Think of a hypothesis as a row of dominoes. A finding about the first or second domino ("the moths flew more circles") says that part of the story is true. It does not show that the last domino falls. A weakener must make the end result less likely, or knock out a link so the dominoes stop. That is why shrinking the chain to its end result is useful: the finding that tests the end is often the most direct check.

For a **support** question on a chain, the best finding often confirms the *middle* link that the question text did not already establish. In a hypothesis such as "fewer wolves lead to more deer, and deer eat saplings", the passage tells you that deer eat saplings. The link that still needs evidence is that deer numbers rise when wolf numbers fall. Look for the link that the passage only claims.

In **rival** questions, read both explanations and decide which one the question favors. Then judge each choice twice. Does it fit the favored explanation? Does it fit the rival as well? A finding that fits both explains nothing. The strongest finding is one that only your explanation predicts: for example, frogs vanished from ponds where a fungus appeared, while polluted ponds without the fungus kept their frogs.

Be careful of two traps. A finding about the **availability** of something does not show that it was used: the fact that prey lived in a pond does not show that the animals ate it. And a finding that shows that an idea **makes sense** is not evidence that it is true. Look for data about what actually happened.
```

# Illustrate: the quotation
Match every part of the claim.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--match-words
```

## Key ideas
In an *illustrate* question, the claim is stated just before the colon and the blank. Pick the quotation that **shows** it.

1. **Split the claim** into parts, at *and*, *but*, a comma, or a detail such as *deep emotional connection*.
2. **Put each quotation in plain words** and ask: what does it say or show?
3. **Match every part.** A quotation that matches one part is half-right, and so are the choices that share a keyword with the claim.

Do not choose a choice because it repeats a word from the claim. Several quotations may be about the right character and the right place, and only one shows the right feeling, action or attitude.

```example
In a story, the narrator shows that Mira is nervous but tries to hide it. Which quotation best illustrates this?
Choices: "Mira's hands shook as she waited." / "Mira smiled and joked with the guests, though she pressed her cold palms against her skirt."
1. Claim: nervous + hides it :: Two parts.
2. (A) = shaking hands :: Nervous only. Half the claim.
3. (B) = smiles, but cold palms :: Hides it and is nervous.
4. Answer: (B) :: Both parts shown.
```

```theory
**Illustrate** means "show an example of". The claim is a general statement about a character, a speaker, or an author's idea, such as "the speaker feels deep wonder and some loneliness". The quotation is a piece of the text that makes that statement concrete. These are the easiest claims questions if you slow down: the claim is given to you, and you only have to find the text that matches it.

The method is a checklist. Break the claim into the words that carry it: who does what, how, and how strongly. A claim with *both* or *but* has two parts, and a choice must contain both. A claim that mentions a time, a place or an audience adds a condition to check too. A claim that says "predicts future success" needs words that look ahead; a quotation about something that already happened is wrong.

Why is "half the claim" the usual trap? Test writers build the wrong choices from the same passage, so each one is true about the text. One may show the right feeling in the wrong character. Another shows the right topic with no feeling. Another shows the opposite feeling. Only one has all the parts.

Shared keywords are tempting but unreliable. A quotation that says "Alexandra read the papers" shares the word *Alexandra* with a claim about her emotional bond with nature, yet it shows practical knowledge, not emotion. Always compare meaning, not words.

Quotations in prose (for example from an article) work the same way. A claim about an *influence on style* needs a quotation that shows that style, not only a quotation about the group.
```

# Old words and figures
Translate first, then match the feeling.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--old-words
```

## Key ideas
Poems and older texts say things in unfamiliar ways. Before you match anything, **translate each choice into plain words**.

- Old English: *thou* = you, *thy* = your, *thro'* = through, *fain* = gladly, *woe* = deep sadness. Short forms like *o'er* and *ne'er* are just *over* and *never*.
- Figures of speech: a comparison (*like a flame*) or a picture (*leaves of gold*) stands for a feeling. Ask what it means.
- The claim may name a **feeling or attitude**, so match the feeling, not the scenery.
- A claim that says *contradictory feelings* or *both* needs a quotation with two feelings at once.

A character's words are not always the author's view. Decide whose attitude the claim describes.

```example
In a poem from 1850, the speaker longs to leave the noisy city. Which quotation best illustrates this?
Choices: "The city's gilded halls do shine so bright" / "Fain would I flee this din and seek the hills"
1. *fain* = gladly; *din* = loud noise :: Translate first.
2. (A) = the halls shine :: No wish to leave. Cross it out.
3. (B) = I would gladly flee this noise for the hills :: Leaving a noisy place.
4. Answer: (B) :: It matches the claim.
```

```theory
Many questions on the SAT use poems and plays from the eighteenth to the early twentieth century. The language is old, but the questions are about simple things: what the speaker feels, wants or says. The test does not require knowledge of the poem. The claim tells you what to look for.

A good habit is to **translate in your head, line by line**. "I wander thro' each charter'd street" becomes "I walk through each street". "Marks of weakness, marks of woe" becomes "signs of weakness and deep sadness". After translating, the right choice is usually clear because wrong choices are often about a different feeling, a different person, or a different action.

Look at the *verb* in the claim. A speaker who "addresses the poem directly to Winter" must talk to Winter, so a line that talks to his own heart is wrong. A speaker who "predicts a future triumph" must look ahead, so a line about something already done is wrong. A speaker who "refuses to obey" resists, so a line that grudgingly agrees is closer to giving in.

When the claim says *contradictory* or *ambivalent* feelings, the correct choice contains two opposite feelings together, such as "smiling through tears". A choice that shows only the smile is half the claim.

**Figurative** language compares one thing with another. The comparison is usually in the claim as a plain statement (the speaker feels old age is coming), and the quotation says it as a picture (yellow leaves hanging on a bare tree). Match the *idea*, not the picture's words.
```
