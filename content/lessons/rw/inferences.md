> **Key idea:** An inference is one small step beyond the text. It doesn't just repeat what's there, and it doesn't leap somewhere new.

## What this question looks like

The passage is a short chain of reasoning that **stops at a blank**, and you supply the missing conclusion. Topics are often a science study, a history puzzle or an economics finding.

The last words before the blank are a lead-in such as "This suggests that ______", "Therefore, ______" or "…because ______". The question almost always reads:

- "Which choice most logically completes the text?"

The College Board describes these passages as arguments cut off just before the end: everything you need is there except the final step. They're often among the hardest questions in a module, so a steady method beats gut feeling.

## The method

Work out the missing step yourself, then find the choice that says it.

1. **Read the words right before the blank first.** "Suggests that", "because", "however", "unless" tell you what goes in the blank: a conclusion, a reason, a contrast or a condition.
2. **Read the whole passage and list the facts.** Fact 1, fact 2, the twist. Skip background that doesn't connect.
3. **Find the gap.** Ask: "Given these facts, what's the next sentence the author is building toward?" The last fact before the blank is usually the key piece.
4. **Predict in a few words** before reading the choices, e.g. "so light isn't what controls it." Rough is fine.
5. **Match, then test.** Find the choice that means your prediction. Read it into the blank and check that the whole passage makes sense.
6. **Check the strength.** The answer should be exactly as strong as the evidence.

> **Tip:** Stuck after about a minute and a half? Pick your best guess, mark the question for review and move on. Come back with fresh eyes at the end of the module.

## Rules and formulas you need

The same few patterns of reasoning show up again and again.

| Pattern | The passage… | The blank says… |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis test | Tests "X causes Y"; Y doesn't follow | X probably isn't the (only) cause |
| Surprising result | Gives a clash, then a clue | What resolves the clash |
| Ruling out | "Not A. Not B either." | So probably C |
| Cause chain | A leads to B, B leads to C | So A affects C |
| Study limits | Studies one group or setting | It may not apply beyond it |
| Comparison | Ranks one thing against others | What the ranking implies, no more |

### Rules to know cold

- **Use only the passage**, plus general knowledge any high schooler has. A choice that needs a fact the passage never gave you is wrong.
- **The answer must add something.** A choice that repeats a fact from the passage doesn't complete the argument.
- **Match the strength.** One experiment earns "may", "likely" or "at least partly". But if the facts truly force a conclusion, a strong answer can be right.
- **Stay in scope.** If the passage is about one species, town or time period, the answer should be too.
- **Pick the best choice, not just a possible one.** Several choices might *possibly* be true. Only one *follows* from the facts.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Hypothesis test

Biologists hypothesized that a species of cave bat leaves its roost each evening in response to falling light levels outside. To test this, they kept a group of the bats in a cave chamber where artificial lights stayed at daytime brightness after sunset. The bats left the chamber at their usual time anyway. This result suggests that ______

- (A) the bats' departure time is controlled, at least in part, by something other than light levels.
- (B) the bats are unable to detect artificial light.
- (C) light levels have no effect on any bat behavior.
- (D) the bats would have left earlier if the chamber had been made darker.

**Solution**

Lead-in: "suggests that," so we need a conclusion. Facts: the idea was *darkness → bats leave*. The test kept the light bright. The bats left on time anyway. That's a hypothesis test that failed. Prediction: *light isn't what's controlling when they leave.*

- (A) matches, and "at least in part" is exactly the right strength for one experiment.
- (B) makes a new claim about the bats' eyesight. The passage gives no evidence about what bats can detect. Too new.
- (C) jumps from one behavior of one species to "any bat behavior." Too broad.
- (D) predicts what would happen in a different experiment that was never run. If anything, the result hints that changing the light doesn't change their timing. Unsupported.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 2: Surprising result

Pottery pieces found at an ancient village site are decorated in a style that, everywhere else in the region, doesn't appear until after 900 CE. However, charcoal from the same soil layer as the pottery has been dated to about 750 CE. Archaeologist Priya Raman notes that this soil layer shows no signs of having been disturbed, so it's unlikely that newer objects slipped down into it from above. Raman therefore concludes that the village's potters ______

- (A) were probably using the decorative style earlier than other potters in the region were.
- (B) copied the decorative style from potters elsewhere in the region.
- (C) traded their pottery throughout the region after 900 CE.
- (D) stopped making pottery before 750 CE.

**Solution**

The twist: the style looks "late," but the soil layer is "early." The next sentence rules out one explanation, that newer objects got mixed in. With mixing ruled out, the pottery really is from around 750 CE. Prediction: *this village used the style before everyone else.*

- (A) matches the prediction. "Probably" fits the evidence.
- (B) gets the timeline backward. The other potters used the style *later*, so this village couldn't have copied them. Reversed.
- (C) Trade is never mentioned, and nothing ties the pottery to after 900 CE. Too new.
- (D) The passage gives no information about when the potters stopped working. Unsupported.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 3: Limits of a study

To find out whether its new protected bike lanes make cyclists feel safer, the city of Ridgeport posted surveys at stations along the lanes. Of the 1,200 riders who filled one out, 85% said they felt safer than before the lanes were built. A city council member cautions, however, that the survey may overstate how much safer cyclists in Ridgeport as a whole feel, since ______

- (A) riders who still feel unsafe might avoid the new lanes and so were less likely to take the survey.
- (B) protected bike lanes have reduced accidents in many other cities.
- (C) 15% of the riders surveyed said they did not feel safer.
- (D) most of the city's cyclists prefer riding on streets without bike lanes.

**Solution**

Lead-in: "since," so we need a *reason*, and specifically a reason the survey might make the whole city's cyclists look happier than they are. Key fact: surveys were only at stations *on the lanes*. So only people already using the lanes were asked. Prediction: *the survey only reached people who use the lanes, who are likely the ones who like them.*

- (A) explains exactly why the sample leans positive. Note the careful wording: "might."
- (B) is about other cities and would support the lanes, not raise a concern. Off topic.
- (C) just repeats the survey result (100% − 85% = 15%). It doesn't explain why the survey might not represent all cyclists. Restatement.
- (D) is a strong claim with no evidence anywhere in the passage. Too new and too strong.

**Answer:** (A)

## Traps to avoid

Wrong choices usually go too far, go the wrong way, or go nowhere:

- **Too new:** brings in a cause, group or fact the passage never mentioned. Even if it's true in real life, it isn't supported here.
- **Too strong:** "always", "proves", "all", "only", "must" need very strong evidence. One study rarely gets you there.
- **Too broad:** takes a result about one species, city or decade and applies it to everything.
- **Reversed:** flips cause and effect, gets the timeline backward or swaps who did what. Re-check the order of events.
- **Restatement:** repeats a fact already in the passage. True, but it doesn't finish the argument.
- **Ignoring the twist:** fits the first half but ignores the "however" or the final clue, usually the most important part.
- **Plausible but unproven:** it *could* be true, but the facts don't push you toward it. You should be able to defend the right answer by pointing at the passage.
- **The fancy answer:** hard questions often have one choice with impressive vocabulary. Judge by logic, not by how smart it sounds.

## Quick checklist

- What do the words before the blank ask for: a conclusion, a reason or a contrast?
- Did I list the facts and notice the twist or last clue?
- Did I predict before reading the choices?
- Does my choice add the missing step instead of repeating a fact?
- Is it as strong as the evidence, no stronger, and in the same scope?
- Does the whole passage make sense with my choice in the blank?
