# Predict the conclusion
Read the facts. Predict before you look.

```visual
inferences--predict
```

## Key ideas
The blank is the last step of a short argument. Your job is to write that step yourself, then find it in the choices.

1. **Give each sentence a job**: background, a finding, a twist (*but, however, yet*), and the cue just before the blank (*so, therefore, suggests that*).
2. **Predict** the conclusion in your own words, starting with "So...". Do this **before** you read the choices.
3. **Match.** The right choice says what your prediction says, maybe in other words. A choice that only repeats a fact does not conclude anything.

Often the text gives a belief, then a fact that cuts against it, and the blank draws the new conclusion. It is not always that pattern: sometimes there are only facts and a result.

Do not predict a huge idea. The correct conclusion is the smallest step that the facts allow.

```example
Many sailors believed that fog slows a ship by blocking the wind. But Captain Lund's ships moved just as fast on foggy days as on clear days with the same wind. This suggests that ______
Choices: fog may not slow ships by itself. / Captain Lund's ships are faster than other ships.
1. Belief: fog slows ships :: Give each sentence a job.
2. Twist: same speed in fog :: The fact after "But".
3. So: fog is not the cause :: Predict in your own words.
4. Answer: (A) :: It matches. (B) adds a new topic.
```

```theory
An **inference** is a conclusion that the facts support. It is not stated in the text. It is shown by it. Inferences questions use the question "Which choice most logically completes the text?" The passage is short, usually four to six sentences, and the last sentence ends in a blank.

A reliable approach is to read as a detective. Sentence one sets up a topic. A middle sentence gives a finding or an observation, often with numbers or a comparison. Frequently a word such as *but*, *however*, *yet* or *although* turns the argument: it introduces a fact that surprises the earlier belief. The last words before the blank (*so*, *therefore*, *thus*, *this suggests that*, *it follows that*) tell you that a result is coming.

The prediction is a rough idea, not the exact wording. The real answer may be phrased very differently. If your prediction is "roads aren't needed", the answer might say "pigeons can navigate without roads". The two mean the same thing. You are looking for a *valid inference*: a rewording or a small step from the facts, and not an off-topic idea.

Why predict first? The wrong choices are written to sound good. If you read them first, a plausible but unsupported choice can pull your thinking away from the text. A prediction anchors you.

Some wrong choices only **repeat** a fact from the text. They are true, but a conclusion has to move forward. Other wrong choices are **new ideas**: they might be true in real life, but no sentence in the passage leads to them. Others go **too far**: they say more than the facts show.

Sometimes the final sentence is not a conclusion but a reason, a contrast or an example. The next subtopic is about those cues.
```

# Read the cue words
Because, but, for example: they set the job.

```visual
inferences--cues
```

## Key ideas
The words just before the blank tell you what kind of sentence belongs there. Turn the cue into a question, then answer it.

| Cue | The blank is... |
|---|---|
| *because, since, as* | a **reason** |
| *therefore, thus, so, suggests that* | a **result** |
| *but, however, yet, by contrast* | a **contrast** with the earlier idea |
| *for example, for instance* | an **example** of the idea just stated |
| *casting doubt on the idea that* | the **old idea** that the facts weaken |

A contrast means the blank is the opposite of what came before. In *by contrast*, the second thing should differ from the first. In *if... then*, assume the condition and follow it to its result.

```example
The mayor says the new bus lane works because 90% of riders on that lane's buses praised it. A reporter replies that the survey may mislead, since ______
Choices: the people surveyed were riders who had already chosen the bus. / the bus lane cost more than planned.
1. Cue: "since" :: The blank is a reason.
2. Question: why may the survey mislead? :: Turn the cue into a question.
3. (B) is about cost :: It does not explain the survey.
4. Answer: (A) :: Riders who chose the bus are likely fans.
```

```theory
The cue is often the best piece of the passage. The same facts can lead to different blanks depending on the words before it. After *because*, a reason must fit the sentence that comes before it: it must explain why that thing happened or is true. After *therefore*, a result must follow from the facts. After *by contrast*, the blank must show a difference. After *for example*, the blank must be a specific case of the idea just stated.

A **contrast statement** (a sentence starting with *but*, *however*, *yet*, *unlike*) is an important cue because it signals that an old idea has been replaced by a new one. The conclusion is usually the new idea. If the passage says "Scientists believed X. However, Y." the blank follows from Y, not from X.

Some questions use a **conditional**: "reasoning that if plants lacked the ability to sense light, then ______". Start with the assumption (the plants cannot sense light) and ask what must follow (they would grow the same way in a lit room and in a dark one). The correct answer often describes what you would see if the opposite of the expected ability were true.

Another cue is **"casting doubt on the idea that ______"**. Here the blank is the idea that the evidence weakens, so the right answer states the old belief, not the new result.

Not every question has a clear cue, and some answers use none of the words above. If there is no cue, use the facts alone. Do not force a cue where none exists.
```

# Stay inside the text
Same place, same group, no new facts.

```visual
inferences--scope
```

## Key ideas
A valid conclusion follows from the sentences in front of you. Draw a circle around what the text covers: **this** place, **this** group, **these** facts. A good answer stays inside it.

Wrong choices leave the circle in three ways:

- **Wider**: one river becomes all rivers, one study becomes every study.
- **A new fact**: a cause, a number or a detail the text never mentions.
- **Another topic**: it sounds related but answers a different question.

Ask of any choice: *which sentence tells me this?* If you cannot point to one, cross it out. "It could be true" is not enough.

```example
A dam was built on the Kell River in 2012. Since then the river's salmon run has shrunk by half, and salmon cannot swim past the dam's concrete wall. This suggests that ______
Choices: the dam may be one reason for the smaller salmon run. / dams harm the fish in every river. / the dam raised the river's water temperature.
1. The text covers one river, one dam :: Draw the circle there.
2. (B) = every river :: Wider than the text.
3. (C) = water temperature :: A new fact.
4. Answer: (A) :: Inside the circle.
```

```theory
Why does the SAT care? Because a conclusion is only as good as the facts that lead to it. The correct answer is not the one that is *probably true in real life*. It is the one that the passage itself shows. This is why strong background knowledge does not help here, and may even hurt: the answer you know to be true may not be in the passage.

The three wrong-choice types come up again and again. **Too wide** (also called "out of scope" or "beyond the text"): the text discusses one species and the choice talks about all species, or the text covers one decade and the choice covers all time. **New fact**: the choice adds an explanation, number or detail that the text does not give. **Another topic**: the choice shares words with the passage but is not about the same thing.

Some wrong choices are built from the passage's own **words** and put them together in a way that the passage never does ("recycled language"). Because the words match, the choice feels safe. Check the *relationship* it asserts, not only the vocabulary.

Be careful of the opposite error too. A choice can be inside the circle even if it rewords the facts. "Mining ended after the Shang dynasty" can be a valid way to say that later bronze objects lack a lead found in earlier ones, as long as the new wording follows from the facts. Paraphrase is fine. A new idea is not.

When the question asks "What does the text most strongly suggest about X?", the same rule applies. The answer must be about X and must follow from the text. Eliminate choices about something else, or choices that need an extra fact.
```

# Not stronger than the text
Never say more than the facts allow.

```visual
inferences--not-stronger
```

## Key ideas
A conclusion can **never be stronger than its evidence**. Compare each choice with the facts and ask: *does the text prove this much?*

- **Too strong:** *all, every, never, always, only, proves, will, best, must*. Cross these out unless the text says it that strongly. A result from one study or one place is not a rule for everyone.
- **Careful words** (*may, might, could, likely, suggests, tends to*) are fine when the evidence is limited. They are tools, not a formula. A choice with *may* can be wrong if it contradicts the text, and a choice with no hedge can be right if the text states the fact outright.
- **Too weak** is wrong too. If the facts point one way, "has no effect" goes against them.

Decide by comparing the choice with the facts, not by looking for a hedge word.

```example
In a small trial, 20 volunteers drank beetroot juice for a week and then ran 5 km 30 seconds faster on average. The researchers did not test other drinks or other runners. This suggests that ______
Choices: beetroot juice may help some runners go faster. / beetroot juice makes every runner faster.
1. Evidence: one small trial :: One group, one week.
2. (B) = every runner :: Too strong. The trial cannot show it.
3. Answer: (A) :: Careful, and it matches the facts.
```

```theory
The SAT expects you to be careful, like a good scientist. A single study, a handful of examples, or a pattern across one region does not prove a universal rule. That is why wrong choices that contain *all*, *always*, *never* or *will* are rarely right, unless the text itself makes such a statement. Predictions about the future ("will soon replace", "will raise") are almost always too strong because no text can promise what will happen.

But there is no magic word. Some correct answers include *may* or *likely*, and many have no hedge at all. For example, if a text says that a result was rated as more negative by one group, the correct answer can simply say that the group "rates the experience as more negative". The hedge should come from the **evidence**, not from the style of the sentence. If the facts show only a link in one study, the careful verb is right. If a fact rules something out, a firm statement is right.

Some wrong choices are too strong in a quiet way. They overgeneralize from one example ("this model suits bands better than solo artists") or make an *absolute* claim from a trend ("older speakers never use the new pronunciation" when the text says 80 percent use the old one).

A related idea is the **limit of the study**. When the group studied is unusual, such as lab mice, one coral species, or students at one university, the careful conclusion is a warning: results from this group may not carry over to others. A choice that says the results apply to everyone is too strong, and a choice that says the studies are useless is too weak. The good answer says to be careful when extending the results, and to check them in other groups.

The skill to practice is calibration: match the choice's strength to the text's strength, no stronger and no weaker.
```

# Use every fact
Link the facts. The answer needs them all.

```visual
inferences--use-every-fact
```

## Key ideas
Many passages give **two or three facts that must be put together**. The conclusion uses all of them, not one.

1. **Number the facts.** Fact 1, fact 2, fact 3.
2. **Link them.** Facts often form a chain (A leads to B, B leads to C, so A leads to C), or two findings point to one idea.
3. **Check every part of the choice.** A good choice has each piece backed by a sentence. A half-supported choice has one piece in the text and one piece new.

Be careful of **recycled language**: a choice built from the passage's words but linking them in a way that the passage does not. Check the connection, not the vocabulary.

```example
Drones can scatter tree seeds from the air. Seeds that land in loose soil sprout more often than seeds that land on hard clay. The soil on burned hillsides near Pell Ridge is loose. Therefore, ______
Choices: seeds dropped by drones on the burned hillsides at Pell Ridge may sprout well. / drones are the best way to plant seeds.
1. Fact 1: drones scatter seeds :: A way to plant.
2. Fact 2: loose soil sprouts more :: Loose soil is better.
3. Fact 3: Pell Ridge soil is loose :: Link all three.
4. Answer: (A) :: All facts used. (B) says "best", which is new.
```

```theory
An argument usually moves in steps. If a text says that heavy rain fills a reservoir, that a full reservoir lets a town water its fields, and that watered fields yield more wheat, then a conclusion must connect all three: heavy rain may lead to more wheat. A choice that mentions only the reservoir and the fields, or that jumps to something else ("the town will stop growing wheat"), leaves out a step or contradicts one.

When facts are **two findings**, the conclusion is often the idea that explains both. If two surveys of a lake found the same number of fish and birds, but the second found twice as many species overall, the extra species must come from some other group. Subtract what is the same, and look at what is left.

**Half-supported** choices are common. "The researchers believed X because of Y" may have X in the text but Y is a new cause. To check, split the choice into parts and look for the sentence behind each part. If any part has no sentence behind it, cross the choice out.

A choice may also reverse who does what. Suppose teachers find tablets easy to use, while parents think paper books look more serious. A choice that gives the teachers the parents' belief uses the right words and the wrong relationship.

If the passage uses pronouns or summary words (*this finding*, *such*, *the former*, *the latter*), go back and find what they stand for before you decide. The conclusion usually builds on exactly those phrases.
```

# Traps: link, cause, reversal
Check who does what to whom.

```visual
inferences--traps
```

## Key ideas
Three traps look like inferences but go beyond the text.

1. **Reversed cause.** The text says *A causes B*, and the choice says *B causes A*. Check which way the arrow points.
2. **A link is not a cause.** The text only says A *goes with* B (a comparison, a survey). The choice says A *caused* B. Only an experiment can show cause. The safe conclusion is that A and B *may be linked*.
3. **Could be true.** The choice is sensible or sounds like advice, but no sentence says it. A *should* is advice, not a result.

Ask: *which sentence proves this?* If the only support is "it makes sense", cross it out.

```example
Towns with more libraries also have higher graduation rates. Many things differ between the towns. This suggests that ______
Choices: libraries may be linked to higher graduation rates, but this does not show a cause. / building libraries will raise graduation rates.
1. Text: libraries go with graduation :: A comparison, not a test.
2. (B) = libraries cause graduation :: A link is not a cause.
3. Answer: (A) :: Careful, and it fits the evidence.
```

```theory
These traps share one feature: the choice uses the right words but gets the **relationship** wrong. That is the strongest sign of a wrong answer on this question type.

A **reversed cause** appears when the text describes the direction of an effect. If a style of pottery appears in town A centuries before it appears in town B, and traders traveled from A to B, the style moved from A to B. A choice saying that B's pottery influenced A reverses the arrow.

A **link without a cause** appears when the evidence is a comparison of two existing groups, a survey, or a pattern across places. Students who use laptops in class earn lower grades: this does not show that laptops lower grades, because weaker students may be the ones who choose laptops, or both may depend on a third thing such as the course. The careful verbs are *may be linked*, *is associated with* and *tends to go with*. Only a controlled experiment, in which the researchers decide who gets the treatment, can show cause. Watch for the verbs *causes, leads to, makes, raises, lowers* in a choice when the text describes only a comparison.

A **could-be-true** choice is a story you might believe but that the text never tells. A bakery's sales rise after it moves near a train station. "The bakery lowered its prices" might be true, but no sentence says it. Advice ("every park should get lights") is also a trap, because a result is not a recommendation and one example does not cover all cases.

In harder questions the trap is a **confused relationship**: the passage says one group holds one belief and another group holds a different belief, and a choice swaps them or mixes their reasons. It is wrong even if all the words appear in the text.
```

## Easy
```task
bank: rw-0473
highlight:
- "In dialects of English spoken in Scotland" :: Scottish English is the source of the "r" pattern.
- "place similar emphasis" :: The Upland South shows the same pattern.
- "Historical records show that the Upland South was colonized largely by people whose ancestors came from Scotland" :: The settlers came from Scotland. The influence ran from Scotland to the Upland South.
explain:
1. **Fact 1:** Scottish English strongly stresses the "r" sound in certain positions.
2. **Fact 2:** Upland South English stresses "r" in the same positions.
3. **Fact 3:** the Upland South was colonized largely by people with Scottish ancestors.
4. **Which way does the influence go?** Settlers moved from Scotland to the Upland South, so the speech pattern traveled that way.
5. Choice A says the Upland South dialects acquired the emphasis from Scottish dialects.
6. Choice C reverses the arrow: it says Scottish dialects were influenced by Upland South dialects. Choice D says Scots abandoned the pattern, which contradicts the shared emphasis. Choice B predicts a future spread that the text never mentions.

The answer is A.
```

## Medium
```task
Researchers surveyed 2,000 adults in one country and found that people who own a dog report lower stress levels than people who do not. The researchers did not assign anyone a dog; they only compared two groups that already existed. This finding suggests that ______
Which choice most logically completes the text?
* owning a dog may be linked to lower stress, although the survey alone cannot show that dogs cause it.
- adopting a dog will lower an adult's stress level. :: That says dogs cause the drop. The survey shows only a link.
- lower stress makes people more likely to own a dog. :: That claims the opposite cause; a survey shows no cause.
- dog owners are happier than people who own cats. :: Cats are never mentioned in the text.
highlight:
- "people who own a dog report lower stress levels than people who do not" :: The finding is a comparison of two groups.
- "did not assign anyone a dog" :: Not an experiment, so it cannot prove a cause.
- "This finding suggests that" :: Cue: a careful conclusion, with the arrow and the strength checked.
explain:
1. **Fact:** dog owners report lower stress than non-owners.
2. **How it was found:** a survey of existing groups. The researchers did not assign dogs to anyone.
3. **So:** the survey shows that two things go together. It cannot show that one causes the other, or which way the arrow points. Stressed people might be less likely to get dogs, or a third factor could explain both.
4. The careful conclusion is that dog ownership "may be linked" to lower stress, without claiming cause. That is choice A.
5. Choice B says dogs cause the drop. Choice C claims a cause in the other direction. Both claim a cause, which a comparison cannot show. Choice D brings in cats, a new topic.

The answer is A.
```

## Hard
```task
bank: rw-0005
highlight:
- "the time spent obtaining a gift will be viewed as a reflection of the gift’s thoughtfulness" :: The norm: more effort looks more thoughtful.
- "recipients tend to view digital gift cards" :: Recipients find digital cards better for use.
- "94.8 percent of participants surveyed indicated that it is more socially acceptable to give a physical gift card" :: Givers feel physical cards are more acceptable.
explain:
1. **Norm:** the time spent getting a gift is seen as a sign of thoughtfulness.
2. **Recipients:** they see digital gift cards as better to use.
3. **Givers (the survey):** 94.8% say it is more socially acceptable to give a physical card.
4. **Link:** digital cards are quick to buy online, so givers likely see them as low effort and therefore less thoughtful, even though recipients like them. Givers are likely wrong about what recipients want.
5. Choice D says exactly this.
6. Choice A puts the givers' mistake in the wrong place: it is about the effort to *use* digital cards, but the givers' reasoning is about the effort to *obtain* them. Choice B says recipients prefer physical cards, but the text says recipients find digital cards better to use. Choice C gives the recipients the givers' reasoning about effort to obtain.

The answer is D.
```
