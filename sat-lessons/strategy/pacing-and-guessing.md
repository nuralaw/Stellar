*Pacing* means spending your time where it earns the most points. The goal is not just to "finish". The goal is to answer as many questions correctly as you can, and to have an answer on every question when the timer hits zero.

## Your time budget

| Section | Time per module | Questions per module | Average per question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading and Writing | 32 minutes | 27 | about 71 seconds |
| Math | 35 minutes | 22 | about 95 seconds |

Three facts shape every pacing plan:

- **Time can't move between modules.** Each module has its own timer. With standard time, you can't leave a module early, and leftover minutes don't carry over to the next module. So spare time is only useful inside the module: use it to check your work.
- **Questions are not equally hard.** In Reading and Writing (R&W), each question type starts easier and gets harder, and then the next type starts easy again. In Math, the whole module goes from easiest to hardest.
- **A wrong answer costs nothing.** There is no penalty for wrong answers, so a blank is never better than a guess.

## Why module 1 matters so much

Your results in module 1 decide which module 2 you get. This is called *routing*.

- Do well in module 1 and you get the harder module 2, which gives you access to the highest scores. The easier module 2 limits how high your section score can go.
- About half of all test takers are routed to the harder module, so the dividing line sits near the middle of the score range.
- Both modules count toward your score. Don't "save energy" for module 2, and never miss questions on purpose: College Board's own studies found that this lowers scores.

What to do in module 1:

- Be careful on easy and medium questions. Careless mistakes there pull your module 1 result down.
- If you finish early, use every spare minute to recheck the questions you were least sure about.
- Leave nothing blank.

What to do in module 2: if it feels hard, that's normal and often a good sign. If it feels easy, keep working carefully. Bluebook doesn't tell you which module 2 you got, so don't spend any time wondering.

## A pacing plan for Reading and Writing

Each R&W module has two blocks:

- **The reading block:** roughly the first 13 to 17 questions (vocabulary, purpose and structure, paired texts, main idea, evidence, graphs and inferences). These take longer.
- **The writing block:** the remaining 10 to 14 questions (grammar, then transitions, then student notes). These are usually faster.

A plan that works for most students: about 20 minutes for the reading block, about 9 minutes for the writing block and about 3 minutes to review.

| Timer shows | Where you should be |
|---|---|
| 32:00 | starting question 1 |
| about 22:00 | around question 9 or 10 |
| about 12:00 | starting the first grammar question |
| about 3:00 | every question answered; go back to your flagged questions |

Rules that keep you on the plan:

- **Time per question:** about 30 to 60 seconds for vocabulary, grammar, transition and notes questions, and up to about 90 seconds for longer reading questions.
- **The 90-second rule:** if you've spent about 90 seconds and still can't narrow a question down to two choices, pick your best guess, flag it (with Bluebook's Mark for Review button) and move on.
- **The reset rule:** the hardest question of one type comes right before the easiest question of the next type. If you're stuck at the end of a group, the next question is probably easier, so don't let one hard question steal the time for several easy ones.
- **If you often run out of time:** many students report that R&W module 2 is where it happens, and the last questions of the module (the notes questions) are usually quick points. Try this on a practice test: do the writing block first (grammar, transitions, notes), then go back to question 1. Keep this order only if your practice scores improve.

## A pacing plan for Math

Math questions get harder as you go, so bank time early for the end of the module.

| Questions | Time budget | Timer when you finish them |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 8 (easier) | about 8 minutes | about 27:00 |
| 9 to 15 (medium) | about 10 minutes | about 17:00 |
| 16 to 22 (harder) | about 14 minutes | about 3:00 |
| Review | about 3 minutes | 0:00 |

- **The 30-second rule:** if after about 30 seconds you have no idea how to start, enter a guess, flag the question and move on. If you do have a plan, a late, hard question may take 2 to 3 minutes.
- **Don't rush the early questions.** Fast but careless work on questions 1 to 8 is the most expensive mistake there is. Bank time by knowing the methods, not by reading faster.
- **Use Desmos when it's faster** (graphing, solving systems, checking an answer), not out of habit. Students report that some questions on recent tests could not be solved quickly with Desmos alone.
- **If you finish module 1 early,** recheck your least certain answers, and check every typed answer against the entry rules (5 characters, 6 with a minus sign, no symbols).

## Skip, flag and come back

The best test takers work in two passes:

1. **Pass 1:** go through the module in order. Answer everything you can. When a question goes past your time limit, choose your best answer, flag it and move on.
2. **Pass 2:** use the time you saved to return to your flagged questions. Start with the ones where you narrowed it down to two choices, because those are the most likely to become points.

Always enter an answer *before* you flag a question. Never leave it blank "for now": you might not get back to it. The question menu shows which questions are flagged and which are still unanswered.

> **Key idea:** Skipping isn't giving up. It moves time from a question you're stuck on to questions you can actually answer.

Different teachers use different names for this. Patrick Choi calls it "now, later or last". Princeton Review calls it your "personal order of difficulty". The idea is the same.

## Guessing: never leave a blank

- A blank can never earn credit. A guess sometimes does, and a wrong answer costs nothing. So every question gets an answer.
- College Board's own advice: if you're unsure, rule out one or two choices first, then guess.
- Your chances on a four-choice question: a pure guess is 1 in 4 (25%). Rule out one choice and it becomes 1 in 3 (33%). Rule out two and it becomes 1 in 2 (50%).
- **Fast elimination in R&W:** cross out choices that are off-topic, too extreme ("always", "proves", "all"), say more than the text supports, or show the wrong relationship.
- **Fast elimination in Math:** cross out choices with the wrong sign, an impossible size (such as a probability above 1) or the wrong units. Try plugging a choice into the question.
- **Typed (student-produced response) questions:** enter your best estimate. Never leave the box empty.
- **Out of time:** enter the same letter in every remaining multiple-choice question. College Board says random guessing is unlikely to raise your score much, so this is a last-seconds tool, not a plan. But it is still better than a blank.
- **Changing answers:** change an answer when you find a real reason, such as a misread word or a calculation error, not just because of a feeling.

## Using the on-screen timer

- You can hide the timer if it makes you nervous, but check it at each checkpoint.
- When 5 minutes remain, Bluebook alerts you and the timer stays visible.
- A watch without an alarm is allowed, and it's handy for timing your 10-minute break.
- Bluebook times each student separately. Ignore it when other people in the room stand up for their break.
- If you're approved for extra time, you can move on after the standard time has passed; everyone else waits until the timer ends.

## The last two minutes

1. Open the question menu. Is anything still unanswered? Answer it now with your best guess.
2. Go to the flagged questions where you were down to two choices, and decide.
3. In Math, look at the answer preview of each typed answer once more.
4. Don't start a long new problem in the final minute. Spend it on decisions you can finish.
5. When the timer reaches zero, Bluebook saves your answers and moves on. You can't come back to this module.

## Building speed in practice

- Take full-length Bluebook practice tests under real conditions: one sitting, real timing, a 10-minute break and your phone away. Practice tests let you move on early; don't, because the real test won't.
- Practice your checkpoints until you don't need to think about them.
- After each test, review every question you missed, guessed on or spent too long on. Find your slow question types.
- Drill those types with a stopwatch in the official Student Question Bank (you can filter by skill and difficulty) or in Khan Academy's official SAT course.
- Learn the fast rules well: grammar rules, transition families and the notes-question method. Fast question types are where you bank time.
- Read a little every day, especially science and history articles, to make reading faster.
- In Math, practice Desmos until it saves time instead of costing time.

## Practice scenarios

### Example 1: Behind at a checkpoint

You're in Reading and Writing module 1. The timer shows 12:00. You're on question 11, a hard question about a table of data, and the grammar questions start at question 16. You've already spent about 90 seconds on question 11 and narrowed it down to two choices. What is the best move?

- (A) Keep working on question 11 until you are sure, because module 1 decides your routing.
- (B) Pick the better of your two choices, flag question 11 and move on.
- (C) Leave question 11 blank, flag it and come back to it later.
- (D) Go back and recheck questions 1 to 10 first, because careless mistakes hurt your routing.

**Solution.** By your plan, you should be starting the grammar questions with about 12 minutes left, so you are about five questions behind. (B) gets you back on pace and keeps a 50% chance on question 11. (A) spends time you don't have: one hard question isn't worth several easier ones. (C) risks a blank if time runs out, and a blank can never earn credit. (D) is the right idea at the wrong time: rechecking belongs at the end, after every question has an answer.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: Four minutes left in Math

You're in Math module 2. The timer shows 4:00. You're on question 20 of 22 and haven't looked at questions 21 and 22 yet. You know how to do question 20, but its long calculation will take at least 4 more minutes. What is the best move?

- (A) Finish question 20 carefully, then try questions 21 and 22 if any time is left.
- (B) Enter your best guess for question 20, flag it, read questions 21 and 22 and answer what you can, then return to question 20.
- (C) Leave questions 20 to 22 blank and use the time to recheck earlier answers.
- (D) Recheck questions 1 to 5, because the easiest questions are worth the most points.

**Solution.** (B) puts an answer on every question first and then uses the remaining time where it helps most; question 21 or 22 might be quick for you, and you can still return to question 20. (A) spends all the remaining time on one question, so questions 21 and 22 end up blank and unread. (C) leaves three blanks, and blanks never earn credit. (D) is built on a false idea: College Board doesn't say easy questions are worth more, and this plan also leaves blanks.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 3: Thirty seconds left

You're in Reading and Writing module 2 with 30 seconds left, and three questions still have no answer. What should you do?

- (A) Read the first of the three questions carefully and answer only that one.
- (B) Enter the same letter for all three, then use any seconds left to improve one of them.
- (C) Leave them blank, because random guesses can lower your score.
- (D) Use the time to finish a flagged question that you had narrowed down to two choices.

**Solution.** With seconds left, an answer beats a blank, and there's no penalty for wrong answers. (B) puts an answer on all three in a few seconds and then uses whatever time is left. (A) leaves two blanks. (C) is false: College Board says it is better to guess than to leave a question blank. (D) leaves three blanks; filling them first takes only a few seconds, and you can still go back to the flagged question afterward.

**Answer:** (B)

## Quick checklist

- I know my checkpoints: R&W, the first grammar question by about 12:00; Math, question 15 done by about 17:00.
- I guess, flag and move on after about 90 seconds in R&W, or after about 30 seconds without a plan in Math.
- I always enter an answer before I flag a question.
- I take module 1 seriously and use any spare time to recheck.
- In the last two minutes I fill every blank first, then decide flagged questions.
- I never leave a question blank: wrong answers cost nothing.
