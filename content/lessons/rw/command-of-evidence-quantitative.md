# Read the labels first
Title, headings and units come before numbers.

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--read-headings
```

## Key ideas
Most wrong choices use real numbers from the wrong place. So before you read a single value, read the **labels**:

1. **Title and caption**: what is being measured, and for whom?
2. **Row and column headings**: a table is a grid. Put one finger on the row, one on the column, and read where they meet.
3. **Units and scale**: *°C*, *percent*, *dollars per box*, *thousands*, *years before present*. A larger number is not always a later or a bigger thing.
4. **Legend** (on a graph): which line or bar is which?

On the Reading and Writing section you never have to calculate. The data are in the table, and the choices state them.

| Tank | Water temp (°C) | Fish length (cm) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 18 | 6.2 |
| B | 24 | 7.9 |

```example
A student notes that the warmer tank had ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?
Choices: water at 18°C and fish 6.2 cm long. / water at 24°C and fish 7.9 cm long. / fish that were 24 cm long.
1. Headings: °C is water, cm is fish :: Read the units first.
2. Warmer tank = 24°C = Tank B :: Find the row.
3. (C) swaps the columns :: 24 is a temperature, not a length.
4. Answer: (B) :: The right row, the right units.
```

```theory
A **table** is a grid of data. The first column usually names the rows (the things compared), and the top row names the columns (what was measured). A **title** or caption tells you the topic, and the column headings tell you the units. In Reading and Writing the units are often tucked into the heading in brackets, for example "(in thousands)" or "(micromoles per liter)". The unit changes what the number means: 150 in a column "in thousands" is 150,000, and a "years before present" column counts backward, so the biggest number is the oldest.

A **graph** adds an axis for each variable and, when there is more than one line or bar, a **legend**. Always find the axis labels and what each color stands for before you look at heights. The question text often names the exact values you need ("in July 2021", "at 75 to 125 meters below the surface"). Treat those as an address, and find the matching cell.

The most common wrong choices read the data in a way that is tidy but wrong: the neighboring cell, the neighboring row, a column that belongs to a different measurement, or a number that is real but belongs to a different group. When a table has two groups and two conditions, such as treated and untreated plots at two depths, say the address in words before you check a number: "untreated plot, shallow soil".

One more habit: the claim sentence before the blank sometimes tells you which part of the table to use ("the older a bridge is, the more repairs it needs..."). Work out what the claim needs, then read the table for that.
```

# Finish the statement
Cross out every choice the data contradict.

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--cross-out
```

## Key ideas
The most common question is "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?" Work in two passes.

1. **What does the sentence need?** Read the words before the blank. Does it need a **contrast** (*differs by location*), a **trend** (*most rose*), an **exception** (*the trend was not universal*), or just a **value**?
2. **Cross out the misreads.** Check each choice against the table. Delete any choice with a wrong number, a wrong row, a wrong direction (up for down), or swapped years.

If more than one choice is left, all of them are *true*. Pick the one that **does the job the sentence needs**. A true choice that does not contrast, or that is not an exception, is wrong.

| Museum | 2019 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Hartley | 120 | 150 |
| Brook | 90 | 110 |
| Quill | 80 | 65 |

```example
Visitors (thousands) at three museums. Most museums gained visitors, but not all: for example, ______
Choices: Hartley had 150 thousand visitors in 2023. / Quill had 80 thousand visitors in 2019 and 65 thousand in 2023. / Brook had 110 thousand visitors in 2019 and 90 thousand in 2023.
1. Needs: a case where visitors fell :: "Not all" asks for an exception.
2. (C) swaps the years :: Brook rose, 90 to 110. Cross it out.
3. (A) is true but rises :: It is not an exception.
4. Answer: (B) :: Quill fell.
```

```theory
Most of these questions have two layers. The first layer is **accuracy**: does the choice report the table correctly? Many wrong choices fail here. They flip the years, give a value from the wrong row, or say "higher" when the table says lower. Cross these out before you think about anything else. That is the fastest elimination on the test.

The second layer is **fit**: does the accurate choice do what the sentence needs? Sometimes every remaining choice is true. A claim like "prices differ from town to town" needs a choice that **contrasts two towns** (40 dollars versus 70 dollars). A choice that says two towns had the *same* price is true but shows similarity, so it does not fit. A sentence that says "that trend wasn't universal" needs a case that went the other way.

Sentences also use small signals. *For example* introduces an instance of the idea just stated. *Yet* or *however* signals a surprise, which means that the data will show something that cuts against the sentence before. *Both* asks for something that is true of both groups. Pay attention to these words, because they tell you which kind of choice to look for.

Do not calculate. The choices give you the numbers. If a choice makes you do heavy arithmetic, it is probably not the answer, or the table already includes the number you need (for example "percent increase" or "inhabitants per guild").

The four typical question phrasings are *complete the statement*, *complete the example*, *complete the comparison* and *complete the claim*. They all ask the same thing: which accurate choice does the job?
```

# Two parts, two groups
Prove every part of the claim.

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--two-parts
```

## Key ideas
Many claims have **two parts**: *longer and lighter*, *higher in one group but lower in the other*, *both groups*. The right choice must prove **every** part, and it must describe **both groups**.

1. **Split the claim.** Break it at *and*, *but*, *while* or a comma. Write what each part needs.
2. **Check each part** against the table, in the right direction.
3. **Check both groups.** A choice that gives numbers for only one group compares nothing.

On "support the hypothesis" questions, write a prediction for each group first. *If squirrels hibernate alone, their torpor bouts should be longer and their arousals shorter than the marmots'.* That is two parts.

| Bike | Range (km) | Weight (kg) |
|---|---|---|
| Aero | 60 | 11 |
| Trek | 45 | 14 |

```example
A reviewer says the Aero goes farther and weighs less than the Trek: ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?
Choices: the Aero ran 60 km. / the Aero ran 60 km and weighs 11 kg. / the Aero ran 60 km and weighs 11 kg; the Trek ran 45 km and weighs 14 kg.
1. Claim: farther + lighter, than the Trek :: Two parts, two bikes.
2. (A) gives range only :: Half the claim.
3. (B) leaves the Trek out :: Nothing to compare.
4. Answer: (C) :: Both parts, both bikes.
```

```theory
A claim with *and* is like a lock with two keys. If a choice proves only one part, it is "half-right", and half-right is wrong. The same is true of comparisons: a claim that Group A is higher than Group B needs data for both groups. A true sentence such as "the Aero ran 60 km" supports nothing, because 60 km is neither long nor short without something to compare it with.

Direction matters. "Longer" means a bigger number in the *range* column, but "lighter" means a smaller number in the *weight* column. If you mix them up, you will pick a choice that has the right words and the wrong direction. Say the direction aloud: "range up, weight down".

Questions about a **hypothesis** or **conclusion** often hide the two parts inside a single sentence. "Cats that live alone would likely sleep longer and wake less often than cats that live in groups." Two parts: longer sleep, fewer wakings. The data that support it show the solo cats with both. A choice that gives only the groups' waking times compares nothing.

When a measurement sits *between* two table rows, do not calculate. Decide which side of a boundary it lands on. If a range runs from 10 to 20 and both values fall between those numbers, both items belong in that range. Your job is only to place each value correctly.

Finally, a wrong choice can restate what the passage already told you. If the text says one site is older, a choice that repeats this adds nothing. The best choice adds a new fact that the data confirm.
```

# Count or percent
Use the column that matches the claim.

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--count-share
```

## Key ideas
A table can show **counts** (how many) and **percentages** (what share). Claims use one or the other, and the two do not always agree.

- A claim about a **share, rate or "more likely"** needs the percent column. Bigger groups have bigger counts without having bigger shares.
- A claim about **how many** needs counts.
- **"Percent increase" is not the same as "the larger increase".** A small plant can grow 55% and still cover less ground than a large plant that grew 28%.

You do not have to divide. Either the table gives the percentage, or the correct choice states both numbers for you. Look for the column or the choice that speaks the claim's language.

| Club | Members | Seniors |
|---|---|---|
| Chess | 40 | 25% |
| Drama | 25 | 40% |

```example
A student claims that a larger share of Drama's members than Chess's members are seniors. Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the claim?
Choices: Chess has 40 members and Drama has 25. / Seniors are 40% of Drama and 25% of Chess. / Chess has 40 members, and 25% of them are seniors.
1. Claim: a larger share :: Share means a percent.
2. Use the % column :: No dividing needed.
3. (A) is about size, (C) names one club :: Neither compares shares.
4. Answer: (B) :: Both clubs, both shares.
```

```theory
A **count** is a number of things: 36 students, 12 loans. A **percentage** (or **share**, **proportion**, **rate**) is a part of a whole: 36 out of 200 is 18 percent. Two groups can have very different counts and the same share, or a larger count and a smaller share. A bigger school has more band students than a small school even when a smaller share of its students join.

On the SAT you do not have to compute shares. The test gives you the percentage or states the count and the total in the choice. Your job is to find the one that matches what the claim talks about. If the claim says "larger share", a choice that compares counts only is true but does not address the claim.

A second pair to watch: **change** and **percent change**. A pond that grew from 2 to 3 square meters (a 50 percent increase) grew by a bigger percentage than a pond that grew from 500 to 600 square meters (20 percent), even though the second pond gained more area. Read what the claim compares and use the column that matches.

Tables also include **overall** figures. A column labeled "Rail (60%)" gives the percentage of all trips that used rail. A claim that something "represents the majority" can be checked against the overall figure, while a claim that "every city" reported it needs the lowest city figure to be above zero.

Finally, notes such as "rows may not add up to 100 because of rounding" are only reminders that percentages were rounded. They do not change the answer.
```

# Read the graph
Check the shape: peak, gap, crossing, trend.

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--line-trend
```

## Key ideas
A graph shows data as lines or bars. Read the **labels** first (title, axes, units, legend), then the **shape**.

- **Peak and low**: the highest or lowest point of a line, or the tallest bar.
- **Gap**: how far apart two lines are, and whether it grows or shrinks.
- **Crossing**: where one line passes the other. "Overtook" means below first, above later.
- **Trend**: the overall direction. For a trend, compare the **first** and **last** points. A few ups and downs in between do not change the overall trend.
- **"No exceptions"** claims fail if one point goes the other way.

The choices give rough values ("about 500"), so you only need to see which is higher, not exact numbers. Check **every** line or time period the choice names.

```example
A graph shows monthly visitors (hundreds) at two parks. Park N: January 8, February 12, March 15. Park S: January 14, February 12, March 9. A guide says Park N overtook Park S: ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the statement?
Choices: Park N had 15 hundred visitors in March. / Park S led in January, but Park N led in March.
1. "Overtook" = below first, above later :: Look for a crossing.
2. The lines meet in February :: Read both parks at both ends.
3. (A) names only one park :: True, but it shows no switch.
4. Answer: (B) :: S above N, then N above S.
```

```theory
Graphs are the same kind of evidence as tables, drawn as shapes. A **line graph** shows how something changes over time. A **bar graph** compares amounts across groups. The **legend** or labels say which line or bar belongs to which group, and the axis title says the unit.

Questions on graphs usually ask you to complete a statement with a pattern. The patterns are: *highest in all three months* (a line stays above the others at every point), *the gap widened* (two lines move apart), *they crossed* (the order swaps), *fell overall* (the end is below the start), and *recovered* (it fell, then rose, but not necessarily to a record). Check the exact words: "highest value" is not the same thing as "greatest increase", and a recovery is not always a new high.

The graph can also use a **scale you must read**: a measure that tracks something else in reverse. If the axis shows "water lost", a taller bar means *less* water left. Read the passage for what the axis stands for.

When choices name two things (two regions, two years), check both. A choice may be right for one line and wrong for the other. Likewise, check the **time window**: "from 1973 to 2013" is different from "from 1993 to 2023". Wrong choices often change one date.

Do not worry about exact numbers. The test writers choose values that are far enough apart to read by eye. If two choices seem close, one of them has misread a line.
```

# Support or weaken with data
Predict what the data should show, then test it.


## Key ideas
Some questions give a hypothesis or conclusion and ask which data **support** or **weaken** it. Treat them like the textual ones, with numbers.

1. **Find the claim.** What does it say will happen, and to which group?
2. **Predict the comparison.** If the claim is true, which group should be higher, and which should look the same?
3. **Test the choices.** The right one is true in the data **and** tests the claim.

To **support** a claim, pick the data in which the group with the factor differs from the control, as predicted, every time the data are shown. To **weaken**, pick the data that show **another cause**: the control group changed too, or a part of the claim is missing.

Watch for **strong words**: *entirely*, *only*, *always*. One accurate counterexample is enough to weaken them.

| Group | Mean score in Sept | Mean score in May |
|---|---|---|
| Used the app | 68 | 76 |
| No app | 69 | 77 |

```example
A teacher claims that a homework app raised her students' scores. Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to weaken the claim?
Choices: The app group's mean rose from 68 to 76. / The no-app group's mean also rose, from 69 to 77.
1. Claim: app → higher scores :: The app group should gain more.
2. (A) is true but supports :: Wrong job.
3. Answer: (B) :: The same gain without the app: another cause.
```

```theory
A **control group** is a group that does everything the same way except for the factor under test. If the factor matters, the test group should differ from the control group. If the control group changes in the same way, then the factor did not cause the change. That is why many correct answers describe the control group: "the no-app group also rose", or "the untreated tank also warmed".

To **support** a claim, look for the pattern the claim predicts across *all* the conditions in the data. A conclusion that a lamp speeds plant growth is supported when plants under the lamp are taller in *every* week, not only one week. A choice that describes only the fertilizer group's growth over time does not compare it with anything.

To **weaken**, look for the same three moves as in the textual lesson: the opposite result, no difference, or an alternative explanation. A conclusion that something is "entirely due to" one cause is weakened by any change in the group without that cause.

"True but off-target" is the trap of this question type. The correct choice is not just accurate. It is accurate about the right comparison. Statements about a single group ("the app group's mean rose") or about the wrong comparison (two groups that differ only in something the claim does not mention) are traps.

On harder questions the student's argument is more modest, for example "the sugar does not entirely explain the birds' choice". Then the data you need show the effect still varies when the sugar is held the same: two feeders with the same sugar but different results. Read the exact claim and ask which comparison would test it.
```

## Easy
```task
bank: rw-0399
highlight:
- "harvested their fruit over three years" :: Check the result in every year.
- "half the plants were grown using a nitrogen fertilizer, and the other half were grown using a control fertilizer that contained no nitrogen" :: Two groups: nitrogen and control.
- "concluded that the nitrogen fertilizer increases cantaloupe yield" :: The claim: the nitrogen group yields more than the control.
explain:
1. **Claim:** nitrogen fertilizer increases cantaloupe yield.
2. **Predict:** in the graph, the nitrogen group should be higher than the control group, and this should hold every year.
3. Choice B says exactly that: in every year of the experiment, the nitrogen plants out-yielded the control plants. The bars confirm it in 2017, 2018 and 2019.
4. Choice A gives a minimum yield for the nitrogen plants but makes no comparison, and it is false for 2019. Choice D tracks only one group over time. Choice C compares different years across groups.

The answer is B.
```

## Medium
```task
bank: rw-0160
highlight:
- "placed spiders in two enclosures, one with lizards and one without" :: Two groups: lizards and no lizards.
- "entirely attributable to the presence of the lizards" :: A strong claim: only lizards caused the drop.
- "weaken the student’s conclusion" :: Find data showing another cause.
explain:
1. **Claim:** the drop in spiders in the lizard enclosure was *entirely* because of the lizards.
2. **Predict:** if lizards were the only cause, spiders in the enclosure without lizards should stay about the same.
3. **Weaken it:** show that the no-lizard enclosure also lost spiders. Choice B says it declined substantially by day 30 (from about 85 to about 43 in the graph). So something besides lizards also reduced the population.
4. Choice D is true (day 30 was lower with lizards), but it supports the role of the lizards, the wrong job. Choice A and choice C are accurate details that do not test whether lizards were the only cause.

The answer is B.
```

## Hard
```task
bank: rw-0269
highlight:
- "tadpoles disproportionately consumed eggs of their own species" :: The result: cane toad eggs were eaten the most.
- "bufadienolide, a chemical produced by the eggs of cane toads but not by the eggs of native amphibians" :: The usual explanation: the chemical attracts tadpoles.
- "doesn’t entirely explain the cane toad tadpoles’ preference" :: The student's claim: the chemical is not the whole story.
explain:
1. **Usual explanation:** tadpoles prefer cane toad eggs because they produce bufadienolide.
2. **Student's claim:** that does not entirely explain the preference.
3. **Predict:** among eggs that all **lack** bufadienolide, the tadpoles should still eat some species more than others. Then something besides the chemical matters.
4. Choice A compares the striped burrowing frog (10% eaten) with the dainty green tree frog (1%). Neither produces bufadienolide, yet the percentages differ. That supports the student.
5. Choice C compares the cane toad's own eggs with another species. That fits the chemical explanation. Choices B and D do not test the chemical: B is about eggs left alone, and D shows two species with the same percentage.

The answer is A.
```
