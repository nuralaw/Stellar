## What this question looks like

These are the "student notes" questions. *Synthesis* means combining several pieces of information into one sentence. *Rhetorical* means the sentence has a purpose. So your job is to find the sentence that combines the notes for one specific purpose.

On screen you see:

- On the left: "While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:" and then 4 to 6 bullet points. Each bullet is one fact.
- On the right: a goal and the question. The goal always starts with "The student wants to…", for example "The student wants to emphasize a similarity between the two species" or "The student wants to introduce the device to an audience unfamiliar with it."
- The question is always the same: "Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?"
- Four answer choices. Each one is a full sentence, often a long one.

**Where and how many.** Notes questions are the last questions in every Reading and Writing module, right after the transition questions. Together, transitions and notes questions form the Expression of Ideas domain: about 20% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, or 8 to 12 per test. So expect a few notes questions at the end of each module.

**The most important fact.** All four choices are grammatically correct, and usually all four use facts from the notes. College Board says the right sentence must include only relevant information from the notes and must also achieve the goal. So the question is never "Which sentence is true?" It is "Which sentence does the job the student asked for?"

The goals come in a few families (these are the sub-types):

- **Compare:** emphasize a similarity, or a difference (a contrast), between two things.
- **Introduce for an audience:** present something to readers who don't know it (an *unfamiliar* audience) or who already know it (a *familiar* audience).
- **Present a study:** its aim, its method (also called *methodology*: what the researchers actually did), its findings, or a mix of these.
- **Generalize or give an example:** make a broad statement about a group, or show one specific case.
- **Emphasize or specify one detail:** a date, a place, a size, a cause or a result.

## The method

1. **Skip the notes at first.** Read the goal sentence ("The student wants to…"). Find the task word (emphasize, contrast, introduce, present, explain, generalize) and the target (what the sentence must be about).
2. **Turn the goal into a checklist.** Write 2 or 3 short words on your scratch paper, for example "contrast + both methods + precision" or "unfamiliar + what it is + one ability". Every part of the goal is a requirement.
3. **Test each choice against the checklist only.** Ask: "Does this sentence do the whole job?" Cross out choices that are about the right topic but do a different job, or only half of the job. The option eliminator helps here.
4. **Check the survivor against the notes.** This takes about 10 seconds. Find each fact in the notes: names, numbers, and which thing has which feature. If anything is changed, swapped, made stronger ("most", "all", "the first") or added, cross the choice out.
5. **If two choices survive,** find the one requirement that one of them misses: the second item in a comparison, the explanation for a new audience, the method instead of the result. Read only the note that settles it.
6. **Never pick a sentence just because it is true or because it uses more notes.** Doing the right job beats including more facts.

> **Tip:** Many tutors say "you don't need the notes", and for most questions that is almost true: the goal alone removes two or three choices. But in 2025 and 2026, tutors and students report more choices that match the goal and still get a fact wrong. Step 4 costs about 10 seconds and protects you from them.

## Rules and formulas you need

### What each goal word demands

| The goal says | The right sentence must… | A typical wrong choice |
|---|---|---|
| emphasize a similarity | name both things and the feature they share, often with *both, like, also, similarly* | is about only one thing, or uses *whereas* or *however* |
| emphasize a difference, or contrast | name both things and show how they differ on the feature in the goal, often with *while, whereas, unlike, but* | lists facts side by side with no contrast, or contrasts a different feature |
| introduce X to an audience unfamiliar with X | name X and say what X is, often in a short phrase after the name | uses the name X with no explanation |
| present X to an audience familiar with X | use the name freely and focus on the new point | spends the sentence explaining basics |
| present the aim of a study | say what the researchers wanted to find out (*to learn whether…*) | gives the result instead |
| present the method of a study | say what the researchers did: what they measured, compared, built or observed | gives the finding or the background |
| present the findings or results | say what the researchers found | gives the method instead |
| explain how or why | give the cause or the steps, linked together (*because, by, which allowed*) | gives only the outcome |
| make a generalization | make a broad statement about a group, usually with no single name or number | gives one specific example |
| give an example, or illustrate | give one concrete case of the idea | gives the general statement |
| emphasize or specify a detail | state that exact detail clearly | mentions it only in passing, or not at all |

### Audience clues

- "an audience unfamiliar with…" or "readers new to…": the sentence must explain what the thing is. The usual way is a short phrase after the name, between commas: "Tardigrades, tiny animals often called water bears, …"
- "an audience already familiar with…": no explanation is needed. The right answer often uses the name directly and gets to the point.
- No audience mentioned: skip this check.

### Signal words inside the choices

Some choices show their job with one small word. Learn to spot these:

| The job | Signal words |
|---|---|
| similarity | both, like, also, similarly, likewise, as well as, share |
| difference | while, whereas, unlike, but, however, in contrast, although |
| cause or result | because, since, so, as a result, led to, allowed |
| example | for example, for instance, such as, including |
| purpose or aim | to + verb (to learn, to test, to measure), in order to |

A wrong signal word is enough to cross a choice out. A similarity goal answered with *whereas* is wrong, even if every fact in it is correct.

### The accuracy check

Wrong but tempting choices change the notes in one of these ways:

- They swap facts between two things (A's feature is given to B).
- They change a number, a date or a place.
- They make a claim stronger than the notes: *some* becomes *most* or *all*, *can* becomes *always*, or they add *the first*, *the only*, *more than any other*.
- They add a cause or a reason that the notes never give.
- They add outside information, even information that is true in real life.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A similarity

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Axolotls are salamanders that live in canals and lakes in Mexico City.
- If an axolotl loses a leg, it can grow a new one, complete with bone and muscle.
- Zebrafish are small striped fish from rivers in South Asia.
- If a zebrafish's fin is damaged, the fish can grow it back.
- Scientists study both animals to learn how damaged body parts might one day be repaired in humans.

The student wants to emphasize a similarity between axolotls and zebrafish. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

- (A) Axolotls live in canals and lakes in Mexico City, whereas zebrafish come from rivers in South Asia.
- (B) Like axolotls, which can regrow a lost leg, zebrafish can regrow a damaged body part, such as a fin.
- (C) Axolotls, which are salamanders, live in canals and lakes in Mexico City.
- (D) Zebrafish are small striped fish that can grow back a damaged fin.

**Solution.** Checklist from the goal: both animals + a shared feature + a similarity signal. (B) names both animals, uses "Like", and gives the ability they share: regrowing a body part. (A) names both animals, but "whereas" sets up a difference (where they live), which is the opposite of the goal. (C) and (D) are each about one animal only, so they cannot show a similarity. All four choices are true; only (B) does the job.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: Introducing something to a new audience

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Tardigrades are tiny animals, usually less than one millimeter long.
- They have eight legs and are often called "water bears."
- They live in water and in damp places such as moss.
- When their surroundings dry out, tardigrades can lose almost all the water in their bodies and survive in this dried-out state for years.
- In 2007, some tardigrades survived ten days exposed to outer space.

The student wants to introduce tardigrades and one of their survival abilities to an audience unfamiliar with tardigrades. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

- (A) Tardigrades can lose almost all the water in their bodies and survive in this dried-out state for years.
- (B) Tardigrades, tiny eight-legged animals often called "water bears," live in water and in damp places such as moss.
- (C) Tardigrades, tiny animals often called "water bears," can survive for years after losing almost all the water in their bodies.
- (D) In 2007, tardigrades survived ten days exposed to outer space, a result that surprised many scientists.

**Solution.** The goal has two parts: say what tardigrades are (because the audience is unfamiliar) and name a survival ability. (C) does both: "tiny animals often called 'water bears'" tells a new reader what a tardigrade is, and the rest of the sentence gives the drying-out ability. (A) gives the ability but never says what a tardigrade is; a new reader can't tell if it's an animal, a plant or a germ. (B) explains what tardigrades are but gives no survival ability. (D) adds "a result that surprised many scientists", which is not in the notes, and it also never says what a tardigrade is.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 3: A study and its method

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Gregor Mendel was a scientist and monk who worked in what is now the Czech Republic.
- He wanted to understand how traits are passed from parent plants to their offspring.
- Over about eight years, he bred thousands of pea plants and recorded traits such as flower color and seed shape in each new generation.
- He found that traits appeared in the offspring in predictable patterns.
- His results were published in 1866 but got little attention until about 1900.

The student wants to present Mendel's study and its methodology. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

- (A) Mendel, a scientist and monk who worked in what is now the Czech Republic, published his results in 1866.
- (B) Mendel found that traits are passed from parent plants to offspring in predictable patterns.
- (C) To learn how traits are passed from parents to offspring, Mendel bred thousands of pea plants and recorded traits such as flower color in each new generation.
- (D) Mendel's results, published in 1866, got little attention until about 1900.

**Solution.** "Methodology" means the method: what the researcher actually did. "The study" means what the research was about. So the sentence needs the topic of the study plus the method. (C) gives both: the aim ("To learn how traits are passed…") and the method ("bred thousands of pea plants and recorded traits…"). (B) is the most tempting wrong answer: it is about the study, but it gives the finding, not the method. (A) and (D) are about Mendel's life and the history of his paper, not about how the study worked.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 4: A generalization

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Tool use means using an object to reach a goal, such as getting food.
- For a long time, tool use was thought to be unique to humans.
- Chimpanzees push sticks into termite mounds and pull out termites to eat.
- Sea otters use rocks to crack open shellfish.
- Some crows shape twigs into hooks to pull insects out of tree bark.
- Scientists have observed tool use in only a small fraction of animal species.

The student wants to make a generalization about tool use in animals. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

- (A) Chimpanzees push sticks into termite mounds and then pull out termites to eat.
- (B) Most animals use tools, from chimpanzees and sea otters to crows.
- (C) Sea otters crack open shellfish with rocks, while some crows shape twigs into hooks.
- (D) Tool use is not unique to humans: several other kinds of animals also use objects to get food.

**Solution.** A generalization is a broad statement about a whole group, not about one case. (A) is one specific example. (C) describes two specific animals side by side. That leaves (B) and (D), which both make broad claims, so check them against the notes. (B) says "most animals use tools", but the last note says tool use has been seen in only a small fraction of species, so (B) is inaccurate. (D) is broad and supported: the notes name three kinds of animals other than humans that use objects to get food, and the second note says tool use was once thought to belong to humans alone.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 5: A contrast for an expert audience

While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:

- Archaeologists can use more than one method to find the age of a wooden object.
- Radiocarbon dating measures how much carbon-14 is left in material that was once alive.
- It works on wood, bone and cloth up to about 50,000 years old.
- Its results usually come as a range of years, such as 1250 to 1290.
- Tree-ring dating compares the pattern of growth rings in a piece of wood with a long record of ring patterns from the same region.
- When the patterns match, it can show the exact year in which each ring formed.

The student wants to emphasize a difference in how precise the two methods are, for an audience already familiar with both methods. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

- (A) While tree-ring dating usually gives a range of years, radiocarbon dating can show the exact year in which a ring formed.
- (B) Radiocarbon dating measures the carbon-14 left in once-living material, whereas tree-ring dating compares ring patterns with a regional record.
- (C) Radiocarbon dating works on material up to about 50,000 years old, while tree-ring dating can show the exact year in which a ring formed.
- (D) Radiocarbon dating usually gives a range of years, while tree-ring dating can show the exact year in which a ring formed.

**Solution.** Checklist: both methods + a difference + the feature named in the goal, which is precision (how exact the date is). Every choice names both methods and uses a contrast word, so signal words don't help here; you must check the feature and the facts. (B) contrasts how the methods work, which is the wrong feature, and it explains basics this audience already knows. (C) is the subtle trap: its tree-ring half is about precision, but its radiocarbon half is about age limits, so it never shows that radiocarbon is less exact. (A) has the right shape but swaps the facts: the notes say radiocarbon gives the range and tree rings give the exact year. (D) states both sides of the precision difference correctly.

**Answer:** (D)

## Traps to avoid

- **True but off-goal.** The sentence is accurate and well written but does a different job, such as giving the finding when the goal asks for the method. Every choice is "true"; only one does the job.
- **Half the goal.** Two-part goals ("introduce X and one ability", "the study and its method") always come with choices that do only one part.
- **One side of a comparison.** A similarity or a difference needs both things named in the same sentence.
- **Wrong signal word.** *Whereas* or *however* in a similarity goal, or *both* or *like* in a contrast goal, makes the choice wrong even when the facts are right.
- **Wrong feature.** The choice contrasts the two things, but not on the feature the goal names (how they work instead of how precise they are).
- **No explanation for a new audience.** If the readers are unfamiliar with the topic, a choice that never says what the thing is fails.
- **Swapped or changed facts.** A number, a date, or which item has which feature is quietly changed. Do the 10-second accuracy check.
- **Stronger than the notes.** Words like *most, all, always, the first, the only* or *more than any other* when the notes say less.
- **More is not better.** The choice that packs in the most bullet points is often wrong, because it includes facts the goal didn't ask for.
- **Specific instead of general, or the reverse.** A generalization goal is not met by one example, and an example goal is not met by a general statement.

## Quick checklist

- I read the goal first and named the task word and the target.
- My answer does every part of the goal, not just one part.
- For a comparison, both things are named and the signal word fits (similar or different).
- For an unfamiliar audience, the sentence says what the thing is.
- Every fact matches the notes: nothing swapped, nothing made stronger, nothing added.
- I did not choose it just because it is true or because it is the longest.
