## What this question looks like

You get one short text (25 to 150 words) and one question about what that text says. The text can be about science, history or social studies, or the arts. It can also be literature: part of a novel, a short story, a play or a poem, often written more than 100 years ago. On screen, the text is on the left and the question with its four choices is on the right. There is no blank, no graph and no "finding" or "quotation" to pick. Don't confuse it with "main purpose" questions, which ask *why* the author wrote the text: that is a different skill.

There are two kinds of question, main idea and detail, and detail questions come in a few forms:

| Kind | Typical question | What you need |
|---|---|---|
| Main idea | "Which choice best states the main idea of the text?" | The whole text in one sentence |
| Detail: a stated fact | "According to the text, why did the town build the dam?" | One fact you can point to |
| Detail: what is true about someone or something | "Based on the text, what is true about Dr. Reyes's study?" | A statement that the text fully backs up |
| Detail: a character's or speaker's feelings | "Based on the text, how does the narrator feel about the move?" | The exact feeling, at the right strength |

**How many and where.** The College Board puts 12 to 14 scored Information and Ideas questions on each test, and main idea and detail questions are usually about 3 or 4 of them. In each module they come right after the vocabulary, purpose and two-text questions. They are the first questions of the Information and Ideas group, ordered from easier to harder.

> **Key idea:** The right answer is a fair restatement of the text, in different words. Wrong answers say a little more than the text, a little less, or something different.

## The method

1. **Read the question first.** Decide: does it ask about the whole text (main idea) or one thing (detail)? For a detail question, note exactly what it asks about: which person, which reason, which moment.
2. **Read the line above the text, then the whole text once.** For literature, the line above the text tells you who wrote it, when, and sometimes who the characters are. While you read, watch for pivot words: *but*, *however*, *yet*, *although*, *in fact*, *instead*, *now*. What comes after a pivot usually matters most.
3. **Say the answer in your own words before you look at the choices.** For a main idea, use **topic + point**: who or what is the text about, and what does the author say about it? Keep it short, and check that it fits both the first and the last sentence. For a story, use **who + what happens + what it shows**. For a poem, use **the speaker + what they feel or think about something + any change**. For a detail, put your finger on the sentence that answers the question and say it in simple words.
4. **Test each choice with four checks.** Is it actually in the text, not just true in real life? Is every part supported, down to the last word? Is it the right size (the whole text for a main idea, the exact thing asked about for a detail)? Is it about the right person, thing and time?
5. **Name the problem with each wrong choice:** too narrow, too broad, half right, never said, twisted, opposite, too strong or wrong person. If two choices survive, go back to the one sentence that decides between them.
6. **Choose and move on.** Aim for about one minute. If you are stuck, cross out what you can with the on-screen cross-out tool, pick the best of the rest, flag the question and return later. Never leave a question blank: a wrong answer costs nothing extra.

> **Tip:** Don't read the text four times. Read it once with the question in mind, predict, then go back only to check one specific line.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Main idea: common text shapes

Short texts follow a small number of patterns. Spotting the pattern tells you where the main idea is.

| Shape | What it looks like | The main idea is… |
|---|---|---|
| Old belief → new finding | "Scientists long thought X. But a new study found Y." | Y, often as "a study challenges or adds to X" |
| Claim → examples | "The painter drew on her hometown. For example, …" | The claim, not the example |
| Question → answer | "Why do some lakes turn green in spring? Researchers found…" | The answer |
| Two sides | "The plan saved money. However, it caused…" | Both sides together |
| Problem → attempted solution | "Frost destroyed the crops. One group of farmers tried…" | The attempt and how well it worked |
| Story scene | A character in a situation; something happens or changes | What happens and what it shows about the character |

- **Topic is not main idea.** "Coyotes" is a topic. "A study found that city coyotes eat mostly wild food" is a main idea. A choice that only names the topic, or only describes how a study was done, is rarely right.
- **Keep both halves.** When a text has two linked parts (strange *but* useful, praised *then* forgotten, scared *and* excited), the right answer keeps both parts.
- **The main idea is not always written in one sentence.** In harder texts you build it from several details, like putting puzzle pieces together.

### Detail questions

- **"According to the text"** means the answer is stated. You should be able to point to the line.
- **"Based on the text"** or **"suggests"** means the answer is stated or is one very small step from what is stated. It is never a guess.
- **Who did what.** Swapping the person, the cause or the time is the most common detail trap. Check the subject of every sentence you use.
- **Match the strength.** The answer can't be stronger than the text.

| The text says | The answer may say | The answer may not say |
|---|---|---|
| may, might, suggests | may, possibly, suggests | proves, definitely |
| some, many | some, many | all, every |
| often, usually | often, usually | always, never |
| a little worried, uneasy | concerned, uneasy | terrified, in a panic |

### Stories and poems

- **The speaker is not the poet.** Say "the speaker" for a poem and "the narrator" for a story told by an "I".
- **Read poems in sentences, not lines.** Follow the punctuation. One sentence often runs over several lines, and a line break is not a full stop.
- **Translate as you go.** Turn each sentence into plain modern English. You need the meaning, not every word.
- **Picture language means something plain.** "Her words were ice" means she spoke coldly. Translate the image before you judge a choice.
- **Feelings come from evidence.** Look at what characters do, say and notice. Match both the feeling and its strength. If the text shows mixed feelings, the answer must show both.
- **Watch for a shift.** In a poem or a scene, "but", "yet", "now" or "at last" often marks a change. The main idea usually includes the change.

Older texts use some words you won't see in a modern newspaper. These come up often:

| Older word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| thou, thee / thy, thine | you / your, yours |
| 'tis, 'twas | it is, it was |
| ere | before |
| oft | often |
| naught | nothing |
| yonder | over there |
| whence | from where |
| wherefore | why |
| bade | told or asked (someone to do something) |

## Worked examples

Every text below was written for this lesson. The people, places and studies are made up.

### Example 1: An old belief and a new finding

For many years, botanists assumed that the tiny hairs covering the leaves of wild tomato plants were there mainly to slow water loss on hot days. A study led by plant biologist Ines Okafor points to another role. When an insect walks across a leaf, the tips of the hairs break and release a sticky liquid that glues the insect's feet in place. In Okafor's tests, leaves with many hairs trapped far more insects than leaves with few hairs did, which suggests that the hairs help defend the plant against insects that feed on it.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

- (A) Okafor's team compared leaves that had many hairs with leaves that had few hairs.
- (B) A study suggests that the hairs on wild tomato leaves also help protect the plant from insects.
- (C) The hairs on the leaves of most plants are used to trap insects.
- (D) The hairs on wild tomato leaves do not help the plant hold on to water.

**Solution.** The question asks for the main idea, so we need the whole text in one sentence. The shape is "old belief → new finding": botanists assumed the hairs were for water, but Okafor's study "points to another role." Prediction: *topic*, hairs on wild tomato leaves; *point*, a study suggests they also defend the plant against insects.

- (A) is true, but it only describes how the study was done. Too narrow.
- (B) matches the prediction and keeps the careful word "suggests". Every part is supported.
- (C) says "most plants". The text is only about wild tomato plants. Too broad.
- (D) twists the text. The study adds a second role; it never says the hairs fail to slow water loss.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: The reason behind a choice

In the late 1800s, some women's clubs in the United States ran "traveling libraries": wooden boxes of about fifty books that were shipped by train to small towns. Historian Grace Thornton explains why the clubs preferred this system to raising money for new library buildings. The towns were many and tiny, most with only a few hundred residents, so building a library in each one would have cost far more than the clubs could raise. A box of books, by contrast, cost little and could be sent on to a new town every few months, so one collection served many places. Some towns later used their boxes as the start of a permanent library.

According to the text, why did the clubs prefer traveling libraries to new library buildings?

- (A) Railroad companies carried the boxes of books at no cost to the clubs.
- (B) The towns that received boxes went on to build permanent libraries.
- (C) Sharing one collection among many small towns cost much less than a building in each town.
- (D) People in small towns preferred to read books at home rather than in a library.

**Solution.** "According to the text" means the answer is stated. The question asks *why the clubs preferred* the boxes. Thornton's reason is in the third and fourth sentences: buildings for tiny towns cost too much, while one box could move from town to town. Prediction: *it was much cheaper to share one collection among many towns.*

- (A) Free shipping is never mentioned. Never said.
- (B) This is in the text, but it happened *later*, so it can't be the clubs' reason. It also says "the towns", while the text says only "some towns". Real detail, wrong job.
- (C) restates sentences three and four. Supported.
- (D) The text says nothing about where people liked to read. Never said.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 3: A character's feelings in an older story

The following text is from Ada Pryor's 1891 short story "The Long Field." Hester's younger brother, Will, has just told her that he plans to leave the family farm to work in the city.

Hester set down the pail of milk with great care, as though it were the thing most likely to break. "The city," she said. "Well. You were always the clever one, Will, and clever ones ought to go where cleverness is wanted." She smiled at him, and he believed the smile. Only after he had gone whistling down to the barn did she sit on the step and look for a long while at the empty road, turning her apron over and over in her hands.

Based on the text, which choice best describes Hester's response to Will's news?

- (A) She is openly angry that Will plans to leave the farm.
- (B) She is delighted, because she has long wanted Will to leave home.
- (C) She seems to support Will's plan but hides her sadness from him.
- (D) She doubts that Will is clever enough to succeed in the city.

**Solution.** This is a feelings question, so collect the evidence. In front of Will, Hester praises him and smiles. But the text says "he believed the smile", which hints that the smile was not the whole truth. When she is alone, she stares at the empty road and keeps turning her apron in her hands, which shows sadness or worry. The careful way she sets down the pail also shows she was shaken. Prediction: *she acts supportive, but she is sad, and she keeps it to herself.*

- (A) She smiles and praises him. Nothing is "openly angry". Opposite.
- (B) "Delighted" could describe at most the first half of the scene, and it ignores what she does when alone. "Long wanted" is never said. Half right, and too strong.
- (C) covers both halves: support in front of Will, sadness once he has gone.
- (D) She calls him "the clever one". Opposite.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 4: The main idea of a poem

The following text is from Edwin Royle's 1909 poem "The Winter Oak."

The oak stood bare beside the frozen lane,
Its branches raised as if in silent prayer;
I thought the winter wind had won at last,
And nothing green would ever waken there.
But kneeling where the lowest branches bend,
I found small buds wrapped tight against the cold,
Each one a promise kept in patient dark,
Each one a spring the branches mean to hold.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

- (A) The speaker describes an oak tree that has been killed by a harsh winter.
- (B) The speaker concludes that every hardship in life will eventually come to an end.
- (C) The speaker kneels beside an oak tree in order to protect its buds from the cold.
- (D) The speaker first believes that a bare tree is dead but then finds signs that it will grow again.

**Solution.** Read the poem as two sentences, not eight lines. Sentence 1 (lines 1–4): the oak is bare, and the speaker *thought* winter had won and nothing green would grow there again. Then comes the pivot "But". Sentence 2 (lines 5–8): looking closely, the speaker finds buds; each is "a promise" and "a spring", which means new leaves will come. Prediction: *the speaker thinks the tree is dead, then sees buds that promise spring.*

- (A) is only the speaker's first thought, and the buds in the second half show that it was wrong. Half right at best.
- (B) is a life lesson the poem never states. It could be the main idea of many poems, so it's too broad.
- (C) The buds are "wrapped tight" by nature; the speaker kneels to look, not to protect them. Twisted.
- (D) keeps both parts: the first belief and the discovery after "But".

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 5: A main idea built from details

When the Eastbrook Public Library opened in 1931, local newspapers mocked its bare brick walls and narrow windows, calling it "a warehouse for books." The architect, Signe Halvorsen, never answered the critics in public. Her notebooks, however, explain each choice. The thick walls kept the reading rooms cool in summer without electric fans, which the town could not afford to run. The narrow windows kept sunlight off a valuable collection of old maps. And the money saved on decoration paid for a children's reading room, a space the town council had refused to fund.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

- (A) Criticism in local newspapers led Halvorsen to change the design of the library.
- (B) Halvorsen chose a plain design mainly because the town council would not pay for decoration.
- (C) According to Halvorsen's notebooks, the features that critics mocked were practical choices made to serve the town.
- (D) The library's narrow windows protected a valuable collection of old maps from sunlight.

**Solution.** No single sentence states the main idea here, so build it. The text opens with criticism, then pivots on "however": the notebooks explain each choice. Three details follow, and each one shows a plain feature doing a useful job for the town. Prediction: *the mocked, plain features were sensible choices that served the town.*

- (A) Halvorsen "never answered the critics", and nothing says she changed the design. Never said.
- (B) The council refused to pay for the *children's room*, not for decoration. Halvorsen saved on decoration in order to pay for that room. The choice uses words from the text but scrambles the cause. Twisted.
- (C) covers the criticism, the notebooks and all three details.
- (D) is true, but it is one of three examples. Too narrow.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **Too narrow:** a true detail (one example, one number, how a study was done) dressed up as the main idea. Ask: does this cover the whole text, or one sentence?
- **Too broad:** the choice turns one case into a rule about everything ("most plants", "every hardship"). Ask: could this be the main idea of a totally different text?
- **Half right:** the first part matches and the rest is unsupported, or the choice keeps only one half of a two-part text, such as the first feeling in a scene that changes.
- **Never said:** it sounds sensible and may even be true in real life, but no line in the text supports it. Outside knowledge never counts.
- **Twisted:** the choice reuses the text's words but changes who did what, what caused what, or when it happened.
- **Real detail, wrong job:** something the text does say, used to answer a different question, such as a later result offered as the original reason.
- **Opposite or old view:** in "people thought X, but actually Y" texts, a choice that states X is the view the text argues against.
- **Too strong:** "may" becomes "proves", "some" becomes "all", "uneasy" becomes "terrified".
- **Wrong person:** the choice describes another character, or the poet instead of the speaker.

## Quick checklist

- Main idea or detail? What exactly does the question ask about?
- Did I read the line above the text and notice any pivot or shift?
- Did I say the answer in my own words before reading the choices?
- Main idea: does my choice fit the first and the last sentence? Detail: can I point to the line?
- Is every word of my choice supported, at the same strength as the text?
- Is it about the right person, thing and time?
