## Spot the two-text question

This is the only Reading and Writing question with **two short texts** on one topic. Usually Text 1 gives a claim or finding, and Text 2 brings in someone who sees it differently.

The stem tells you **who answers whom**:

- "How would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to… Text 1?"
- "How would [someone in Text 1] most likely respond to… Text 2?"
- "Both authors would most likely agree with which statement?"

First move: jot down the direction, like **2 → 1**.

```quiz
"Based on the texts, how would Kim (Text 2) most likely respond to the theory described in Text 1?" What do you jot down first?
* 2 → 1: Kim answers Text 1's theory
- 1 → 2: Text 1 answers Kim :: Backward. Kim is the one responding, and Kim is in Text 2.
- Agree: find what both accept :: That's a different stem ("both authors would agree"). This one asks for a response.
> The name before "respond" is the speaker. Kim is in Text 2, so the right answer gives Kim's view of Text 1's theory, never the reverse.
```

## Shrink each text to one line

Before you look at the choices, write each text's **claim** in a few words. Not the topic ("Text 1 is about bees") but what the author says about it ("bees pick flowers by color").

Skip background. Text 1's claim often lands in its last sentence, or in the underlined part if there is one. For Text 2, note what it says about **that same claim**.

```quiz
Text 1: Many gardeners plant marigolds next to tomatoes. Some horticulturists say the marigolds' strong scent keeps pests away from the tomatoes.
Which is the best one-line summary of Text 1's claim?
- Text 1 is about marigolds and tomatoes. :: That's the topic. A topic can't tell you what Text 2 agrees or disagrees with.
- Many gardeners plant marigolds. :: Background. The claim is what the horticulturists say the marigolds do.
* Marigold scent keeps pests off tomatoes. :: Right. That's the idea Text 2 will react to.
> The claim lands in the last sentence: the scent protects the tomatoes. Keep that line. Text 2's job is to say something about it.
```

## Find where the texts meet

Text 2 rarely attacks everything. Find the **one point** it touches:

| Text 2 targets | It says |
|---|---|
| the claim | "That's wrong." |
| the evidence | "Your data can't show that." |
| the scope | "True here, not everywhere." |
| the cause | "Real pattern, wrong reason." |

> **Tip:** Text 2 may never mention Text 1. A new study that points somewhere else still counts as a response.

```quiz
Text 1: Researchers found that 200 people who drink green tea daily had fewer colds than average. They argue that green tea strengthens the immune system.
Text 2: Physician Omar Saleh notes that all 200 people were volunteers from a fitness club, whose members exercise far more than most people do.
Which part of Text 1 does Saleh target?
- The finding that the group had fewer colds :: He doesn't question that. His point is about who was in the group.
* The evidence: the people studied may not be typical
- The scope: tea helps only people who exercise :: Saleh never says tea helps anyone. He doubts this group can show what tea does at all.
> Saleh says nothing about tea itself. He talks about who was studied, so the point of contact is the evidence: very fit people may get fewer colds whatever they drink.
```

## Ask what Text 2 keeps

To name the relationship, ask one question: **what does Text 2 keep from Text 1?**

| Relationship | Text 2 keeps |
|---|---|
| Disagree | nothing |
| Yes, but | the claim, with a limit |
| Other cause | the facts, not the reason |
| Support | all of it, plus evidence |

Disagree and "yes, but" tend to come up most, and "yes, but" is the easiest to miss.

```quiz
Text 1: City parks lower summer temperatures in the neighborhoods around them.
Text 2: Engineer Kofi Mensah measured the air near 30 parks. Large parks cooled nearby streets by about 2°C, but parks smaller than one city block had no measurable effect.
What does Text 2 do to Text 1's claim?
- Rejects it :: Too strong. Large parks did cool their streets, so Mensah keeps part of the claim.
* Accepts it with a limit
- Offers another cause for the cooling :: Mensah gives no new cause. He limits which parks have the effect.
- Supports it fully :: Small parks had no effect, so Text 1's claim about parks in general goes too far for him.
> What does Mensah keep? "Parks cool streets", but only for large parks. Claim kept, limit added: that's "yes, but".
```

## Predict with a reason

Now say the response **in the responder's voice, with a reason**: "That's wrong, because…" or "True, but only when…". A bare "she disagrees" isn't enough, since several choices will disagree.

Then pick the choice that matches both the relationship **and** the reason.

```quiz
Text 1: Some biologists argue that zebras' stripes confuse lions, making it hard for a lion to single out one zebra in a running herd.
Text 2: Biologist Nia Brooks found that lions catch zebras as often as plain-colored antelope of similar size. She argues that stripes more likely protect zebras from biting flies, which avoid striped surfaces.
Based on the texts, how would Brooks most likely respond to the biologists' argument?
- She would accept it, since lions hunt zebras in herds. :: Her finding shows stripes give zebras no edge against lions. She rejects the argument.
- She would argue that lions can't see stripes. :: Right attitude, wrong reason. Brooks never discusses lion eyesight. Her evidence is how often zebras get caught.
* She would argue that stripes probably don't protect zebras from lions, since lions catch zebras as often as plain antelope.
- She would claim that antelope are harder for lions to catch than zebras. :: Her finding is that both get caught about equally often. This choice twists it.
> Direction: Brooks (2) → biologists (1). Text 1: stripes confuse lions. Text 2: lions catch zebras as often as plain antelope, so stripes give no edge against lions.
> Prediction: "Wrong, because zebras get caught just as often." Several choices disagree, but only one gives her reason.
```

## What would both authors sign?

For "both authors would most likely agree", ignore what they argue about. Look for what **both take for granted**. It's usually modest and general.

Test every choice: would **each** author sign it? A choice only one author would sign is wrong, however true it sounds.

```quiz
Text 1: Coach Ella Ward says young swimmers should train year-round, since their skills fade during long breaks.
Text 2: Physician Raj Patel warns that year-round training wears out young swimmers' shoulders. He advises a three-month break each year, even though some skills will slip.
Both authors would most likely agree with which statement?
* Young swimmers can lose some skill during a long break.
- Young swimmers should train all year. :: Only Ward signs this. Patel advises a break every year.
- Year-round training harms young swimmers' shoulders. :: Only Patel says this. Ward never mentions injuries.
- Healthy shoulders matter more than lost skills. :: That's Patel's trade-off. Ward puts skills first and never mentions injuries.
> They disagree about breaks. Underneath, Ward says skills fade during breaks, and Patel admits some skills will slip. That modest shared point is the answer.
```

## The trap: all or nothing

The right answer matches the **strength** of the response.

- Text 2 only raises a doubt (*may*, *one study*)? The answer doubts too: *premature*, *not yet supported*. "Disproves" is too strong.
- Text 2 rejects the claim? "Largely correct, needs a small fix" is too mild.
- Text 2 says "yes, but"? Both "fully accepts" and "completely rejects" are out.

```quiz
Text 1: Archaeologists found a 9,000-year-old clay figure of a woman in the village of Kesra. They argue that its people worshipped a mother goddess.
Text 2: Historian Hassan Adeyemi notes that it is the only such figure found at the site, and that similar figures found elsewhere were children's toys. One object, he says, cannot settle what it meant.
How would Adeyemi most likely respond to the archaeologists' argument?
- By proving that the villagers had no religion :: Too strong. He says one object can't settle the question, not that he can prove the opposite.
- By calling it largely correct but in need of a small adjustment :: Too mild. He doubts the whole conclusion, not one detail of it.
* By warning that the conclusion may be premature, since it rests on one figure that could have been a toy
> Adeyemi raises a doubt, nothing more: one figure, maybe a toy. The answer must match that strength. "Proving" overshoots, and "a small adjustment" undershoots.
```

## When Text 1 answers back

Some stems flip the direction: how would **Text 1's author** respond to Text 2? That author never read Text 2. So take their claim or rule and **apply it to Text 2's facts**.

The trap choice states Text 2's own view, as if the speakers had swapped.

```quiz
Text 1: Critic Maya Lund argues that film music should never draw attention to itself. The best scores, she says, go unnoticed by the audience.
Text 2: The 2024 film "Northern Line" has a loud, unusual score that audiences discuss after every screening. Many reviewers call the score the film's greatest strength.
Based on the texts, how would Lund most likely respond to the reviewers' praise?
- She would agree that the score is the film's greatest strength. :: That's the reviewers' view, from Text 2. Lund prizes scores that go unnoticed.
* She would see a score that audiences notice this much as a flaw, not a strength.
- She would argue that audiences didn't really notice the score. :: Text 2 says they discuss it. Lund's rule is about whether being noticed is good, not whether it happened.
> Direction: Lund (1) → reviewers (2). Apply her rule to Text 2's facts: good scores go unnoticed, and this one is the talk of every screening. So she'd call it a flaw.
```

## Recap

- Mark the direction: who answers whom, and about which claim.
- Shrink each text to one line: the claim, not the topic.
- Find the point of contact: claim, evidence, scope or cause.
- Ask what Text 2 keeps, then predict the response with a reason.
- Match the strength: a doubt isn't a disproof, and "yes, but" is neither "fully" nor "completely".
- Agree questions: pick the modest idea each author would sign.
