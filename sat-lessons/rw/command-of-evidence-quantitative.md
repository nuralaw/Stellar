## What this question looks like

You get a short text plus a **table, bar graph or line graph**, shown together on the left of the screen, with the question and choices on the right. Those are the only three kinds of graphics in the Reading and Writing section. The text usually describes a study, a survey or some records, and it ends with a claim or with a sentence that has a blank. The question asks you to use the data.

Typical questions:

- "Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to **complete the statement**?" (or "…to complete the example?")
- "Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to **support** the researchers' claim?"
- "Which choice best describes data from the graph that **support** the hypothesis?"
- "Which choice best describes data from the table that **weaken** the official's claim?" (rare)

**How many and where.** Expect about 2 to 4 per test. They sit in the Command of Evidence part of the Information and Ideas group, mixed in with the "finding" and "quotation" questions and ordered from easier to harder.

These questions look like math, but they are reading questions. The College Board says these questions don't require calculations, and no calculator is allowed in this section. At most you compare numbers or notice that one is much bigger than another. What they really test is whether you pick the data that matches the *exact* claim.

> **Key idea:** The right choice passes two tests at once. It must be **accurate** (true according to the graphic) and **relevant** (it does the job the claim needs). Most wrong choices pass one test and fail the other.

## The method

1. **Read the question and the claim first.** Skip the graphic for now. Find the job word (*complete*, *support* or *weaken*). Then find the claim: the sentence with the blank, or the sentence that states the hypothesis or conclusion.
2. **Break the claim into parts.** Which groups or items? Which measure (number, percent, days, temperature)? Which time or condition? Which relationship (*more than*, *rose*, *the highest*, *varied widely*, *both*)?
3. **Predict the data you need.** Turn the claim into a test. For example: "I need white rocks lower than black rocks on *both* temperature and water loss." For weaken: "I need something the claim says should not happen."
4. **Do a relevance pass.** Without looking at the graphic, cross out any choice that wouldn't do the job *even if its numbers were right*: it talks about one group when you need two, uses the wrong measure, or supports when you need to weaken. This often removes two choices in seconds.
5. **Do an accuracy pass.** Now read the graphic: title, column headers or axis labels, units and legend. Find only the rows, bars or points you need, and check every number in the remaining choices.
6. **Choose the choice that is accurate and relevant.** A quick test: finish two sentences, "The data show …" and "That proves the claim because …". If you can't finish the second one, the choice is irrelevant. If two choices still look good, you skipped part of the claim. Read it again.

> **Tip:** Some teachers scan the graphic first. Claim first is faster and safer: until you know what you're looking for, every number looks important. Just make sure you read the labels and units before you trust any number.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Reading the three graphics

- **Tables:** each number sits where a row meets a column. Read both headers before you use a number, and read any note under the table (for example, "rows may not add up to 100 because of rounding").
- **Bar graphs:** each bar's height is a value. The legend tells you which color is which group. Check where the vertical axis starts: if it doesn't start at 0, a bar that looks twice as tall may be only a little bigger.
- **Line graphs:** the horizontal axis is usually time. Each line is one group, named in the legend. Look for rises, falls, peaks and places where lines cross. The gap between two groups is the vertical distance between their lines.
- **Units:** "percent", "in thousands", "per 1,000 people" and "average" all change what a number means. In this lesson, graphs are given as tables of the values they show.

### Claim words and what they need

| The claim says | The data must show |
|---|---|
| increased / decreased | The end value is higher / lower than the start value |
| the largest increase / the biggest change | The biggest difference between start and end, which may not be the highest final value |
| steadily / every year / consistently | Every step moves the same way; one reversal breaks it |
| peaked | The highest point, with lower values on both sides |
| the most / the highest | Higher than *every* other item, not just one of them |
| more than half / a majority | Above 50%; exactly 50% is not a majority |
| varied widely / differed sharply | Values that are far apart, usually two items compared |
| similar / about the same | Values that are close together |
| the gap widened / narrowed | The difference between two groups is bigger / smaller at the end than at the start |
| both / each / all | Every named group meets the condition |

### Count versus share

"More people bike to work" is about a **count**. "A higher rate", "a larger share" or "more common" is about a **percent**. A big city can have more cyclists *and* a smaller share of cyclists than a small city. Always check which one the claim is about.

### What a strong answer usually looks like

It names the right groups, uses the right measure and the right years, gets the numbers right, and makes the comparison the claim needs. Very often it has the shape "A … compared with B …", because most claims are about a difference or a change.

## Worked examples

Every text and data set below was written for this lesson. The people, places and studies are made up.

### Example 1: Complete an example

**Preferred format for reading books (percent of members in each age group)**

| Age group | Printed books | E-books | No preference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 16–24 | 38 | 49 | 13 |
| 25–39 | 52 | 35 | 13 |
| 40–59 | 64 | 22 | 14 |
| 60 and older | 79 | 9 | 12 |

Librarian Ana Kovač surveyed 2,000 library members about whether they prefer to read printed books or e-books. She found that members' preferences depended strongly on their age. For example, ______

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the example?

- (A) 79% of members aged 60 and older preferred printed books, compared with only 38% of members aged 16–24.
- (B) 13% of members aged 16–24 and 13% of members aged 25–39 had no preference.
- (C) 64% of members aged 40–59 preferred printed books.
- (D) 49% of members aged 60 and older preferred e-books, compared with only 9% of members aged 16–24.

**Solution.** The claim: preferences *depended strongly on age*. To show that, we need two age groups with very different results. Relevance pass: (C) talks about one age group only, so it can't show a difference. (B) compares two groups but gives the *same* number, which shows no difference at all. Accuracy pass on (A) and (D): the table gives 79% (60 and older, printed) and 38% (16–24, printed), so (A) is accurate and shows a big difference. (D) swaps the numbers: e-books were 9% for the oldest group and 49% for the youngest.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 2: Support a two-part hypothesis

The bar graph shows two measurements for snails resting on rocks of three colors: average body temperature at noon and average water lost over six hours (as a percent of body mass). The values shown in the graph are:

| Rock color | Body temperature at noon (°C) | Water lost in six hours (% of body mass) |
|---|---|---|
| White | 31 | 4 |
| Gray | 36 | 7 |
| Black | 41 | 11 |

Desert snails spend hot days sealed inside their shells, resting on rocks. Biologist Kwame Asante hypothesized that snails resting on lighter-colored rocks would stay cooler and lose less water than snails resting on darker rocks. To test this, he placed snails on white, gray and black rocks in the same sunny field.

Which choice best describes data from the graph that support Asante's hypothesis?

- (A) Snails on white rocks had an average body temperature of 31°C, and snails on black rocks lost an average of 11% of their body mass in water.
- (B) Snails on gray rocks lost more water than snails on black rocks did.
- (C) Snails on black rocks had a higher average body temperature than snails on gray rocks did.
- (D) Snails on white rocks had the lowest body temperature and lost the least water, while snails on black rocks had the highest body temperature and lost the most water.

**Solution.** The hypothesis has two parts: lighter rocks mean **cooler** and **less water lost**. We need both, compared across rock colors.

- (A) Both numbers are correct, but it gives temperature for one group and water loss for a different group. Nothing is compared, so it supports nothing. Accurate but irrelevant.
- (B) False: gray lost 7% and black lost 11%.
- (C) True (41 is more than 36), but it covers temperature only. Half the hypothesis.
- (D) White is lowest on both measures and black is highest on both (31 vs. 41°C, 4% vs. 11%). Both parts, accurate.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: A change over time on a line graph

The line graph shows the number of days each winter that two lakes in the same region were covered by ice, from 1970 to 2020. Avery Lake is deep, and Birch Lake is shallow. The values plotted in the graph are:

| Year | Avery Lake (deep) | Birch Lake (shallow) |
|---|---|---|
| 1970 | 98 | 112 |
| 1980 | 96 | 106 |
| 1990 | 93 | 99 |
| 2000 | 91 | 90 |
| 2010 | 88 | 82 |
| 2020 | 86 | 74 |

Lake scientist Ruth Adeyemi expected shallow lakes to respond more strongly to warming winters than deep lakes do. Average winter temperatures in the region rose steadily from 1970 to 2020. Her records for Avery Lake and Birch Lake support her expectation: over the fifty years of her study, ______

Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the statement?

- (A) the ice cover on both lakes declined, with Avery Lake falling from 98 days in 1970 to 86 days in 2020 and Birch Lake falling from 112 days in 1970 to 99 days in 2020.
- (B) the ice cover on Birch Lake fell from 112 days to 74 days, while the ice cover on Avery Lake fell only from 98 days to 86 days.
- (C) the ice cover on Avery Lake fell from 98 days in 1970 to 86 days in 2020, a loss of 12 days over the fifty years.
- (D) the ice cover on Birch Lake fell from 112 days to 86 days, while the ice cover on Avery Lake fell from 98 days to 74 days.

**Solution.** The claim: the *shallow* lake changed *more* than the deep lake over the fifty years. So we need the change in both lakes, from 1970 to 2020, with Birch's change bigger.

- (A) Birch's value for 2020 is 74, not 99; 99 is its value for 1990. Wrong year. With these numbers, the two lakes would also seem to change by about the same amount.
- (B) Birch dropped from 112 to 74 (38 days); Avery dropped from 98 to 86 (12 days). Accurate, compares both lakes, and shows the shallow lake changed more.
- (C) Accurate, but it is only the deep lake. Half the comparison.
- (D) Inaccurate: the two 2020 values are swapped. Birch ended at 74 and Avery at 86. This is what happens when you read the wrong line.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: Weaken a claim (count versus share)

**Survey of workers in four cities, 2024**

| City | Workers surveyed | Workers who bike to work | Share who bike to work (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alton | 12,000 | 1,080 | 9 |
| Brisk | 3,000 | 450 | 15 |
| Calder | 20,000 | 1,400 | 7 |
| Dunmore | 5,000 | 600 | 12 |

A transportation group surveyed workers in four cities about how they get to work. In a report, a Dunmore city official claimed that Dunmore has the highest rate of bicycle commuting of the four cities, meaning that a larger share of its workers bike to work than in any of the other three.

Which choice best describes data from the table that weaken the official's claim?

- (A) Calder had more workers who bike to work (1,400) than Dunmore did (600).
- (B) Brisk had a larger share of workers who bike to work (15%) than Dunmore did (12%).
- (C) Dunmore had a larger share of workers who bike to work (12%) than Alton did (9%).
- (D) Dunmore had more workers who bike to work (600) than Brisk did (450).

**Solution.** The claim is about a **rate**, a share of workers, not a count. To weaken "Dunmore is the highest", we need a city with a *higher share* than Dunmore's 12%.

- (A) Accurate, and it sounds strong, but it is a count. Calder has far more workers in the survey (20,000), so its count is big, yet its *share* is only 7%, lower than Dunmore's. Count-versus-share trap.
- (B) Brisk's 15% is higher than Dunmore's 12%, so Dunmore is not the highest. Accurate and weakens the claim.
- (C) Accurate, but it shows Dunmore beating a city, which fits the claim. Opposite job.
- (D) Accurate, and it makes Dunmore look good, but it is a count again. Brisk has fewer cyclists because far fewer of its workers were surveyed (3,000), yet its share (15%) is higher. A count can't support or weaken a claim about a rate.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 5: Use data to finish an argument

**Frogs in four ponds over five summers, 2019–2023**

| Pond | Frogs infected with the fungus (%) | Summers the pond dried up (out of 5) | Change in number of frogs over the five years (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| North | 62 | 0 | −8 |
| East | 35 | 3 | −37 |
| South | 18 | 0 | −4 |
| West | 40 | 3 | −41 |

A fungus has infected frogs of one species in many ponds, and some researchers blame it for the species' decline. Ecologist Lucia Brandt tracked four ponds over five summers. She concluded that the fungus alone cannot explain the pattern of losses and that ponds drying up in summer may also play a role, since ______

Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?

- (A) North Pond had the highest share of infected frogs (62%) but lost only 8% of its frogs, while East Pond and West Pond, the two ponds that dried up, lost 37% and 41%.
- (B) South Pond had the lowest share of infected frogs (18%) of the four ponds, and it did not dry up in any of the five summers.
- (C) East Pond and West Pond, which each dried up in three of the five summers, lost 37% and 41% of their frogs, the largest losses of the four ponds.
- (D) North Pond had a higher share of infected frogs (62%) than East Pond did (35%) but lost a much smaller share of its frogs (8% versus 37%).

**Solution.** Brandt's conclusion has two parts, and the blank must give evidence for both. **Part 1:** the fungus alone doesn't explain the losses, so we need a case where lots of fungus did *not* mean a big loss. **Part 2:** drying may matter, so we need the ponds that dried up to have the bigger losses. Every choice here is accurate, so this question is all about relevance.

- (A) North has the most fungus but lost only 8% (Part 1). The two ponds that dried up, East and West, lost 37% and 41%, far more than the ponds that never dried up (Part 2). Covers both parts.
- (B) It never mentions losses, so it can't show what explains them.
- (C) Supports Part 2, but East and West also have plenty of fungus (35% and 40%), so on its own this doesn't show that the fungus fails to explain the losses. Half the claim.
- (D) Supports Part 1, but it says nothing about drying. Half the claim.

This is the hardest version of the question: you have to combine columns with the logic of the text. Read the conclusion slowly, list its parts, then look for the one choice that proves each part.

**Answer:** (A)

## Traps to avoid

- **Accurate but irrelevant:** the numbers are right, but they don't prove the claim. This is the most common trap. Being true is not enough.
- **Half the claim:** the choice proves one part of a two-part claim (temperature but not water loss, one lake but not the other).
- **Wrong row, year or line:** a real number from the table, attached to the wrong group or year. Reading the wrong line on a line graph is very common.
- **Count versus share:** "more people" used to answer a claim about a rate or a percent, or the reverse.
- **Nothing compared:** two numbers about different measures or different groups that can't be compared, so they show no pattern.
- **Same values:** two equal numbers offered as proof of a big difference.
- **Highest value versus biggest change:** the group that ends highest is not always the group that grew the most. Check start and end.
- **Opposite job:** data that support the claim when the question asks you to weaken it, or the reverse.
- **Overreach:** a choice that adds a conclusion the data can't show, such as a cause, a prediction or "all", when the data only show a pattern in a few cases.

## Quick checklist

- Did I read the job word and the claim before the graphic?
- Did I list the claim's parts: groups, measure, time and relationship?
- Did I cross out choices that wouldn't do the job even with correct numbers?
- Did I read the title, labels, units and legend before trusting a number?
- Does my choice cover every part of the claim, in the right direction?
- Count or share: does my choice use the same kind of number as the claim?
