# Compare the claims
Text 2 can agree, add a limit, or disagree.

```visual
cross-text-connections
```

## Key ideas
Two short texts discuss one subject. Most questions ask how the author of Text 2 would respond to Text 1: "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to…?"

1. Shrink Text 1 to its **claim** (its main point) in one line.
2. Read Text 2 and find its claim **and its evidence**.
3. Name the relationship. Text 2 **supports** the claim (agrees, with evidence), **limits** it (*yes, but* only in some cases), or **opposes** it (disagrees).
4. Pick the answer that fits that relationship.

Disagreement is the most common pattern, but not the only one. Read Text 2's evidence before you decide.

```example
Text 1: Many gardeners say that mulch keeps soil moist.
Text 2: Hassan measured soil in 40 beds. Mulched beds held 30% more water than bare beds.
How would Hassan most likely respond to Text 1?
1. Text 1: mulch keeps soil moist :: Shrink Text 1.
2. Text 2: mulched beds held more water :: Shrink Text 2.
3. Answer: His data support the gardeners' claim. :: Text 2 supports Text 1.
```

```theory
**Claim and evidence.** A claim is what an author wants you to accept. Evidence is what backs it: a result, a number, an example. Text 2's *evidence* tells you its position, so read it closely.

**Three relationships.**
- *Supports*: Text 2 agrees and often adds proof.
- *Limits* (a qualification): Text 2 agrees in part, or only under some conditions. Signals: *but*, *however*, *only when*, *in some cases*.
- *Opposes*: Text 2's evidence or reasoning goes against Text 1.

**Positive and negative.** If the authors clearly disagree, the right answer is negative in tone ("challenges", "disputes", "questions"). Cross out positive choices. If they agree, do the opposite.

**Reported views.** "Some argue…" or "Researchers have long believed…" reports a view. The author of Text 1 may not hold it. Only judge the view as it is written.

**When Text 2 never mentions Text 1.** Ask: if Text 1's claim were true, what would Text 2's evidence look like? If the evidence matches, Text 2 supports; if it conflicts, Text 2 opposes; if it fits only some cases, Text 2 limits.

**Wording.** Choices for a "respond" question often begin with *By* plus a verb: *By pointing out…*, *By arguing…*. The verb must be as strong as Text 2: do not choose "By proving…" when Text 2 only suggests.
```

# When Text 2 adds
Text 2 can build on Text 1 instead of arguing.

```visual
cross-text-connections--adds
```

## Key ideas
Texts do not always fight. Text 2 may give an **example** of Text 1's general point, a **reason** for what Text 1 describes, a **solution** to a problem Text 1 raises, or **new details**.

Ask: "What does Text 2 do with Text 1's idea?" The question often reads: "Which choice best describes the relationship between the texts?" Typical answers:
- Text 2 offers a possible **explanation** for the trend in Text 1.
- Text 2 presents a **solution** to the problem in Text 1.
- Text 2 gives a **specific case** of the general idea in Text 1.

```example
Text 1: The perch in Lake Verda have declined for ten years.
Text 2: A survey found that the lake's reeds were dredged ten years ago. Perch lay their eggs in reeds.
Which choice best describes the relationship between the texts?
Choices: Text 2 offers a possible explanation for the trend in Text 1. / Text 2 questions whether the trend in Text 1 is real.
1. Text 1: perch are declining :: A trend.
2. Text 2: reeds gone, perch need reeds :: A possible cause.
3. Answer: Text 2 offers a possible explanation :: It builds on Text 1.
```

```theory
**Complementary texts.** Two texts are *complementary* when each adds something to the other. They can agree about a fact and still have different jobs.

**Common relationships.**
- *Explanation*: Text 1 states what happened; Text 2 says why.
- *Example*: Text 1 states a rule; Text 2 shows a case.
- *Solution*: Text 1 describes a problem; Text 2 proposes or reports a fix.
- *New evidence*: Text 2 adds data that supports, complicates or questions an explanation in Text 1.

**How to find it.** Describe each text with a verb: "Text 1 reports…", "Text 2 offers…". Then pick the choice that names both jobs. Both halves of a choice must be true. "Text 2 questions whether the trend is real" fails if Text 2 never doubts the trend.

**A general-to-specific pattern.** If Text 1 says something is generally true and Text 2 describes a particular case where it holds, an answer such as "Text 2 gives a specific instance of what Text 1 describes" fits. If the case is surprising, the answer might use *unexpected*.

**Trap.** Assuming Text 2 must disagree. Look for words that show Text 2 *accepts* Text 1's facts (it repeats them, builds on them, or uses them without questioning).
```

# How would they respond?
Test Text 1's claim against Text 2's evidence.

```visual
cross-text-connections--evidence-test
```

## Key ideas
Often Text 2 never mentions Text 1's claim. Then you must test it yourself.

1. Write Text 1's claim in one sentence.
2. Write Text 2's result or evidence in one sentence.
3. Ask: if the claim were true, would this evidence look like that? Match means support; clash means opposition; a partial match means a limit.

If **Text 1's** author responds to Text 2, flip the roles: take the **rule or opinion** Text 1 states and apply it to Text 2's facts.

```example
Text 1: Some ecologists argue that old trees speed up the growth of nearby seedlings by sharing nutrients through underground fungi.
Text 2: Ito planted seedlings at 20 sites. They grew at the same rate beside old trees and far from them.
How would Ito most likely describe the view in Text 1?
1. Text 1: old trees speed up seedlings :: A view to test.
2. Text 2: same growth near and far :: Her result.
3. Answer: Her results do not support it. :: The evidence clashes with the claim.
```

```theory
**Why this works.** The SAT rewards you for using evidence, not guesses about what authors "feel". Text 2's data decide what Text 2's author can say.

**Reported views.** "Some ecologists argue…" is a view, not Text 1's own claim. You do not need to know whether Text 1's author agrees. The question asks only how Text 2's author would treat the view.

**Strength.** Match the strength of the answer to the strength of the evidence. One experiment that shows no effect supports "does not support the view" or "likely makes no difference", not "proves it false".

**Two-part answers.** Choices such as "It may seem plausible, but the findings do not support it" are fine **only if both parts are backed by the texts**. The first half needs a reason in Text 2 too (for example, the researchers expected the idea to hold). If nothing in Text 2 makes the idea plausible, drop that half.

**Text 1 responds.** Chef Okoro holds that using more than five ingredients spoils a dish. A new restaurant praises a soup with nineteen. What would Okoro say? Apply his rule to the new fact: the soup has too many ingredients. Do not take the reviewers' side just because Text 2 sounds persuasive.

**Trap.** Choosing an answer that sounds like Text 2's topic but ignores its evidence.
```

# Both agree
Find the one point both texts state.

```visual
cross-text-connections--venn
```

## Key ideas
A "both authors would agree" question wants a point that **both texts support**. It is usually modest, not either author's main claim.

1. List what Text 1 says. Then list what Text 2 says.
2. Cross out any choice that only one text supports.
3. Cross out extreme choices and anything neither text mentions.
4. The survivor is on both lists.

Each author's main claim often belongs to one list only, so it is a trap answer. Watch for choices that use a word from the texts but give it to the wrong author.

```example
Text 1: Lee says cities should ban cars downtown, because traffic there endangers walkers.
Text 2: Novak opposes a ban, which would hurt shops. She admits downtown traffic endangers walkers and wants lower speed limits.
On what would both authors agree?
1. Lee: ban cars; traffic endangers walkers :: List Lee's points.
2. Novak: no ban; traffic endangers walkers :: List Novak's points.
3. Answer: Downtown traffic endangers walkers. :: It is on both lists.
```

```theory
**Why the shared point is small.** Authors who disagree about the big question still often share a background fact. The question rewards you for finding that fact.

**Wrong answers.** *One-sided*: true for one author only. *Extreme*: adds *always*, *only*, *all*. *Mixed*: combines Text 1's claim with Text 2's detail. *Unmentioned*: a reasonable idea neither text states.

**Same stem, other jobs.** "Based on the texts, what do the authors mainly disagree about?" asks for the point where the two lists **clash**. "Both texts discuss which topic?" asks for the shared subject only.

**Reasons can differ.** Two authors can agree on a fact for different reasons. One may say a bakery chose a corner shop to attract commuters, the other that it chose it for the cheap rent. Both still agree the choice was deliberate.

**Method.** For hard questions, read Text 1, then test each choice against Text 1 and cross out those it does not support. Then test only the survivors against Text 2. This avoids confusion between the two texts.

**Trap.** A word from the text attached to the wrong author or the wrong idea. Always ask: which text says it?
```

# Describe a difference
Text 1 says this, while Text 2 says that.

```visual
cross-text-connections--halves
```

## Key ideas
A difference question asks: "Which choice best describes a difference in how the author of Text 1 and the author of Text 2 view…?" Each choice has **two halves**: "Text 1 …, while Text 2 …".

1. Name the subject the question names.
2. Write each author's view of **that subject** in a few words.
3. Find the choice whose two halves match, in the right order.
4. Check each half against its own text. One wrong half is enough to reject a choice.

```example
Text 1: New bike lanes made Pine Street safer; accidents fell by a third.
Text 2: The drop is real, but traffic also fell after a bridge opened.
How do the authors differ in their view of the lanes?
Choices: Text 1 credits the lanes, while Text 2 doubts they caused the drop. / Text 1 doubts the drop is real, while Text 2 credits the lanes.
1. Text 1 view: the lanes made it safer :: Half 1.
2. Text 2 view: drop is real, cause unclear :: Half 2.
3. Answer: Text 1 credits the lanes, while Text 2 doubts they caused the drop. :: Both halves true, right order.
```

```theory
**What "view" can mean.** The question may ask about how the authors view a study, a claim, a person, an event, or a thing (for example, the moon in two poems). It may also ask for a difference in *focus* or *approach*: which part of the issue each text stresses, or what method each uses.

**The half-right trap.** The SAT's most common wrong answer gets one half right and bends the other. Suppose Text 1 calls a film beautiful but too long, and Text 2 calls it beautiful but badly acted. A tempting wrong choice says Text 2 focuses *only* on the film's flaws, but Text 2 also praises it.

**Other traps.** *Swapped authors*: the right ideas, but given to the wrong text. *A difference that is not there*: a contrast neither text makes. *A true but minor difference*: something real that is not about the subject in the question.

**Be specific.** The right answer names concrete views. If you can write each half yourself (for example "Text 1: benefits arrive automatically; Text 2: benefits need effort"), you can match it quickly.

**Poems and passages from fiction.** Compare *how each speaker portrays* the subject: tone (fond, bored, amazed), images, and what is stressed.
```

# Trap: strength and halves
Match every word of the answer to the texts.

```visual
cross-text-connections--strength
```

## Key ideas
Wrong answers on cross-text questions usually fail in one of three ways:
- **Too strong**: *proves*, *only*, *always*, *entirely*, when the text hedges.
- **Half-right**: the first half fits, the second is invented.
- **Wrong owner**: a real idea from the texts given to the wrong author.

Match the **strength** of Text 2's evidence. A small study that doubts a claim supports "needs more testing", not "is false". Doubt is not disproof. When the text hedges (*may*, *suggests*, *one study*), the right answer hedges too.

```example
Text 1: A study of 12 people found that a new tea improves sleep.
Text 2: Kaur says 12 people are too few to show anything. She calls for a larger study.
How would Kaur respond to the study?
Choices: The finding needs more testing. / The tea does not work.
1. Kaur: the sample is too small :: Her point.
2. She never says the tea fails :: Check how far she goes.
3. Answer: The finding needs more testing. :: Doubt, not "the tea fails".
```

```theory
**Strength ladder.** From weaker to stronger: *needs more testing*, *premature*, *uncertain*, *not supported*, *likely wrong*, *false*. If Text 2 says "too few to show anything", it sits at the weak end.

**Hedge words in Text 2.** *May*, *suggests*, *likely*, *one study*, *small sample*: the correct answer can include hedges too. Extreme words in a choice (*always*, *never*, *completely*, *only*) are suspect unless the text uses them.

**Half-right check.** Split each choice in two. For "Handwriting helps, but only with ideas", ask: does Text 2 show it helps? Does it show it helps *only* for ideas? Real SAT choices are often built so that the first half is solid and the second half over-reaches.

**Word-from-text trap.** A choice may use a word from one text but attach it to the other author or to a different idea. Check *whose* idea each phrase is.

**Do not guess from tone alone.** Use evidence. Sometimes Text 2 goes beyond doubt and offers a result that reverses Text 1's claim. Then a mild choice such as "more research is needed" is too weak. Match the strength both ways: not stronger, and not weaker, than the text.

**Mixed answers.** A "yes, but" choice must have support for both the yes and the but: Text 2 should show some agreement and some limit.
```
