# Topic plus point
The point often follows *but*, *however*, *yet*.

```visual
central-ideas
```

## Key ideas
The **main idea** is the whole text in one sentence: **topic + point**. The topic is who or what the text is about. The point is what the author says about it.

1. Find the **pivot**. *But*, *however*, *yet*, *actually* and *in reality* turn the text. The point usually comes after the turn.
2. Spot the **old idea**: "Many people believe…", "For decades…", "Traditionally…". The new idea follows: "Recently…", "New research shows…".
3. Say the main idea in your own words **before** you read the choices.

If there is no pivot, the point is often in the first or last sentence, and the rest supports it.

```example
For decades, biologists assumed octopuses sleep in one quiet state. But when Lena Park's team filmed them, the animals sometimes entered an active stage: their skin flashed colors and their arms twitched.
What is the main idea of the text?
1. Topic: octopus sleep :: Who or what is it about?
2. *But* ends the old view, starts the finding :: Find the word that turns.
3. Main idea: octopus sleep also has an active stage. :: Say it before the choices.
```

```theory
**Main idea vs. details.** A detail is one fact or example. The main idea is what all the sentences add up to. A good test: if you remove a sentence and the main idea still stands, that sentence was a detail.

**Old idea, new idea.** Many central-ideas texts follow the pattern "people used to think X, but now Y". Signals of the old idea: *many people believe, some argue, traditionally, for decades, long assumed, historically*. Signals of the new idea: *but, however, yet, actually, in reality, recently, it turns out*. The main idea is almost always the new idea (though a good answer may mention both).

**Studies.** In a text about research, the main idea is usually *what researchers found*, with a hint at why it matters. Methods and numbers are details.

**Same idea, different words.** The right answer **rephrases** the text. A choice that copies the text's words but changes the meaning is a trap.

**Not the same as purpose.** A purpose question asks what the text *does* (to argue, to explain). A main idea question asks what the text *says*. If the stem says "main idea", answer with a statement, not a verb.

**Stuck?** Cross out choices that cover only one sentence, choices that say more than the text, and choices that say the opposite.
```

# The right size
Not one detail. Not a rule about everything.

```visual
central-ideas--right-size
```

## Key ideas
A good main idea is **the size of the text**: it covers every sentence and nothing more.

- **Too narrow**: true, but it covers only one sentence or example.
- **Too broad**: it says more than the text, for example about all farming when the text is about one insect.
- **Just right**: a short summary of the whole text, using the text's own topic.

Check each choice: does it match the first, middle and last parts of the text? If a choice only fits one part, it is too narrow. If it needs facts the text does not give, it is too broad.

```example
Honeybees do more than make honey. They pollinate apple trees, almond groves and clover fields. Farmers even rent hives to raise their harvests.
What is the main idea of the text?
1. "Farmers rent hives." :: Too narrow: one detail.
2. "Insects are vital to all farming." :: Too broad: the text is only about honeybees.
3. Honeybees help crops grow, not just make honey. :: Fits all three sentences.
```

```theory
**Too narrow.** Test writers copy a real fact from the text into a choice. It is true, but it only covers one sentence. Ask: does this choice explain *why* the author mentioned the other sentences?

**Too broad.** A choice that jumps to a general rule ("Insects are vital to all farming") sounds smart but claims more than the text shows. If you cannot point to evidence in the text, it is too broad.

**Wrong focus.** A choice can have the right topic and the wrong point. If the text is about a scientist's *finding*, a choice about her *career* or *method* is off.

**The summary test.** Imagine a friend who has not read the text. Would the choice tell them what the text is about and what it says? If it leaves out a main part, or adds something new, reject it.

**Mixed findings and explanations.** When a text reports a finding and then explains it, the best main idea often joins them: "A shows B, and C explains why." A choice that gives only one half is too narrow.

**Do not rely on the first sentence.** Sometimes the first sentence is just a setup, and the point arrives later.
```

# Grant, then point
Authors may admit a point, then give their own.

```visual
central-ideas--grant-point
```

## Key ideas
An author can **grant** (admit) a point, then argue for another. The main idea is the **point**; it may mention the grant as context: "X may be true, but Y matters more." A choice with only the grant is wrong.

Signals of a grant: *although*, *while*, *admittedly*, *granted*, *it may seem*, *of course*.

Double negatives are mild positives. *Not unreasonable* means reasonable; *not inconceivable* means possible; *hardly implausible* means plausible. A "not + negative word" grant is a **small yes**, not a strong one.

A choice that keeps only the granted part is a trap.

```example
Critics praise the novel's plot. It is not unreasonable to admire the plot, but the book's real strength is its narrator, whose voice grows stranger and more convincing with each chapter.
What is the main idea of the text?
1. Grant: "not unreasonable" to admire the plot :: A small agreement.
2. Point: after *but*, the narrator :: The author's own claim.
3. Main idea: the narrator, not the plot, is the strength. :: The point; the plot is only the grant.
```

```theory
**What a grant does.** The author gives the other side a small point to look fair, then explains why another point matters more. Because it comes first, test-takers often pick the granted idea by mistake.

**Where the real claim is.** After *but*, *however*, *yet* or *still*. In "Although X, Y", the point is Y. In "X, but Y", the point is also Y.

**How to read mild words.** "Not inconceivable" is weaker than "likely". It means "possible, I will give you that". So an answer that says the granted idea is *definitely true* is too strong.

**The best choice.** It states the author's point, and it may mention the granted point as context ("Although [the granted point], [the author's point]"). A choice that drops the grant can still be right, since the point is what counts; a choice that keeps only the grant is wrong.

**Study texts.** A text may say "researchers believed X" (the grant) and then give the new result. The main idea is the new result with the old belief as context.

**Where else it shows up.** The pattern also appears in purpose, function and cross-text questions, so practice spotting the word that turns the text.
```

# Stated details
Find the sentence that answers the question.

```visual
central-ideas--find-line
```

## Key ideas
Questions with **According to the text** or **Based on the text** ask for something the passage states. The answer is written in the text. Do not guess, and do not bring in outside facts.

1. Underline what the question asks: *why*, *how*, *what*, *who*, and any limit such as *first* or *only*.
2. Find the sentence in the text that answers it.
3. Choose the choice that **restates** that sentence, often in different words.
4. Check it answers *this* question. A true detail can answer a different one.

```example
The first bicycles had no pedals: riders pushed off the ground with their feet. In the 1860s, pedals were added to the front wheel.
According to the text, how did the first riders move?
1. Asked: the *first* riders :: Mark the exact question.
2. Sentence 1: "pushed off the ground with their feet" :: Find its line.
3. Answer: They pushed off the ground with their feet. :: Pedals came later.
```

```theory
**What the stems look like.** "According to the text, why…?", "Based on the text, what…?", "The text says…". Both *according to* and *based on the text* ask you to retrieve what the text says. Save inference for questions that use words like *suggests* or *implies* (next lesson part).

**Question words.** *Why* needs a reason (look for *because*, *so*, *led to*). *How* needs a method or manner. *What* needs a fact. A "why" question is not answered by a detail that only says *what* happened.

**Limit words.** *First*, *only*, *main*, *before*, *after*: a choice that is true for the wrong time or the wrong item is wrong.

**Paraphrase.** The correct choice rarely copies the text. It uses new words for the same fact: "the roof was patched with tin" may become "metal was used to repair the roof".

**Traps.** (1) A detail that is true but answers another question, for example a later result when the question asks for the cause. (2) A choice that reverses cause and effect. (3) An unstated reason that sounds sensible. (4) A choice built from recycled words.

**Speed tip.** Find the key word of the question in the passage, read one sentence before and after it, then match.
```

# Implied details
*Most strongly suggests* allows one small step.

```visual
central-ideas--small-step
```

## Key ideas
Questions that say **most strongly suggests**, **implies**, **indicates** or **can be inferred** allow **one small step** beyond the words of the text. They do not allow a leap.

- The answer is a **rephrasing** of one sentence, or a **combination** of two sentences.
- It adds no new facts, no extreme words, and no second step.

Method: find the relevant lines, say in plain words what they add up to, then choose the answer that goes no further. If a choice needs you to guess a motive, a plan or a number the text does not give, it is a leap.

```example
Veln harbor freezes solid each winter, and by February every fishing boat sits stuck in the ice. Tomas, who owns one of the boats, spends that month repairing his nets and repainting his hull.
What does the text most strongly suggest about why Tomas does these jobs in February?
Choices: He cannot fish then, so he uses the time for repairs. / He plans to sell the boat before spring.
1. Sentence 1: boats are stuck in ice in February :: Fact 1.
2. Sentence 2: Tomas repairs gear :: Fact 2.
3. Answer: He cannot fish then, so he uses the time for repairs. :: One small step joins the facts.
```

```theory
**Retrieval vs. inference.** "According to the text" and "Based on the text" ask what is written. "Most strongly suggests", "implies" and "most reasonably be inferred" ask what the text makes clear without stating it.

**One small step.** If the text says boats are stuck in ice and Tomas repairs gear, the one-step conclusion is that he cannot fish. A second step ("so he will sell the boat") is a leap, because nothing supports it.

**Ways to get an inference.**
- *Rephrase*: a comparison such as "like a safe harbor for every sailor" shows that something shelters those in it.
- *Combine two sentences*: one gives a cause, the other the result.
- *Apply a definition*: a text that lists what a term includes lets you decide whether a new case counts.

**What is not allowed.** New facts you know from outside the text. Extreme words (*always, all, only, never*). Choices that reverse cause and effect. A choice built around a detail that is only mentioned in passing.

**Hedges.** If the text says *often* or *may*, the answer cannot say *always* or *definitely*.

**Check.** Point at the two phrases in the text that support your answer. If you need a third, you probably took too big a step. There is a separate lesson on Inferences that goes deeper.
```

# Stories and poems
Read the blurb, then watch what characters do.

```visual
central-ideas--says-does
```

## Key ideas
Fiction and poetry passages start with a **blurb**: one or two lines before the text, giving the author, the year, and often who is speaking and what is happening. **Read it.** It tells you who the characters are and why the scene matters.

Then read **literally**. Do not invent symbols or motives.
- Track what the character **says** and **does**, and the narrator's word choices.
- Watch for a **shift**: *but*, *yet*, *after*, a change of place or time.
- If words and actions differ, keep **both**: calm face, restless night.

For poems, rewrite each line in plain words. Old words like *thee*, *'tis*, *o'er* and *ere* are just old-fashioned English.

```example
The following text is from a short story. Mr. Aldous has entered his roses in a village contest.
Mr. Aldous declared himself perfectly indifferent to the prize, yet he rose before dawn to tend his roses with extraordinary care. When the judges arrived, he discovered urgent business in the far field.
What is the main idea of the text?
1. Says: "perfectly indifferent" :: His words.
2. Does: up at dawn, then hides away :: His actions.
3. Answer: He pretends not to care about the prize, but he does. :: Keep both halves.
```

```theory
**Why the blurb matters.** It can tell you who is talking ("Jane, the narrator"), what just happened, or what the setting is. A choice can be wrong simply because it contradicts the blurb.

**Characters.** A character's feelings show in actions, speech and thoughts. Quote-based reading: find the words in the text that prove the feeling. If you cannot find any, the choice is guessing.

**Tone of the narrator.** Narrators can be gently humorous, admiring, or critical. Words like *simple-hearted*, *transparent*, *vanity* show gentle teasing. Notice the narrator's attitude, as well as the character's.

**Shifts.** A character may start calm and end troubled. The main idea often joins the two moods: "She acts calm but is privately troubled."

**Poems.** Read by punctuation, not by line breaks. Translate old words: *thee* = you, *'tis* = it is, *o'er* = over, *ere* = before. Then ask what the speaker feels and what causes it. Images and metaphors stand for feelings: a seed that sleeps all winter can stand for a hope that is waiting.

**Avoid.** Big symbolic readings, outside knowledge about the author, and choices that add a motive the text never gives.
```

# Trap: one word too strong
*Some* is not *all*. *May* is not *proves*.

```visual
central-ideas--strength
```

## Key ideas
One word the text does not support makes a choice wrong. Compare strength words.

- **Strong**: *all, every, always, never, only, proves, best, completely.*
- **Weaker**: *some, most, often, may, suggests, slightly, likely.*

Pick the choice whose strength matches the text's own words. Watch for **recycled language**: a choice that uses the text's words but changes the meaning, or adds a claim (a cause, a ranking, a prediction) the text never makes.

*Most gardeners* is moderate. *The most important reason* is extreme.

```example
In a survey of 40 gardeners, most preferred clay pots. The author says the result might reflect local habits.
Which statement does the text support?
1. "Gardeners always prefer clay pots." :: "Always": the text says "most" of 40.
2. Most of the surveyed gardeners preferred clay pots. :: Same strength as the text.
```

```theory
**Hedged texts.** SAT passages about studies are usually careful: *may, suggests, small study, slightly, some*. The right answer is equally careful. An answer with *proves, always, every, the best, the only* usually goes beyond the text.

**Two kinds of "most".** *Most of 40 gardeners* is a quantity: more than half. It is weaker than *all*. *The most important reason* is a superlative that ranks one thing above all others and needs strong support.

**Added claims.** A choice may be built from correct facts and then add a ranking ("the best way"), a cause ("because"), or a prediction. If the text does not state it, it is wrong.

**Recycled language.** The wrong choice repeats the text's phrases but links them in a way the text does not. Check the *relationship* the choice states, not just the words.

**Opposite or reversed.** A choice that has the right words but swaps cause and effect, or the group studied, is wrong.

**Quick check.** Read the choice and ask "Does the text say exactly this much?" If the text says "may help some students" and the choice says "helps students", it is too strong.

**Remember.** Strength words work in every R&W lesson: purpose verbs, cross-text answers and inferences.
```
