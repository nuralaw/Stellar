# Name the link
Cover the choices. Join the ideas: and, but or so.

```visual
transitions--buckets
```

## Key ideas
A **transition** shows how the sentence it starts relates to the sentence before it. Even when the blank sits in the middle of a sentence, the word links back to what came before.

1. Cover the choices.
2. Read the sentence before the blank, then the sentence with the blank.
3. Join the two ideas with **and**, **but** or **so**.
4. Pick the choice from that bucket.

- **and**: an added point, an example, a restatement, a step in time.
- **but**: a contrast or a concession.
- **so**: a result or a conclusion.

Two choices that mean the same thing (*Therefore* and *Thus*) cannot both be right, so cross out both. A word inside the sentence, such as *because*, does not decide the answer. Compare the two whole ideas.

```example
Marta trained every day for the race. ______ she finished last.
Choices: Therefore, / However, / For example,
1. Idea 1: she trained every day :: Read the sentence before the blank.
2. Idea 2: she finished last :: Read the sentence after it.
3. Trained, but finished last :: Join them with and, but or so.
4. Answer: However, :: It is the only word from the but bucket.
```

```theory
A **transition** is a word or phrase that tells the reader how two ideas connect. The SAT asks: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?" All four choices are grammatical, so only logic can decide.

The transition always connects its own sentence to the sentence before it, wherever it stands. That is why a blank in the middle of a sentence still needs the previous sentence.

Transitions fall into three families. **Continuers** (and) add to an idea or repeat it: *Moreover*, *For example*, *Similarly*, *In fact*, *Later*. **Contradictors** (but) turn against it: *However*, *By contrast*, *Instead*, *Nevertheless*. **Cause-and-effect words** (so) show a result: *Therefore*, *As a result*, *Thus*. Continuers are the most common family on the test, so do not hunt for a contrast that is not there.

Why cover the choices first? Each choice is a ready-made story, and your brain will happily invent a reason for any of them. Naming the link yourself keeps you honest.

The synonym rule: if two choices mean the same, neither can be the answer, because only one choice is allowed to be right. Close neighbors can still differ in a small way (*Still* turns against a claim, *Instead* replaces it), and later parts of this lesson separate them.

Finally, read the two sentences again with your answer in place. The link should sound like the one you named.
```

## Easy
```task
bank: rw-0815
highlight:
- "Raw acorns have an extremely bitter taste" :: The bitter taste is the cause.
- "it was necessary to process the acorns" :: Processing is the result of the bitter taste.
explain:
Name the link before you look at the choices.

Sentence 1 says raw acorns taste extremely bitter. Sentence 2 says it was necessary to process them before eating. The bitter taste is the cause, and the processing is the result: bitter, **so** process them. That is the *so* bucket.

*Therefore* is the only word from that bucket. *However* turns against the first idea, but processing the acorns does not go against their bitterness. *Similarly* would add a second, alike thing, and *Afterward* would only give the order in time, not the reason.

The answer is **Therefore,**.
```

# Add and give examples
Moreover adds. For example narrows. Similarly compares.

```visual
transitions--add-example
```

## Key ideas
In the *and* bucket, ask what sentence 2 does with sentence 1.

- A **separate new point** on the same topic: *Moreover*, *Furthermore*, *In addition*, *Additionally*.
- **One case** of what sentence 1 said in general: *For example*, *For instance*.
- The **exact detail** of the same claim: *Specifically*, *In particular*.
- **A second thing that is alike**: *Similarly*, *Likewise*.

*Moreover* is not a trap. It is correct whenever sentence 2 is its own point. It is wrong only when sentence 2 explains, illustrates, contrasts with or results from sentence 1.

Position helps. A first example often sits in sentence 2, so use *For example*. If an example was already given, the next one is an addition (*Moreover*, *In addition*).

```example
The town library offers free tutoring for students. ______ it runs a weekly job-skills workshop for adults.
Choices: Moreover, / For example, / Nevertheless,
1. Idea 1: free tutoring, for students :: One service.
2. Idea 2: a workshop, for adults :: A different service.
3. Not a case of tutoring, not against it :: Example, contrast or new point?
4. Answer: Moreover, :: A separate point of the same kind.
```

```theory
Everything in this bucket keeps the text moving in the same direction, but the SAT separates the kinds carefully.

**Addition** words (*Moreover*, *Furthermore*, *In addition*, *Additionally*, *Also*, *What's more*) bring a second point. Two good reasons for one conclusion, or two examples of one idea, are the typical setting. If the first example already used *For example*, the second one is introduced by one of these words.

**Example** words (*For example*, *For instance*) introduce one case drawn from a group named before. Sentence 1 is general, and sentence 2 is specific. If sentence 2 is the same kind of thing as sentence 1 (not a member of it), it is an addition instead.

**Detail** words (*Specifically*, *In particular*) zoom in. They do not add a new point. They spell out exactly what sentence 1 said in broad terms, such as how, what or how much. The real test does not ask you to choose between *For example* and *Specifically* in the same question.

**Similarity** words (*Similarly*, *Likewise*, *In the same way*) bring a second subject that behaves the same way as the first. Two ducks, two cities or two experiments each doing the same thing fit.

How to avoid the usual mistakes: do not choose *Moreover* when sentence 2 is only a detail (use *Specifically*) or when it is a stronger version of the same idea (use *In fact*, covered next). Do not choose *For example* when the second thing is just another item of the same rank.
```

# In fact or reworded
Stronger detail, or the same idea in new words?

```visual
transitions--in-fact
```

## Key ideas
Two words in the *and* bucket look alike, but they do different jobs.

- **In fact / Indeed**: sentence 2 makes sentence 1 stronger with a more striking detail of the same idea. It does not start a new point.
- **In other words / That is**: sentence 2 says sentence 1 again in other words. It gives no new information.

Ask one question: **does sentence 2 give me something new and stronger?**

- Yes, same direction, stronger: *In fact*.
- No, only reworded: *In other words*.
- A separate point: *Moreover* (previous part).

*In fact* can also announce what is really true after a common belief. Check the logic: if sentence 2 corrects sentence 1, it is a contrast.

```example
Many students find the exam stressful. ______ some of them lose sleep for weeks beforehand.
Choices: In fact, / In other words, / Similarly,
1. Idea 1: many find the exam stressful :: A general claim.
2. Idea 2: some lose sleep for weeks :: A stronger detail.
3. Same direction, stronger, new fact :: Stronger, or only reworded?
4. Answer: In fact, :: It strengthens the claim.
```

```theory
Emphasis words repeat the direction of sentence 1 and push it further. *In fact*, *Indeed* and *Actually* introduce a stronger, more specific or more surprising detail that backs up what was just said. The writer is saying "and here is how true that is." The new sentence does not open a different point, which is why *Moreover* is the weaker choice when sentence 2 simply intensifies sentence 1.

Restatement words work the other way. *In other words*, *That is*, *Put differently* and *To put it simply* repeat the idea in simpler or clearer words. If you could delete sentence 2 and lose no information, it is a restatement. If you could delete it and lose a striking fact, it is an *In fact* sentence.

How the SAT builds the wrong choices:
- *Moreover* or *Furthermore* when the second sentence is really a stronger version of the first.
- *In other words* when sentence 2 brings a new fact (a number, a result, an unexpected detail).
- *However* or *Nevertheless* when the two sentences actually agree. A big detail can feel surprising, but it still points the same way.

The semicolon version works the same. In "The road was closed for weeks; ______ the detour added two hours to every trip," the clause after the semicolon is the stronger detail, so *in fact* fits.

If you cannot decide, imagine sentence 2 without the transition. Does it read as "even more so" (*In fact*), "put simply" (*In other words*) or "another thing" (*Moreover*)?
```

# Kinds of contrast
However, By contrast, Instead: same bucket, different jobs.

```visual
transitions--contrast-kinds
```

## Key ideas
All of these are in the *but* bucket, but each one says something different.

- **Still, Nevertheless, Even so, Regardless**: "despite that." Sentence 2 holds true against what you would expect from sentence 1.
- **However**: the general-purpose turn against the previous idea.
- **By contrast, In contrast, Conversely, On the other hand**: two different things placed side by side.
- **Instead, Rather**: the real thing takes the place of the expected one.
- **On the contrary**: flatly rejects the previous claim.

Two choices from the same group cannot both be right. Decide which kind of contrast the passage shows.

```example
The film got terrible reviews. ______ it earned more than any other release that summer.
Choices: Still, / By contrast, / Instead,
1. Idea 1: terrible reviews :: Expect low sales.
2. Idea 2: top earnings :: The opposite happened.
3. One film, success despite reviews :: Side by side, replacing, or despite?
4. Answer: Still, :: It holds despite the reviews.
```

```theory
The SAT uses several kinds of contrast and checks whether you can tell them apart.

**Despite-contrast** (*Still*, *Nevertheless*, *Nonetheless*, *Even so*, *Regardless*, *All the same*). Sentence 1 sets up an expectation, and sentence 2 happens anyway. "The recipe looked ordinary. *Still*, it won the cooking contest."

**Side-by-side contrast** (*By contrast*, *In contrast*, *Conversely*, *On the other hand*, *In comparison*). Two different subjects, cases or sides are set next to each other: one village finished its bridge in a week, while the next village took a year. There must be two things being compared, each on the same feature.

**Replacement** (*Instead*, *Rather*, *Alternatively*). What was expected or what was assumed is replaced by what is actually true. "Researchers suspected the spiders were aggressive. *Instead*, they found them gentle." *Alternatively* offers another option rather than a replacement.

**Rejection** (*On the contrary*). Sentence 2 directly denies sentence 1.

*However* is the neutral choice and often the right one when no more specific word is offered. If the choices contain several contrast words, the specific meaning decides.

*Meanwhile* means "at the same time" and often works as a soft *however*, showing a parallel situation elsewhere.

Be careful with a contrast word that is wrong because the text only adds a small qualification or agrees. Then you are in the next part, concession, or back in the *and* bucket.
```

## Easy
```task
bank: rw-0572
highlight:
- "isolate a relatively pure sample of selenium in 1817" :: Selenium: isolated the same year it was discovered.
- "the isolation process took longer for molybdenum" :: Molybdenum: it took longer. Two elements, opposite results.

Two elements are compared. Selenium was isolated in 1817, the same year it was discovered. For molybdenum, the process took three more years.
```

# Concession
Admit a point, then turn back.

```visual
transitions--concession
```

## Key ideas
In a **concession**, the writer admits a point that seems to weaken their own claim, then turns back to the main claim. Two sentences can play the two roles:

- **The admission** (the writer gives a little): *Admittedly*, *Granted*, *To be sure*, *Of course*.
- **The turn back** (the writer returns to the point): *Still*, *Even so*, *That said*, *Nevertheless*.

Look at the sentence **after** the blank. If it starts with *Still* or *But*, the blank is probably the admission. If the sentence before admitted something, the blank is the turn back.

Both roles are in the *but* bucket, because the whole move goes against a point the writer just made.

```example
Locals call Bean & Leaf the best café in town. ______ it is small. Still, its coffee beats every rival's.
Choices: Admittedly, / Therefore, / Similarly,
1. Sentence before: praise :: The claim: the best café.
2. Sentence after: "Still, its coffee beats…" :: It turns back, so the blank admits.
3. "It is small" is a weakness :: The blank gives a little.
4. Answer: Admittedly, :: Granted would work too.
```

```theory
Concession is the SAT's favorite way to test whether you follow an argument. A writer who wants to be fair names a limit or counterpoint before returning to the main idea.

**Admission words**: *Admittedly*, *Granted*, *To be sure*, *Of course*, *It is true that*. They mean "I agree this is true, but...". They almost always come with a turn-back word in the next sentence or the same sentence (*but*, *Still*, *yet*, *even so*).

*Of course* is flexible. It can simply stress that something is obvious, and then the logic is agreement. In a concession, the clue is that the sentence after it turns back, and the sentence before it made a claim that the admitted point limits. Check the surrounding sentences instead of the word alone.

**Turn-back words**: *Still*, *Even so*, *That said*, *Nevertheless*, *Nonetheless*, *All the same*. They follow an admission and return to the main claim: "The painting does not truly show a fourth dimension. *That said*, its many viewpoints suggest what one might look like."

Typical wrong choices:
- *Therefore* or *Hence*, as if the admitted weakness followed from the praise.
- *Furthermore* or *Similarly*, which treat the admission as more of the same.
- *For example*, as if the weakness were a case of the strength.
- A turn-back word placed where the writer is still admitting, or the reverse.

Method: say the passage out loud as "A is true. Yes, B is also true, but A still holds." The sentence that holds "yes, B" is the admission.
```

```task
- Therefore, :: Being steep is not a result of being beautiful.
* Admittedly,
- For example, :: A steep path is not one case of beauty.
- Similarly, :: Steepness goes against the praise. It is not alike.

- "it is steep and rocky in places" :: A weakness. The writer gives a little.
- "Even so," :: The next sentence turns back, so the blank admits.
explain:
Name the link. Sentence 1 praises the trail. The blank sentence says it is steep and rocky, which is a drawback. The following sentence begins with *Even so*, which turns back to the positive side: hikers walk it anyway.

So the blank sentence admits a point against the writer's praise, and the next one turns back. An admission is a concession, and *Admittedly* is the word for it.
```

# Result, purpose and time
Therefore, To that end, Previously: so and when.

```visual
transitions--result-time
```

## Key ideas
**Result (so):** *Therefore*, *Thus*, *Hence*, *Consequently*, *As a result*, *Accordingly*, *For this reason*. Test: "sentence 1, so sentence 2" must make sense, and sentence 1 must really be the cause. *As such* means "because of what it is": "As such, her work refutes the old view."

**Purpose:** *To that end*, *To this end*, *With this in mind*. Sentence 2 is a step taken for the goal in sentence 1.

**Time:** *Previously*, *Earlier*, *Before that* look back. *Later*, *Next*, *Then*, *Afterward*, *Subsequently* look ahead. *Finally* ends a list. *Meanwhile* means at the same time.

Watch for the near-twins. *Subsequently* means "next in time", but *Consequently* means "as a result". Happening later does not make it a result.

```example
Heavy rain had washed out the road to the quarry. ______ the crew rerouted its trucks through the village.
Choices: As a result, / Previously, / By contrast,
1. Idea 1: the road washed out :: A problem.
2. Idea 2: the crew rerouted :: A response.
3. Road out, so reroute :: Result, time or contrast?
4. Answer: As a result, :: The reroute follows from the washout.
```

```theory
Cause-and-effect words sit on the effect side: they point forward from a cause to what followed. Check that sentence 1 really is the cause. The SAT likes to build wrong choices where one event merely comes first or where the second sentence is the cause rather than the effect.

**Result words**: *Therefore*, *Thus*, *Hence*, *Consequently*, *As a result*, *Accordingly*, *For this reason*. Several of them never appear together as choices, because they mean the same. *As such* needs a subject that fits: "The museum is a public institution. *As such*, it must open its doors free on Sundays" means "being what it is, it must."

**Purpose words** link an action to a stated goal. "The council wanted fewer cars downtown. *To that end*, it banned parking on Main Street." The ban was a step toward the goal.

**Time words** place two events on a line. *Previously* and *Earlier* tell what happened before the time of sentence 1 ("The bridge opened in 1950. *Previously*, travelers crossed the river by ferry."). *Later*, *Subsequently*, *Afterward*, *Next*, *Then* move forward, and *Finally* closes a series. *Meanwhile* shows two things at once.

A sentence can also be a conclusion at the end of a passage: *In sum*, *In conclusion*, *Ultimately*, *In short*. Use them only when sentence 2 wraps up the whole passage, not when it adds another point.

Common traps: *Therefore* where the order is only chronological; *Subsequently* where the text means a result; *As a result* where sentence 1 is actually the effect of the later sentence.
```

```task
*In sum* would claim that the course title summarizes sentence 1. *That is* would claim it is a restatement. *In addition* would add an unrelated point.

The answer is **To that end,**.
```

## Key ideas
Some choices are phrases, not single words: *Based on these models,* / *Undermining this explanation,* / *Beyond the simple coining of a term,*.

1. Name the link as usual (and, but, so).
2. Split the phrase into its **relation word** (*Based on*, *Undermining*, *Despite*, *Beyond*) and the **thing it names** (*these models*, *this explanation*).
3. Check each part against the text. The thing it names must be in the sentence before, and the relation must be true.

Every word has meaning, so one false word makes the choice wrong. A phrase can sound fine and still point at the wrong target.

```example
Sandra's team built a model of the river's flow. ______ they predicted the flood would peak on Tuesday.
Choices: Based on this model, / Despite this model, / In addition to this model,
1. Relation: where did the prediction come from? :: Split relation from target.
2. Target "this model" :: It is in sentence 1. Good.
3. The prediction came out of the model :: So, not but.
4. Answer: Based on this model, :: Despite would make it contradict the model.
```

```theory
Phrase transitions are the hardest transition questions because each word can help or hurt. They look like a participial or prepositional phrase followed by a comma: *Confirming this hypothesis,*, *Drawing a similar conclusion,*, *Despite these estimates,*, *Acting on this finding,*.

Use a two-step check.

**Relation.** What does the new sentence do to the old idea? *Confirming* and *Supporting* strengthen it. *Undermining*, *Contradicting* and *Contrary to* weaken it. *Based on* and *Acting on* mean that the new sentence comes from the old one. *Beyond* and *In addition to* add something to it. *Despite* admits an obstacle.

**Target.** What is named? *This hypothesis*, *this explanation*, *these models*, *this phenomenon*. Each of these must be a specific thing in the previous text, and not another one. A choice can pick the right direction but point at the wrong target. For example, a flood was blamed on a broken levee, and then inspectors find the levee held. The finding does not weaken the flood, which is a fact. It weakens the *explanation* of the flood, so *Undermining this explanation* is true, while *Contrary to this event* is not.

Do not choose the phrase that sounds most academic. Choose the one for which every word can be traced back to the passage. If two choices share the same relation but differ in target, the target decides. If they share the target but differ in relation, name the link again.
```

## Easy
```task
A city engineer compared two designs for a footbridge. Tests showed that the steel design carried twice the weight of the wooden one. ______ the city chose the steel design for the new park.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?
- Despite these test results, :: The choice agrees with the tests. Nothing goes against them.
- Contrary to these test results, :: The city followed the tests, not the opposite.
* Acting on these test results,
- In addition to these test results, :: Choosing a design is not one more test.
highlight:
- "the steel design carried twice the weight of the wooden one" :: The test result: steel is stronger.
- "the city chose the steel design" :: The city followed the result.
explain:
Name the link first. Sentence 2 gives a test result: steel carried twice the weight of wood. The blank sentence says the city chose steel. A decision that follows from evidence is a **so** link.

Now check the phrase. *Acting on these test results* says the city used the results to decide, and the results are in sentence 2, so both the relation and the target are true.

*Despite* and *Contrary to* would mean the city chose steel against the results, but steel is exactly what the results favor. *In addition to* treats the choice as one more test, which it is not.

The answer is **Acting on these test results,**.
```
