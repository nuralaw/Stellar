# Claim first
Read the claim, then find it in the table.

## True and on target

The right choice is **true in the data** and **proves the claim**.

| Season | Urban foxes | Rural foxes |
|---|---|---|
| Winter | 4.1 | 6.3 |
| Summer | 4.8 | 6.1 |

```example
The table shows foxes' daily travel in km. A researcher says urban foxes travel less than rural foxes in both seasons: ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?
Choices: Urban foxes traveled 4.8 km in summer. / Rural foxes traveled 6.3 km in winter and 6.1 km in summer. / Urban foxes traveled less than rural foxes in winter (4.1 versus 6.3) and summer (4.8 versus 6.1).
1. Claim: urban less than rural, both seasons :: Two seasons, one comparison.
2. (A) urban only, (B) rural only :: Nothing is compared. Cross out.
3. Answer: (C) :: Both seasons, both groups.
```

```more
Read a table like a map. The first column names the rows, and the top row names the columns. Find the cell where the claim's row meets its column. The title or the sentence above the table gives the unit, here km per day.
```

## Cut what misses

| Year | Walked | Biked |
|---|---|---|
| 2010 | 22% | 30% |
| 2020 | 35% | 25% |

```quiz
The table shows how Lake County students got to school. A student claims that in 2020, walking was more common than biking.
Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the claim?
* In 2020, 35% walked and 25% biked.
- In 2010, 22% walked and 30% biked. :: Wrong year. The claim is about 2020.
- From 2010 to 2020, walking rose from 22% to 35%. :: Walking alone cannot show that it beat biking.
> The claim compares walking with biking in 2020. Only one choice gives both, for that year.
```

# Count or share
Different group sizes? Compare percent, not counts.

## Compare shares

A **share** is part ÷ whole. Different group sizes? Compare shares.

| School | Students | In band |
|---|---|---|
| East | 100 | 30 |
| West | 200 | 36 |

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--count-share
```

```example
The table shows how many students at two schools joined band. A teacher claims that a larger share of East students than West students joined band: ______ Which choice completes the statement with data from the table?
Choices: West had more band students than East, 36 versus 30. / Band had 30 of East's 100 students but 36 of West's 200 students.
1. Share = band ÷ students :: Not just the band count.
2. East 30 ÷ 100 = 30%; West 36 ÷ 200 = 18% :: East's share is larger.
3. Answer: (B) :: It gives both totals, so shares can be compared.
```

```more
Read each row. East: 100 students, 30 in band, so 30 ÷ 100 = 30%. West: 200 students, 36 in band, so 36 ÷ 200 = 18%. Choice A is true, but it compares counts. West is a bigger school, so more band students does not mean a larger share.
```

## Count or share yourself

| Club | Members | Seniors |
|---|---|---|
| Chess | 40 | 10 |
| Drama | 25 | 10 |

```quiz
A student claims that a larger share of Drama's members than Chess's members are seniors.
Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the claim?
- Chess and Drama each have 10 seniors. :: Equal counts do not show a larger share.
- Chess has more members than Drama, 40 to 25. :: True, but it does not give the seniors.
* Seniors are 10 of Drama's 25 members and 10 of Chess's 40 members.
> Drama: 10 ÷ 25 = 40%. Chess: 10 ÷ 40 = 25%. Only one choice gives both totals and both counts.
```

# Two-part claims
A claim with "and" needs both parts proved.

## Prove both parts

A claim with *and* has two parts. The choice must prove both.

| Model | Battery (hours) | Weight (g) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 14 | 180 |
| B | 9 | 210 |

```example
A reviewer says Model A lasts longer and weighs less than Model B: ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?
Choices: Model A ran 14 hours; Model B ran 9. / Model A ran 14 hours and weighs 180 g. / Model A ran 14 hours and weighs 180 g; Model B ran 9 hours and weighs 210 g.
1. (A) battery only :: Half the claim.
2. (B) Model B is missing :: Nothing to compare.
3. Answer: (C) :: Longer and lighter. Both proved.
```

```more
Break the claim at *and*: part 1 is "lasts longer", part 2 is "weighs less". Then check each part against the table. Battery (14 > 9) is part 1. Weight (180 < 210) is part 2. A choice that gives one part cannot prove both.
```

## Both or nothing

| Rock | Temp (°C) | Water lost |
|---|---|---|
| White | 31 | 4% |
| Black | 41 | 11% |

```quiz
Snails were placed on white rocks and on black rocks. The hypothesis is that snails on lighter rocks stay cooler and lose less water than snails on darker rocks.
Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the hypothesis?
* White-rock snails were cooler and lost less water than black-rock snails.
- Black-rock snails were hotter than white-rock snails, 41°C versus 31°C. :: True, but only temperature. Half the hypothesis.
- White-rock snails were 31°C, and black-rock snails lost 11% of their water. :: Two different measures. Nothing is compared.
> Both parts hold: cooler (31°C < 41°C) and less water lost (4% < 11%).
```

# Read the trend
On a line graph, read the shape: gap, peak, crossing.

## Read the lines

A **line graph** shows change over time. Check every point the claim names.

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--line-trend
```

```example
The graph shows daily visitors at two museum sites. A guide says Site X overtook Site Y: ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to complete the statement?
Choices: Site X had 90 visitors in 2022 and 40 in 2019. / Site Y led in 2020 (60 to 50), but Site X led in 2021 (70 to 60). / Both sites gained visitors from 2019 to 2022.
1. "Overtook" = below first, above later :: Look for a crossing.
2. The lines cross between 2020 and 2021 :: Read both years.
3. Answer: (B) :: It shows the switch.
```

```more
The graph has a **legend** or labels that say which line is which. Read the lines' labels first. Then ask what the claim needs: a gap that grows or shrinks, a peak (the highest point), or a crossing (one line passes the other). Choice A is true but ignores Site Y. Choice C is false: Site Y fell from 60 to 50.
```

## Compare growth

| Year | Library A | Library B |
|---|---|---|
| 2019 | 30 | 20 |
| 2020 | 34 | 29 |
| 2021 | 37 | 39 |

```quiz
A student claims that Library B's loans grew faster than Library A's from 2019 to 2021.
Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the claim?
- In 2021, Library B had 39 loans, its highest total. :: True, but it says nothing about Library A.
- In 2019, Library A had 10 more loans than Library B. :: That is one year. Growth needs two years.
* From 2019 to 2021, Library A rose from 30 to 37, while Library B rose from 20 to 39.
> A grew by 7. B grew by 19. Growth needs the first and last year for both libraries.
```

# Trap: true but useless
Often every choice is true. Only one proves the claim.

## True isn't enough

True numbers can still miss the claim. Ask: do they compare what the claim compares?

```visual
command-of-evidence-quantitative--grouped-bars
```

```example
The graph shows heights of four tomato plots. A gardener concludes that sunlight, not watering, explains the height differences: ______ Which choice most effectively uses data from the graph to support the conclusion?
Choices: Plot A, with 8 hours of sun, reached 95 cm. / With 8 hours of sun, plants reached 92–95 cm on either watering; with 4 hours, 58–60 cm. / Plot A (95 cm) grew taller than Plot B (92 cm).
1. Same sun, different water: 2–3 cm :: Watering changes little.
2. 8 hours versus 4 hours: about 35 cm :: Sun changes a lot.
3. Answer: (B) :: It gives both comparisons.
```

```more
The claim has two parts: sun matters, watering does not. Choice A is one plot, so nothing is compared. Choice C compares plots that differ only in watering, which is the wrong comparison. Only choice B shows sun making a big difference and watering a small one.
```

## Spot the useful one

| Soil | Cover | Sprouts |
|---|---|---|
| sand | yes | 44 |
| sand | no | 41 |
| clay | yes | 22 |
| clay | no | 19 |

```quiz
Fifty seeds were planted in each plot. A student concludes that soil type, not the cover, explains the difference in sprouts.
Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to support the conclusion?
- The plot in sand with a cover had the most sprouts, 44. :: True, but one plot compares nothing.
- Covered sand sprouted 44 seeds; uncovered sand, 41. :: Trap: that compares the cover, not the soil.
* In sand, 41–44 seeds sprouted with or without the cover; in clay, 19–22.
> Same soil, different cover: a 3-seed change. Sand against clay: about a 22-seed change.
```

# Weaken a claim
Flip the job: true data against the claim.

## Flip the job

To weaken a claim, pick true data that goes against it.

| Class | Average score |
|---|---|
| With music | 78 |
| No music | 81 |

```example
A teacher claims that background music raises scores. Which choice weakens the claim?
1. Claim predicts: music class scores higher :: Say what the claim expects.
2. Data: 78 with music, 81 without :: The opposite happened.
3. "The music class averaged 78, below the other's 81" :: True and against the claim.
```

```more
**Weaken** is the opposite job from **support**. Ask: what would the claim expect to see? Then choose the true data that shows something else. Always reread the verb in the question before you choose.
```

## Weaken it yourself

| City | Share who bike |
|---|---|
| Alton | 9% |
| Brisk | 15% |
| Dunmore | 12% |

```quiz
The table shows the share of workers who bike to work in three cities. An official says that Dunmore's new bike lanes explain why it has the highest rate of the three cities.
Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to weaken the official's claim?
* Brisk's rate (15%) is higher than Dunmore's (12%).
- Dunmore's rate (12%) is higher than Alton's (9%). :: True, but that supports the claim.
- Brisk's rate (15%) is higher than Alton's (9%). :: True, but Dunmore is not in it.
> "Highest" fails if any city beats Dunmore. Brisk does, 15% to 12%.
```
