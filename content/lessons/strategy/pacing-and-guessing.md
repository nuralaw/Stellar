## Spend time where points are

*Pacing* means using your time where it earns the most points. Your average budget:

| Section | Per module | Average |
|---|---|---|
| Reading and Writing | 27 questions, 32 min | about 71 s |
| Math | 22 questions, 35 min | about 95 s |

An average isn't a rule. Some questions take 30 seconds, others 3 minutes. A good plan spends time **unevenly**: quick on the easy ones, more on the hard ones, and never so long on one question that easier points go unanswered.

```quiz
Your Math plan: exactly 95 seconds on every question. What's the problem with it?
- None: 95 seconds is the average, so it fits perfectly :: It fits the clock, but it ignores the order. Math runs from easier to harder.
- You'd run out of time before question 22 :: 22 × 95 seconds is about 34.8 minutes, so it fits. The problem is where the time goes.
* The hardest questions, at the end, get no more time than the easy ones :: Right. Save time on the early questions so the last ones get about 2 minutes each.
> Math modules go from easier to harder. An even split gives easy questions time they don't need and leaves the hardest ones short.
```

## Minutes stay in their module

Spare minutes can't move to the next module, so they only help **inside** the one you're in. And module 1 decides which module 2 you get. So in module 1:

- Work carefully on the easy and medium questions. Careless misses there drag down your result.
- Don't save energy for module 2.
- If you finish early, recheck your least-sure answers.

```quiz
You finish R&W module 1 with 5 minutes left. What's the best use of them?
- Click Next to start module 2 sooner :: With standard time you can't leave early, and the 5 minutes wouldn't carry over anyway.
- Change a few answers that now feel wrong :: Change an answer only for a real reason, like a misread word or a math slip, not a feeling.
* Recheck your flagged and least-sure questions :: Right. Spare minutes only help this module, and module 1 decides your module 2.
> Start with the questions you narrowed to two choices. A fresh read often shows which one the text really supports.
```

## Reading and Writing: two speeds

Each R&W module has two blocks:

- **Reading block**, roughly the first 13 to 17 questions: vocabulary, purpose, paired texts, main idea, evidence, graphs. Up to about 90 seconds each.
- **Writing block**, the rest: grammar, transitions, student notes. Often 30 to 60 seconds each.

Our plan: about 20 minutes for reading, 9 for writing, 3 to review. Check the timer at these points, not after every question:

| Timer shows | You should be |
|---|---|
| about 22:00 | around question 10 |
| about 12:00 | starting the grammar questions |
| about 3:00 | all answered; back to flags |

```quiz
R&W, 9 minutes left. You just finished question 15, and questions 16 to 27 are grammar, transitions and notes. You feel behind. What now?
- Go back and recheck questions 1 to 15 first :: Right idea, wrong time. Recheck at the end, once every question has an answer.
* Keep a steady pace of about 45 seconds each :: Right. 9 minutes for 12 fast questions is tight but doable. Guess and flag anything that stalls.
- Slow down and make each one perfect :: Then the last few questions get no time at all and end up as blind guesses.
> By the plan you'd start grammar at about 12:00, so you're 3 minutes behind and your review time is gone. 9 minutes ÷ 12 questions = 45 seconds each. Writing questions are quick: see what differs between the choices, apply the rule, move on.
```

## Math: bank time early

Math questions get harder through the module, so **bank time early** for the end. A plan:

| Questions | Budget | Timer when done |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 8 (easier) | about 8 min | about 27:00 |
| 9 to 15 (medium) | about 10 min | about 17:00 |
| 16 to 22 (harder) | about 14 min | about 3:00 |

The last 3 minutes are for review. Bank time by knowing the methods, not by rushing: a careless slip on question 3 throws away a point you already had.

```quiz
Math module, the timer shows 17:00, and you're just starting question 12. By the plan, question 15 should be done by now. What do you do?
- Keep your pace and catch up on the last questions :: The last questions are the slowest. There's no time to win back there.
- Jump to question 22 and work backward :: That skips medium questions, which are quicker points than the hard ones at the end.
* Speed up on questions 12 to 15 by guessing and flagging anything without a plan :: Right. Catch up on the medium questions so the hard ones still get their time.
> In Math you can't catch up at the end, because the end is the hardest part. Win the time back now, on questions you can do quickly.
```

## Know when to let go

Two rules stop one question from eating your time:

- **R&W, the 90-second rule:** after about 90 seconds, if you can't narrow it to two choices, guess, flag and move on.
- **Math, the 30-second rule:** after about 30 seconds, if you have no idea how to start, guess, flag and move on. With a plan, a late, hard question can take 2 to 3 minutes.

In R&W, stuck on the last question of a type? The next one starts a new type, so it's probably quicker.

```quiz
Math module 1, question 7. You've worked for 2 minutes, filled half your scratch paper and still have no answer. 26 minutes are left. What now?
- Keep going: question 7 should be easy, so you're close :: Being stuck this long on an early question often means a misread. Fresh eyes later help more.
- Leave it blank, flag it and come back later :: If time runs out, a blank is a sure zero. Enter a guess before you flag.
* Rule out what you can, guess, flag and move on :: Right. You've spent twice an early question's budget, and the hard questions at the end need that time.
> When you come back after question 22, reread the question from its first line, as if it were new. A detail you missed the first time often jumps out.
```

## Two passes through every module

1. **Pass 1:** go in order and answer everything you can. Past your time limit? Enter your best guess, flag it with **Mark for Review** and move on.
2. **Pass 2:** spend the time you saved on the flagged questions. Start with the ones you narrowed to two choices: they're the likeliest to turn into points.

Always enter an answer **before** you flag. You might not get back.

> **Key idea:** Skipping isn't giving up. It moves time from a question you're stuck on to questions you can actually get.

```quiz
4 minutes left, and three flagged questions have guesses entered. On A you were down to two choices. On B you had no idea how to start. C needs a method you know, but the calculation takes at least 4 minutes. Which do you open first?
* A, the two-choice question :: Right. A quick second look may settle it, and then C gets whatever time is left.
- B, the no-idea question :: With no plan, it's the least likely to become a point.
- C, the long calculation :: It needs all 4 minutes or more, so it may not finish, and A never gets its quick second look.
> Go from closest-to-a-point to furthest. A is a 50/50 that a short reread can decide; B is the last thing to touch.
```

## Guess smart: eliminate first

Every choice you cross out raises your odds:

| Choices left | Chance of a right guess |
|---|---|
| 4 | 1 in 4 (25%) |
| 3 | 1 in 3 (33%) |
| 2 | 1 in 2 (50%) |

Quick ways to cross out choices:

- **R&W:** off topic, too extreme (*always*, *proves*), says more than the text supports, or shows the wrong relationship.
- **Math:** wrong sign, impossible size (a negative length, a probability above 1), wrong units. Or plug a choice into the question.

```quiz
No time to solve a probability question. The choices are −0.2, 0.35, 0.6 and 1.4. What's your best move?
- Pick any choice at random :: That's a 1-in-4 chance, and two of these choices can't be probabilities at all.
* Cross out −0.2 and 1.4, then pick 0.35 or 0.6 :: Right. A probability is between 0 and 1, so your odds double to 1 in 2.
- Leave it blank and hope to come back :: If time runs out, a blank is a sure zero.
> Ten seconds of elimination doubled your chances. Use the Option Eliminator so you don't reread dead choices.
```

## Never leave a blank

- **Typed math answers:** enter your best estimate. An empty box can't earn credit.
- **Out of time on several questions:** enter the same letter on every remaining multiple-choice question. College Board says random guessing is unlikely to raise your score much, so this is a last-seconds tool, not a plan. It still beats a blank.

```quiz
R&W module 2, 30 seconds left. Three questions still have no answer. What should you do?
- Read the first one carefully and answer just that :: That leaves two blanks, and a blank is a sure zero.
* Enter the same letter on all three, then use any seconds left on one :: Right. Filling them takes a few seconds, and wrong answers cost nothing.
- Leave them blank, since random guesses can lower your score :: There's no penalty for wrong answers. College Board says a guess beats a blank.
> With seconds left, an answer on every question comes first. Then spend whatever time remains improving one of them.
```

## The last two minutes

1. Open the **question menu**. Anything blank? Answer it now.
2. Go to the flagged questions you narrowed to two choices, and decide.
3. In Math, check the preview of each typed answer.
4. Don't start a long new problem in the final minute.

```quiz
Math module 2, 3 minutes left. Questions 21 and 22 are untouched, and flagged questions 12 and 18 have no answer entered. What do you do first?
- Solve question 21 carefully, then move on :: That can use all 3 minutes and leave three blanks.
* Put a guess on all four, then spend the rest on the one you're likeliest to solve :: Right. About 20 seconds turns four sure zeros into four chances.
- Go back to question 12, since earlier questions are easier :: Fill the blanks first. Then pick the quickest real solve, wherever it is.
> On multiple choice any letter will do; on a typed answer, enter a sensible estimate. Then use the 2-plus minutes left on the single question you're most likely to get right.
```

## Practice the plan

- Take full Bluebook practice tests under real conditions: one sitting, real timing, a 10-minute break, phone away. Practice tests let you move on early; don't.
- After each test, review every question you missed, guessed or spent too long on. Find your slow question types and drill them with a timer.
- Often run out of time in R&W? On a practice test, try the writing block first (grammar, transitions, notes), then go back to question 1.

```quiz
On your last practice test you ran out of time in R&W module 2 and guessed on the quick questions at the end. A friend suggests doing the writing block first. When should you use that order on the real SAT?
- Right away: it can't hurt :: It changes your whole rhythm. Test it under real timing first.
* Only after it raises your score on a practice test :: Right. Keep a new order only if your practice results improve.
- Never: you must answer in order :: You can move freely inside a module, so any order is allowed.
> Doing the writing block first protects its quick points, but you have to find your way back to question 1 and keep track of time. Prove it on a practice test before trusting it.
```

## Recap

- Spend time unevenly: quick on easy questions, more on hard ones, never stuck on one.
- R&W plan: question 10 by about 22:00, grammar by 12:00, all answered by 3:00.
- Math plan: question 8 done by about 27:00, question 15 by 17:00, all done by 3:00.
- Stuck? About 90 seconds in R&W, or 30 seconds with no plan in Math: guess, flag, move on.
- Eliminate before you guess, and always enter an answer before you flag.
- Last two minutes: fill every blank first, then decide the flagged two-choice questions.
