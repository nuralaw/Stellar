## What this question looks like

Words in Context questions open every Reading and Writing module. You get one short text (often just two or three sentences, never more than about 150 words) and one of two questions:

- **Fill the blank** (the most common version). The text has a blank (______), and the question asks: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?"
- **Meaning of a word.** A word in the text is quoted, and the question asks, for example: *As used in the text, what does the word "reserve" most nearly mean?* This version often comes with an older literary text, and it usually tests a less common meaning of an everyday word.

The four choices are real words or short phrases (like *attest to*), and they usually all fit the grammar of the sentence. So "it sounds fine" proves nothing. The real question is: which word does the passage *prove* belongs here?

**How many?** Craft and Structure is 13–15 of the 50 scored Reading and Writing questions (the section has 54, but 4 are unscored tryout questions), and Words in Context is the biggest part of it: roughly 8–11 per test (counts vary from test to test). Within the skill, questions go from easier to harder, so the last ones in a module use rarer words and harder texts.

**Sub-types:** one-word blanks; phrase blanks, where the small word matters (*comparable to*, *confined to*); "most nearly means" questions, where an easy word has a second meaning; and harder versions where the clue is far from the blank, two clues must be combined, or a negative (*not*, *far from*) flips the meaning.

> **Key idea:** This is a reading question first and a vocabulary question second. The passage always contains a clue that tells you what the blank must mean. Find the clue, and even a list of hard words becomes easy to sort.

These are the fastest questions on the test. Aim for about 45 seconds each and save the extra time for longer questions later in the module.

## The method

1. **Don't read the choices yet.** Read the whole text first. If you look at the choices first, the most impressive-sounding word tends to pull you in.
2. **Find the clue.** Ask: "What does the text tell me about the blank?" The clue is very often *after* the blank: after a colon or a dash, in a phrase like "that is", in the next sentence, or in a noun such as "this caution" that names the blank's idea again. Also mark signal words: *but, however, although* (contrast), *because, so, as a result* (cause), *for example* (example), *not, far from* (negation).
3. **Predict in plain words.** Say a simple word or short phrase that would fit: "necessary", "doubtful", "made worse". It doesn't have to be fancy. If no word comes, decide the **direction**: positive or negative? more or less? supporting or opposing? certain or uncertain?
4. **Match and eliminate.** Now read the choices and cross out every one that doesn't match your prediction. Start with the words you know. Compare three things: meaning, charge (positive, negative or neutral) and strength (mild or extreme).
5. **Check the fit.** Put your answer in the blank and reread the whole text. The word must work with the words around it: who is doing what to what? For a phrase, check the small word too.

For **"most nearly means"** questions, use the same routine. Cover the quoted word in your mind, treat it as a blank, predict a meaning from the clues, and then match. Never choose the meaning you know best just because you know it best.

**When you don't know some of the choices:**

- Eliminate the words you *do* know first. If three of them clearly fail, the unknown word is the answer, even if you have never seen it before.
- Don't pick a word because it is hard. One count of every Words in Context question in eight official practice tests found that the hardest-looking choice was right only about 27% of the time, about as often as a blind guess. Most right answers were ordinary words.
- Use the parts you recognize. *Un-, in-, im-, a-, dis-* and *non-* often mean "not" (*atypical* = not typical). *-able* and *-ible* mean "can be" (*perceptible* = can be noticed). A word you know inside a new one can help: *negligible* is related to *neglect*. Treat these as hints, not proof.
- Even the charge can be enough. If a word sounds clearly negative and the blank needs something positive, cross it out.
- Never leave a question blank: a wrong answer costs nothing. If you're stuck after about a minute, pick your best option, flag it and move on.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Clue types

| Clue | Signal words | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Restatement | colon, dash, "that is", "in other words", *this* or *such* + noun | The text says the blank's meaning again in other words, often right after the blank |
| Contrast | but, however, yet, although, while, despite, instead, rather than, unlike | The blank goes the opposite way from the other idea |
| Cause and effect | because, since, so, therefore, as a result, thus | The blank must make sense as a cause or a result of the other idea |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, including | The examples show the general quality the blank names |
| Continuation | and, also, similarly, moreover, in fact | The blank keeps going in the same direction (*in fact* can also make it stronger) |
| Negation | not, never, no longer, far from, by no means, hardly | Work out what is true, then flip it: "far from ______" needs the opposite of what is true |

> **Tip:** The test writers love the pattern "a ______ thing: [explanation]". When a colon or dash follows the blank, the answer is almost always defined right there in plain words.

### Charge and strength

Words that mean almost the same thing can still differ in two ways.

**Charge** (also called connotation) is the feeling a word carries. *Frugal* (careful with money, a compliment) and *stingy* (unwilling to share, an insult) describe similar behavior. So do *confident* and *arrogant*, or *curious* and *nosy*. Match the feeling of the text.

**Strength** is how extreme a word is. Line related words up from weak to strong and pick the strength the text supports:

| Idea | Weaker → stronger |
|---|---|
| Showing something is true | suggest → indicate → support → demonstrate → prove |
| Disagreeing | question → doubt → challenge → reject → refute |
| Being small | modest → slight → marginal → negligible |
| Disapproving | have doubts about → criticize → condemn |
| Being sure | speculative → tentative → likely → definitive |

Hedge words in the text (*may, might, some, slightly, tends to, only*) are a signal: the answer should be mild too.

### Precise means "fits its partners"

A word can match the general idea and still be wrong because it doesn't fit the words it connects. Ask who or what is doing the action, and to what. A survey can *reveal* a trend but cannot *cause* one; analysts can *predict* growth but cannot *produce* it. Official answer explanations reject many choices exactly this way: they "wouldn't make sense" with their subject or object.

**Phrases.** In one count of the official practice tests, almost a quarter of the right answers were phrases, not single words. The small word is part of the meaning:

| Phrase | Meaning |
|---|---|
| attest to | be clear evidence of |
| account for | explain; also, make up a share of ("rice accounts for half the harvest") |
| comparable to | similar to |
| confined to | found only in; kept inside |
| prevail over | win against, defeat |
| at odds with | in conflict with, disagreeing with |
| in keeping with | matching, consistent with |
| give rise to | cause, lead to |
| depart from | differ from, move away from (a habit, a plan, a tradition) |
| contingent on | depending on something that may or may not happen |

|---|---|---|---|

| novel | a long story | new and original | a novel way to store solar energy |
| temper | anger | soften, make less extreme | He tempered his criticism with kind words. |
| check | look at | stop or slow | New rules checked the spread of the disease. |
| yield | give way | produce | The experiment yielded surprising results. |
| reserve | book a seat | restraint, holding back feelings | She answered with polite reserve. |

### High-value word list

Most right answers are ordinary words, but the harder questions do use words like these. We picked them from analyses of the official practice tests and College Board's question bank and from students' reports after recent tests, where they appear as right answers, as tempting wrong answers, or in the texts themselves. They are grouped by meaning because the test often puts words from one group side by side. Learn each word in the sense given here and read the example out loud.

**Support or prove**

| Word | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| corroborate | confirm with more evidence | A second lab corroborated the first team's results. |
| bolster | strengthen, support | The new data bolster her argument. |
| buttress | support, reinforce (an idea or a claim) | He buttressed his case with old tax records. |
| substantiate | show with evidence that something is true | No one could substantiate the rumor. |
| attest to | be clear evidence of | The worn stone steps attest to centuries of visitors. |
| vindicate | show that someone was right after doubt | The discovery vindicated the scientist her colleagues had mocked. |
| underscore | emphasize, show how important something is | The drought underscored the need to save water. |

**Weaken, block or remove**

| Word | Plain meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|

## Worked examples

The texts below were written for this lesson. Some of the people and studies in them are invented for practice.

### Example 1: The clue comes after the blank

Marine biologists describe sea otters as ______ to the health of many kelp forests along the Pacific coast. Where otters disappear, the sea urchins they normally eat multiply quickly and chew through the kelp until little of the forest remains.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) detrimental
- (B) comparable
- (C) integral
- (D) incidental

**Solution.** The clue is the whole second sentence, a cause and effect: without otters, the forest is destroyed. So otters are needed for the forest to stay healthy. Prediction: "necessary" or "essential".

- (A) Detrimental means harmful. That reverses the logic: the text shows otters protect the kelp.
- (B) Comparable to means similar to. Otters aren't "similar to the health" of a forest, so the word doesn't fit its partner.
- (C) Integral means essential, a necessary part of the whole. It matches.
- (D) Incidental means minor, happening only on the side. The second sentence shows the opposite.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: A colon plus a contrast

When the city first proposed a bike-sharing program, many local reporters were ______: they predicted that few residents would ride the bikes and that most of the bikes would soon be stolen. Two years after the program launched, however, riders were taking three times as many trips as planners had forecast, and fewer than 1 percent of the bikes had gone missing.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) skeptical
- (B) sanguine
- (C) ambivalent
- (D) meticulous

**Solution.** There are two clues. The colon restates the blank: the reporters predicted failure. Then "however" sets their view against what really happened, a success. So the reporters doubted the program. Prediction: "doubtful".

- (A) Skeptical means doubting, not easily convinced. It matches.
- (B) Sanguine means hopeful. That's the reverse of predicting failure.
- (C) Ambivalent means having mixed feelings, both for and against. The reporters had only doubts; the text gives them no positive feelings at all.
- (D) Meticulous means very careful about details. Nothing in the text is about care or detail.

Even if sanguine and ambivalent were new to you, skeptical matches the prediction and meticulous clearly fails, so (A) is safe.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 3: A phrase, where the small word matters

Archaeologists once assumed that farms in the remote valleys of Viking Age Iceland were cut off from the wider world, producing almost everything they used. Excavations at several of these farms, however, have uncovered glass beads, silver coins, and soapstone bowls carried from overseas, objects that ______ regular trade with distant markets.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) account for
- (B) conform to
- (C) depart from
- (D) attest to

**Solution.** The clue: objects brought from overseas turned up on the farms, and "however" says this goes against the old view that the farms were isolated. Imported objects are evidence that the farms traded. Prediction: "are evidence of".

- (A) Account for means explain. It turns the logic around: trade would explain the objects, but the objects don't explain the trade.
- (B) Conform to means follow (a rule or a pattern). Objects can't follow a trade.
- (C) Depart from means differ from. The objects do differ from the archaeologists' old assumption, but the blank must connect the objects to "regular trade", and they support that trade instead of differing from it. This trap fits a nearby idea, not the blank's partner.
- (D) Attest to means be clear evidence of. It matches.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 4: An easy word with a second meaning

The following text is from a story.

The villagers greeted the new schoolmaster politely, but with a certain reserve. They had watched three teachers arrive full of promises and leave before the first snow, and they had learned to keep their hopes to themselves. Only after he spent his first free Saturday mending the schoolhouse roof did a few of them begin to speak to him as a neighbor.

As used in the text, what does the word "reserve" most nearly mean?

- (A) stockpile
- (B) restraint
- (C) sanctuary
- (D) substitute

**Solution.** Treat "reserve" as a blank: "politely, but with a certain ______". "But" sets it against politeness, so it is something cooler. "Keep their hopes to themselves" restates it, and the last sentence shows the villagers only warmed up later. Prediction: "holding back" or "coolness".

- (A) Stockpile is a real meaning of reserve (oil reserves), but no one greets a person with a supply of goods.
- (B) Restraint means holding back your feelings. It matches.
- (C) Sanctuary matches a nature reserve, a protected area. It has nothing to do with how people greet someone.
- (D) Substitute matches a reserve player waiting on the bench. Wrong context.

All four are real meanings of "reserve"; only one fits these villagers. That is exactly what this question type tests.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 5: A negative flips the meaning

The rapid color flashes of one species of reef fish had been recorded only in laboratory tanks under bright lamps, so some researchers suspected that the flashes were just reflections of the lamps on shiny scales, not real color changes. But when a team filmed the same species on an open reef in natural light, they saw identical flashes during courtship. The flashes, it seems, are far from ______.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) conspicuous
- (B) widespread
- (C) illusory
- (D) deliberate

**Solution.** First decide what is true. The reef videos show the flashes are real color changes, not reflections of lab lamps. Now handle the negative: "far from ______" means "not at all ______", so the blank must be the opposite of "real". Prediction: "fake" or "not real".

- (A) Conspicuous means easy to notice. "Far from conspicuous" would mean the flashes are hard to see, but the question in the text was whether they are real, not how visible they are.
- (B) Widespread means found in many places. The text compares one tank with one reef and says nothing about how common the flashes are.
- (C) Illusory means not real, based on an illusion. "Far from illusory" means definitely real. It matches.
- (D) Deliberate means done on purpose. "Far from deliberate" would mean accidental, which the courtship evidence doesn't suggest and the text never discusses.

If you predicted "real" and forgot about "far from", a positive-sounding word like (A) or (B) would have looked right. Always deal with the negative before you predict.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 6: Getting the strength right

In a survey of 300 farms, farms that planted strips of wildflowers between crop rows reported larger harvests per acre than farms that did not. Even so, the researchers called the connection between wildflowers and harvest size ______, pointing out that the harvest figures were the farmers' own estimates and that the wildflower farms also tended to be larger and better funded.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) definitive
- (B) negligible
- (C) counterintuitive
- (D) tentative

**Solution.** "Even so" tells us the researchers held back from fully trusting their own result. The reasons that follow are reasons for doubt: the numbers were estimates, not measurements, and the wildflower farms differed in other ways that could explain the bigger harvests. Prediction: "uncertain" or "not proven".

- (A) Definitive means final and certain. That's the reverse of a cautious judgment.
- (B) Negligible means so small that it doesn't matter. Like tentative, it sounds cautious, but it is about size, and the text never says the difference was small.
- (C) Counterintuitive means surprising, against common sense. Nothing suggests anyone expected the opposite result.
- (D) Tentative means not certain, open to change. It matches.

Negligible and tentative both sound "weak", which makes this hard. The reasons after "pointing out" decide it: they are about whether the connection is real, not about how big it is.

**Answer:** (D)

## Traps to avoid

- **The reversal.** The choice means the opposite of what the text needs, usually because you missed a *however*, *although* or *far from*. It is the most common wrong answer, so find the direction first and cross out reversals right away.
- **The everyday meaning.** A real meaning of the word, just not the one used here (reserve as "stockpile" in Example 4). Test each meaning against the text, not against your memory.
- **Right idea, wrong strength.** *Proves* when the text only *suggests*, *furious* when someone is only *annoyed*. Hedge words like *may*, *slightly* and *tends to* call for mild answers.
- **Right idea, wrong charge.** Similar meanings with different feelings, like *frugal* and *stingy*. Match the attitude of the text.
- **Wrong partner.** The word fits the topic but not the words it connects: imported objects *attest to* trade but don't *account for* it (Example 3). Ask who does what to what.
- **On topic, no support.** A word that suits the subject (a "debate" word in a history text) but that no clue in the text supports.
- **The surface echo.** A choice that sounds like a word in the text without matching the blank's logic. An echo is not evidence.
- **The fancy-word magnet.** The hardest-sounding choice is right only about as often as a guess. Plain words like *reduced*, *created* and *consistent* are correct all the time.

> **Trap:** Don't stop reading at the blank. On this test the clue very often comes after it: in the next clause, after a colon, or in the next sentence.

## Quick checklist

- Did I read the whole text before looking at the choices?
- Can I point to the clue: a restatement, contrast, cause, example or negative?
- Did I predict a word, or at least a direction (positive or negative, more or less)?
- Does my answer match the text's meaning, charge and strength?
- Does it fit its partners, including the small word in a phrase?
- For "most nearly means": did I pick the meaning used here, not the one I know best?
