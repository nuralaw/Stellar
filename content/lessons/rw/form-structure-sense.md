## The words change shape

The question wording is the same as in Boundaries, but here the **words** change, not the marks. What changes between the choices tells you the rule:

| Choices like | Rule tested |
|---|---|
| *is / are*, *shows / show* | Subject-verb agreement |
| *shows / showing / to show* | Main verb or not |
| *showed / shows / had shown* | Verb tense |
| *its / their*, *city's / cities'* | Pronouns and apostrophes |
| Same words, new order | Opening phrase (modifier) |

Name the rule first. Then you know which word in the text to hunt for.

```quiz
The choices are *its / it's / their / they're*. Which rule is being tested?
- Subject-verb agreement :: That needs verb choices like *is / are*. These are all pronouns.
- Verb tense :: Tense choices are one verb at different times, like *showed / shows / will show*.
* Pronouns and apostrophes :: Right. Check two things: one owner or many, and owning or a contraction.
- Opening phrase (modifier) :: That shows up as long choices with the same words in a new order.
> Pronoun choices send you looking for the noun the pronoun replaces. The apostrophe asks: owning (*its*) or a short form of "it is" (*it's*)?
```

## Find the true subject

A verb matches its **subject**, not the noun nearest to it. On verbs, the *-s* means one: *the bird sings*, *the birds sing*.

To find the subject, cross out the extras:

1. Phrases that start with *of, in, on, with*
2. *Who, which* and *that* clauses
3. Anything between commas or dashes

The noun left in front of the verb is the subject.

```quiz
The collection of old maps and letters that the museum bought last spring ______ new details about how the town began.
- reveal :: Plural. It matches "maps and letters," but those sit inside the *of* phrase.
- have revealed :: Plural. The singular form would be *has revealed*.
* reveals :: Right. Cross out the extras and you get "The collection reveals." One collection, singular verb.
- are revealing :: Plural. *Are* goes with a plural subject.
> Cross out "of old maps and letters" and "that the museum bought last spring." What's left is "The collection ______ new details." A collection is one thing, so the verb is singular.
```

## Tricky subjects

| Subject | Verb |
|---|---|
| *A and B* | plural |
| *A, along with B,* | matches A |
| *each, every, one of the…* | singular |
| *team, company, series, collection* | singular |
| An *-ing* activity: *Measuring ice* | singular |
| *There is / There are* + noun | matches that noun |

Only *and* turns two nouns into a plural subject. *Along with*, *as well as* and *together with* add an extra, not a second subject.

```quiz
The lead scientist, along with two graduate students, ______ the glacier every summer.
* visits :: Right. Bracket "along with two graduate students," and the subject is one scientist.
- visit :: *Along with* doesn't make the subject plural. Only *and* does.
- are visiting :: Plural. The true subject is one scientist.
- have visited :: Plural. The true subject is one scientist.
> "Along with two graduate students" is an extra between commas. Take it out: "The lead scientist visits the glacier every summer."
```

## One main verb per clause

A **main verb** shows time and works with its subject: *reports, reported, has reported, is reporting*. On their own, *reporting*, *to report* and *having reported* can't be one.

Count the main verbs in the clause:

| The clause has… | The blank needs… |
|---|---|
| no main verb yet | a main verb |
| its main verb already | an *-ing*, *-ed* or *to* form |

A second main verb needs *and* to join it: *Rain fell and caused floods.* After a complete clause, *to* + verb means a goal ("in order to"), not a plain result.

```quiz
Heavy rain fell for six days straight, ______ the river to rise above its banks.
- causes :: A second main verb with no *and* to join it. The sentence already has *fell*.
- to cause :: *To* + verb means "in order to," and the rain had no goal.
- caused :: Another main verb after a comma, with nothing joining it to *fell*.
* causing :: Right. "Heavy rain fell" is complete, so the blank starts a phrase that adds the result.
> The clause already has its main verb, *fell*. After the comma, an *-ing* phrase adds what happened as a result.
```

## Match the timeline

Tense follows the **time clues** and the other verbs nearby.

| Clue | Tense |
|---|---|
| *in 1850, last year* | past: *found* |
| *since 2010, so far* | *has / have found* |
| *by 1900*, before another past event | *had found* |
| facts, what a book or study shows | present: *finds* |

No clue? Copy the tense of the verbs around the blank.

```quiz
By the time researchers found the wreck of the *Titanic* in 1985, it ______ on the ocean floor for 73 years.
- rests :: Present tense clashes with a scene set in 1985.
* had rested :: Right. The resting stretched up to another past event, the discovery, so it takes *had*.
- has rested :: *Has rested* runs up to now, so it can't sit beside a finished past moment like 1985.
- will rest :: The future can't describe years that ended in 1985.
> "By the time … in 1985" sets a past moment. An action that ran up to that moment takes *had* + verb.
```

## Pronouns and apostrophes

A pronoun matches the noun it replaces. One company, team, city or museum is **it / its**; several things are *they / their*.

On nouns, an apostrophe shows owning, never a plain plural. On pronouns, owning takes **no** apostrophe:

| Form | Means |
|---|---|
| *scientists* | more than one, owning nothing |
| *scientist's* | one scientist owns something |
| *scientists'* | several scientists own something |
| *its / their* | owned by it / by them |
| *it's / they're* | it is / they are |

```quiz
The city library reopened ______ rooftop garden last week after a year of repairs.
- it's :: *It's* means "it is." "Reopened it is rooftop garden" makes no sense.
- their :: Plural. A library is one thing, even though many people work there.
- they're :: *They're* means "they are," and it's plural. Wrong on both counts.
* its :: Right. One library owns the garden, so the word is singular and possessive, with no apostrophe.
> Two checks. Whose garden? The library's, so one owner. Owning or "it is"? Owning. That gives *its*.
```

## Opening phrase, then its noun

When a sentence opens with a describing phrase and a comma, the noun **right after the comma** must be the thing the phrase describes.

*Built in 1890, engineers still inspect the bridge* says the engineers were built in 1890.

> **Trap:** A possessive doesn't count. In *the author's novel*, the main noun is *novel*, not *author*.

```quiz
Raised in a fishing village on the coast of Chile, ______
- the poet's early work is full of images of the sea. :: The main noun is *work*, not *poet*. Work wasn't raised in a village.
- images of the sea fill the poet's early work. :: This says the images were raised in a fishing village.
* the poet filled her early work with images of the sea. :: Right. The poet was raised there, and *the poet* comes right after the comma.
- there are many images of the sea in the poet's early work. :: *There* isn't a person, so nothing after the comma fits the phrase.
> Ask the phrase: who was raised in the village? The poet. So *the poet* has to be the subject, right after the comma.
```

## Trap: the smooth -ing word

An *-ing* word sounds like action, but it can't run a sentence alone. If the subject still has no main verb, an *-ing* choice leaves a fragment.

Ask: what did the subject do? Some word must answer that as a main verb.

```quiz
In 1928, a scientist working in a London hospital lab ______ that mold had killed the bacteria growing around it.
- noticing :: The smooth *-ing* trap. *Noticing* can't be a main verb, so the sentence has none.
- to notice :: *To* + verb can't run a sentence. The scientist still has no main verb.
* noticed :: Right. "A scientist … noticed" gives the subject its main verb, and "In 1928" points to the past.
- having noticed :: This only describes. The sentence is still left without a main verb.
> *Working* only describes the scientist. The subject still needs a main verb, and *noticed* supplies it.
```

## Recap

- Read the choices first: what changes names the rule.
- Verbs: cross out the extras and match the true subject. *Along with* doesn't make it plural.
- One main verb per clause. None yet: add one. Already there: use *-ing*, *-ed* or *to*, or add *and*.
- Tense follows time clues; an action done before another past moment takes *had*.
- Pronouns: one company or city is *it / its*. Test *it's* by reading "it is."
- After an opening phrase and comma, the next noun is what the phrase describes. A possessive doesn't count.
