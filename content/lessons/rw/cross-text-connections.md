# Compare the claims
Text 2 can agree, add a limit, or disagree.

## Shrink, then compare

Shrink each text to its **claim** (main point). Then ask how Text 2's evidence treats Text 1's claim.

```example
Text 1: Many gardeners say that mulch keeps soil moist.
Text 2: Hassan measured soil in 40 beds. Mulched beds held 30% more water than bare beds.
How would Hassan most likely respond to Text 1?
1. Text 1: mulch keeps soil moist :: Shrink Text 1.
2. Text 2: mulched beds held more water :: Shrink Text 2.
3. Answer: "The gardeners are right." :: His data support the claim.
```

```more
There are three main relationships. Text 2 **supports** the claim (agrees, with evidence). Text 2 **limits** it (*yes, but* only in some cases). Text 2 **opposes** it (disagrees). Do not assume Text 2 always argues against Text 1: read its evidence first.
```

## Add a limit

```quiz
Text 1: Teachers often say that taking notes by hand helps students remember lectures.
Text 2: Ito tested 200 students. Handwritten notes raised scores on questions about ideas. Scores on questions about plain facts stayed the same.
How would Ito most likely respond to Text 1?
- Handwriting helps students remember everything. :: Too broad. Fact scores stayed the same.
- Handwriting does not help memory. :: Too negative. Idea scores went up.
* Handwriting helps, but only with remembering ideas.
> Ito keeps the claim for ideas and drops it for facts: "yes, but".
```

# Text 2 disagrees
Use Text 2's own evidence against Text 1.

## Keeps nothing

Text 2 keeps nothing of the claim? It disagrees. The answer uses Text 2's own evidence.

```example
Text 1: Many gardeners say that talking to plants makes them grow faster.
Text 2: Ruiz grew 50 bean plants in silence and 50 with daily speech. Both groups grew at the same rate.
How would Ruiz respond to Text 1?
1. Text 1: talking speeds growth :: Its claim.
2. Text 2: same growth either way :: Her result.
3. Answer: "Talking likely makes no difference." :: Keeps nothing. Uses her data.
```

```more
"Likely" is important. One experiment with 100 plants does not prove that talking never helps, so a careful author says *likely makes no difference*. Match the answer's strength to the strength of Text 2's evidence.
```

## Offer another cause

```quiz
Text 1: Some biologists say zebras' stripes confuse lions during a hunt.
Text 2: Brooks found that lions catch zebras as often as plain antelope of the same size. Her team also found that biting flies land far less often on striped coats than on plain ones.
How would Brooks most likely respond to Text 1?
- Lions cannot see stripes. :: Brooks never mentions lion eyesight.
- Stripes help zebras escape lions. :: That is Text 1's claim. Her data go against it.
* Stripes probably do something else, such as keep flies away.
> Stripes do not help against lions, but they keep flies off. Brooks offers a different explanation.
```

# A reported view
Text 1's author may only report a view.

## Test the reported view

*Some argue…* reports a view. Do not assume Text 1's author holds it. Test the view against Text 2.

```example
Text 1: Some ecologists argue that old trees speed up the growth of nearby seedlings by sharing nutrients through underground fungi.
Text 2: Ito planted seedlings at 20 sites. They grew at the same rate beside old trees and far from them.
How would Ito most likely describe the view in Text 1?
1. Text 1: a view ("some ecologists argue") :: A claim to test.
2. Text 2: no growth difference :: Ito never mentions fungi.
3. Answer: reasonable, but her results do not support it :: Infer her answer.
```

```more
Text 2 may never mention Text 1's claim. Then ask: if the claim were true, what should Text 2's results show? Choices often have two parts, such as "It seems plausible, but ...". Check each part: is the first part fair, and does Text 2's evidence match the second?
```

## When Text 2 never says

```quiz
Text 1: Ecologists often state a rule: the larger an island, the more species it holds.
Text 2: Yamada counted ant species on 12 Pacific islands and 12 Atlantic islands. On the Pacific islands, larger islands held more species. On the Atlantic islands, size made no difference.
How would Yamada most likely view the rule in Text 1?
- It is false everywhere. :: Too strong. It held on the Pacific islands.
- It holds everywhere, but only for ants. :: The Atlantic ants do not follow it.
* It holds in some places but not in others.
> Yamada's data support the rule on Pacific islands and not on Atlantic ones.
```

# Both agree
Find the one point both texts state.

## Find the overlap

Pick the modest point **each** author states. One author's main claim is a wrong answer.

```visual
cross-text-connections--venn
```

```example
Text 1: Lee says cities should ban cars downtown, because traffic there endangers walkers.
Text 2: Novak opposes a ban, which would hurt shops. She admits downtown traffic endangers walkers and wants lower speed limits.
On what would both authors agree?
1. Lee: ban cars; traffic endangers walkers :: List Lee's points.
2. Novak: no ban; traffic endangers walkers :: List Novak's.
3. Answer: "Downtown traffic endangers walkers." :: It is on both lists.
```

```more
List each author's points in two short lists. The agreement is only the point that appears on both lists. Each author's main claim (Lee: ban cars; Novak: lower speed limits) belongs to one list only, so it is a trap answer.
```

## Spot the shared point

```quiz
Text 1: Coach Ward says young swimmers should train all year, because skills fade during long breaks.
Text 2: Dr. Patel says year-round training wears out shoulders. He advises three months off, even though some skills will slip.
Both authors would most likely agree that
* long breaks cost swimmers some skill.
- young swimmers should train all year. :: Only Ward says this. Patel wants a break.
- year-round training hurts shoulders. :: Only Patel says this. Ward never mentions injuries.
> Ward: skills fade during breaks. Patel: some skills will slip. Both state it.
```

# Text 1 responds
Apply Text 1's rule to Text 2's facts.

## Apply the rule

Sometimes Text 1's author reacts to Text 2. Use the rule Text 1 states, and apply it to Text 2's facts.

```example
Text 1: Chef Okoro holds that using more than five ingredients spoils a dish.
Text 2: A new restaurant's most praised soup contains nineteen ingredients.
How would Okoro respond to the praise?
1. Rule: more than five spoils a dish :: Find Okoro's rule.
2. Fact: the soup has nineteen :: Find Text 2's fact.
3. Answer: "The soup has too many ingredients." :: Rule + fact.
```

```more
The question changes direction. Now Text 1's author is the one who answers. Ask: what rule or opinion does the Text 1 author state? Then apply it to the new fact in Text 2, even if Text 2's author praises it.
```

## Use the rule

```quiz
Text 1: Critic Lund argues that film music should never draw attention to itself.
Text 2: Audiences discuss the loud score of the film *Northern Line* after every screening. Reviewers call it the film's greatest strength.
How would Lund most likely respond to the reviewers?
- The score is the film's greatest strength. :: That is the reviewers' view, not Lund's.
* A score this noticeable is a weakness, not a strength.
- Audiences did not really notice the score. :: Text 2 says they discuss it every time.
> Lund's rule: music should not draw attention. This score does, so she would fault it.
```

# Trap: too strong
Doubt is not disproof. Match Text 2's strength.

## Match the strength

Text 2 only doubts? The answer only doubts. *Disproves* goes too far.

```example
Text 1: A study of 12 people found that a new tea improves sleep.
Text 2: Kaur says 12 people are too few to show anything. She calls for a larger study.
How would Kaur respond to the study?
1. Kaur: the sample is too small :: Her point.
2. She never says the tea fails :: Check how far she goes.
3. Answer: "The finding needs more testing." :: Doubt, not "the tea fails".
```

```more
Compare verbs: *doubts* is weaker than *disproves*. If Text 2 says "too few to show anything" and asks for more tests, it doubts the result. It does not say the result is false. Choose words like *premature*, *needs more testing* or *uncertain*.
```

## Doubt or disprove?

```quiz
Text 1: Archaeologists found one clay figure of a woman in an ancient village. They argue that its people worshipped a goddess.
Text 2: Adeyemi notes that similar figures found elsewhere were toys. One object, he says, cannot settle what it meant.
How would Adeyemi most likely respond to the archaeologists?
- The villagers did not worship a goddess. :: Trap: he doubts the claim. He does not disprove it.
- Their conclusion is right but needs a small fix. :: Too mild. He doubts the whole conclusion.
* Their conclusion is premature, since it rests on one figure.
> He says one object cannot settle it. That is doubt, so the answer doubts too.
```
