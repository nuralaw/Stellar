## What this question looks like

Area and volume questions ask how much space a shape takes up, or how much material covers it. Look for the words **area**, **perimeter**, **volume**, **surface area**, **capacity** (how much a container holds) or **shaded region**. Some questions show a figure. Others describe a real object in words: a water tank, a box, a ball, a can, a garden path.

Typical question stems:

- "What is the area of the shaded region?"
- "What is the volume, in cubic inches, of the cylinder?"
- "A box has a volume of 360 cubic feet. What is its height, in feet?"
- "Each edge of a cube is tripled. The volume of the new cube is how many times the volume of the original cube?"
- "The area of the circle is kπ square units. What is the value of k?"

Geometry and Trigonometry is about 15% of the Math section, which is 5 to 7 of the 44 questions. Expect about one to three of them to be area and volume, at every difficulty level. Most are multiple choice. In a student-produced response you type a number, and you can't type π, so these questions often ask for k in "kπ" or for a plain number.

Figures on the Digital SAT are drawn to scale unless the question says otherwise. Use the picture to check that an answer is reasonable, but get your numbers from the given information.

The main sub-types:

- **Plug in:** choose the formula and calculate.
- **Work backward:** you are given the area or volume and must find a length, like a radius, a height or an edge.
- **Composite and shaded figures:** a shape built from simple pieces, or a shape with a piece cut out.
- **Surface area:** the total area of all the outside faces of a solid.
- **Scaling:** every length is multiplied by the same number, and you find the new area or volume.

## The method

1. **Sketch and label.** Copy the figure on scratch paper, or draw one if there is none. Write every given number on it. Put a question mark on the thing you need.
2. **Circle the exact quantity asked.** Area, perimeter, volume and surface area are different things. So are radius and diameter.
3. **Name the shapes.** If the shape is unusual, split it into simple pieces (rectangles, triangles, circles), or see it as a big shape minus a cut-out.
4. **Write the formula with letters first, then plug in numbers.** Open the reference sheet if you are not sure. Use the radius, not the diameter. Use the height that is perpendicular (at a right angle) to the base.
5. **Work backward when needed.** If you know the area or volume and need a length, solve the equation. Divide first, then take a square root (for r²) or a cube root (for s³ or r³).
6. **For scaling, find k first.** k is the number every length is multiplied by. Lengths change by k, areas by k², and volumes by k³.
7. **Check.** Area is in square units and volume in cubic units. Did you answer the exact question? Does the size make sense next to the figure?

> **Key idea:** Most volumes are "area of the base × height." A cone or a pyramid is exactly one third of that.

## Rules and formulas you need

### What the reference sheet gives you

The reference sheet in Bluebook lists these formulas, and you can open it at any time. Knowing them by heart still saves time.

| Shape | Formula | What the letters mean |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | A = ℓw | length × width |
| Triangle | A = ½bh | h is perpendicular to the base b |
| Circle | A = πr² and C = 2πr | r is the radius, C is the circumference (the distance around) |
| Rectangular box | V = ℓwh | length × width × height |
| Cylinder | V = πr²h | circle base × height |
| Sphere (a ball) | V = (4/3)πr³ | r is the radius |
| Cone | V = (1/3)πr²h | one third of a cylinder with the same base and height |
| Rectangular pyramid | V = (1/3)ℓwh | one third of a box with the same base and height |

The sheet also gives the Pythagorean theorem and the two special right triangles. You often need them to find a missing height.

### What you must know yourself

- **Perimeter** is the distance around a shape: add all the outside sides. A rectangle's perimeter is 2ℓ + 2w.
- **Square:** area s², diagonal s√2.
- **Cube** (a box with every edge equal to s): volume s³, surface area 6s². The diagonal across one face is s√2. The diagonal through the inside, from one corner to the opposite corner, is s√3.
- **Box diagonal** through the inside: √(ℓ² + w² + h²). If you forget it, use the Pythagorean theorem twice: first across the bottom, then up.
- **Parallelogram:** base × height. **Trapezoid** (a shape with two parallel sides): half the sum of the parallel sides × height.
- **Any prism or cylinder:** volume = (area of the base) × height. This works for any base, even a triangle.
- **Diameter:** d = 2r. A **hemisphere** is half of a sphere.
- **Units:** 1 foot = 12 inches, but 1 square foot = 144 square inches and 1 cubic foot = 1,728 cubic inches.

### Surface area

Surface area is the total area of every outside face. You don't need a separate formula for each solid. Imagine unfolding it and add up the pieces.

- **Box:** 2ℓw + 2ℓh + 2wh (three pairs of matching rectangles).
- **Cylinder:** 2πr² + 2πrh. The two circles are the top and the bottom. The side unrolls into a rectangle whose length is the circumference 2πr and whose width is the height h.
- **Sphere:** 4πr². This is not on the reference sheet, so a question that needs it usually gives it to you.
- **Open box or open can:** leave out the missing top.

### Shaded regions and composite figures

- **Shaded area = whole area − unshaded area.** Find the big shape, then subtract what is cut out.
- **Joined shapes: add the pieces.** A grain silo can be a cylinder plus a cone. An ice cream cone can be a cone plus a hemisphere.
- **Perimeter of a composite figure** uses only the outside edges. Lines inside the shape don't count.

### Scaling

If every length of a figure is multiplied by the same number k (the **scale factor**), the new figure is **similar** to the old one: same shape, different size.

| What you measure | It is multiplied by | Example with k = 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Lengths, perimeter, radius, height | k | 3 times as long |
| Area, surface area | k² | 9 times as much |
| Volume | k³ | 27 times as much |
| Angles | nothing, they stay the same | same angles |

Going the other way:

- If you know the **area** ratio, k is its square root. An area ratio of 25 means k = 5.
- If you know the **volume** ratio, k is its cube root. A volume ratio of 8 means k = 2.
- A percent change is a multiplier: "increased by 20%" means × 1.2, and "decreased by 30%" means × 0.7.
- If only **some** lengths change, don't use k² or k³. Put each change into the formula instead (Example 6 shows how).

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Working backward to a diameter

A spherical ball has a volume of 36π cubic inches. What is the diameter of the ball, in inches?

- (A) 3
- (B) 6
- (C) 9
- (D) 27

**Solution.** Write the formula, then plug in: (4/3)πr³ = 36π. Divide both sides by π: (4/3)r³ = 36. Multiply both sides by 3/4: r³ = 27. The cube root of 27 is 3, because 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. So r = 3, and the diameter is 2r = 6. Choice (A) is the radius, the most common slip. Choice (D) is r³, because the cube root step was skipped. Choice (C) divides 27 by 3 instead of taking the cube root.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: From surface area to volume

A cube has a total surface area of 96 square inches. What is the volume of the cube, in cubic inches?

*Student-produced response: enter your answer.*

**Solution.** A cube has 6 equal square faces. Each face has an area of 96 ÷ 6 = 16 square inches. A square with area 16 has sides of length 4, so each edge of the cube is 4 inches. The volume is 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cubic inches. Watch out for two traps: 16 is the area of one face, not the volume, and 16³ = 4,096 cubes the face area instead of the edge.

**Answer:** 64

### Example 3: The area of a path

A circular pond has a radius of 4 meters. A stone path that is 5 meters wide goes all the way around the pond. What is the area of the stone path, in square meters?

- (A) 16π
- (B) 25π
- (C) 65π
- (D) 81π

**Solution.** Sketch it: the pond is a small circle, and the path is the ring around it. The outer edge of the path is a circle with radius 4 + 5 = 9. Path area = big circle − pond = π(9²) − π(4²) = 81π − 16π = 65π. Choice (D) forgets to subtract the pond. Choice (A) is the area of the pond, not the path. Choice (B) treats the path width 5 as a radius. You can't subtract the radii first: (9 − 4)² = 25 is not 81 − 16.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 4: Water rises in a tank

A fish tank is shaped like a rectangular box. Its base is 50 centimeters long and 30 centimeters wide, and it is 40 centimeters tall. The tank holds water to a depth of 25 centimeters. A rock is placed in the tank and sinks to the bottom, and the water rises to a depth of 28 centimeters. What is the volume of the rock, in cubic centimeters?

*Student-produced response: enter your answer.*

**Solution.** The rock pushes the water up. The volume of the rock equals the volume of the extra "layer" of water: a box with the same base as the tank and a height equal to the rise. The water rose 28 − 25 = 3 centimeters. Rock volume = 50 × 30 × 3 = 4,500 cubic centimeters. The height of the tank (40) is extra information. Using 25 or 28 instead of the rise gives 37,500 or 42,000. Those measure the water (or the water plus the rock), not the rock. Type the answer without a comma.

**Answer:** 4500

### Example 5: Similar solids, working backward

The volume of sphere B is 8 times the volume of sphere A. The surface area of sphere A is 36π square centimeters. What is the surface area of sphere B, in square centimeters?

- (A) 72π
- (B) 144π
- (C) 288π
- (D) 2,304π

**Solution.** All spheres are similar, so use scaling. The volume ratio is k³ = 8, so the length ratio is k = 2 (the cube root of 8). Surface area is an area, so it is multiplied by k² = 4: 36π × 4 = 144π. You can check with the radii: 4πr² = 36π gives r = 3 for sphere A. Sphere B has radius 6, and 4π(6²) = 144π. Choice (A) multiplies by k instead of k². Choice (C) multiplies by the volume ratio 8. Choice (D) treats 8 as the length ratio and multiplies by 8² = 64.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 6: Two different changes

The radius of a cylinder is increased by 20%, and its height is decreased by 20%. Which statement best describes the change in the volume of the cylinder?

- (A) The volume does not change.
- (B) The volume decreases by 4%.
- (C) The volume increases by 15.2%.
- (D) The volume increases by 44%.

**Solution.** The lengths don't all change by the same factor, so k³ won't work. Put each change into V = πr²h. The new radius is 1.2r and the new height is 0.8h. New volume = π(1.2r)²(0.8h) = π(1.44r²)(0.8h) = 1.152πr²h. The volume is multiplied by 1.152, which is an increase of 15.2%. Choice (A) assumes that +20% and −20% cancel. Choice (B) forgets that the radius is squared (1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96). Choice (D) ignores the height change (1.2² = 1.44).

> **Tip:** You can also pick easy numbers. Let r = 10 and h = 10, so V = 1,000π. The new cylinder has r = 12 and h = 8, so V = π × 144 × 8 = 1,152π. That is 15.2% more.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **Radius vs diameter.** A diameter of 10 means r = 5. Putting 10 into πr² makes the area 4 times too big.
- **Answering the wrong quantity.** The radius when they asked for the diameter, one face when they asked for the total surface area, or the perimeter when they asked for the area.
- **Forgetting the 1/3** for cones and pyramids, or using the cylinder formula for a cone.
- **Slant height.** The h in the volume formulas is the straight-up height, at a right angle to the base. A slanted edge is longer. Use the Pythagorean theorem to find the real height.
- **k instead of k² or k³.** When every edge of a cube doubles, its surface area becomes 4 times bigger and its volume 8 times bigger.
- **Dividing by 3 instead of taking a cube root.** If s³ = 64, then s = 4, not 64 ÷ 3.
- **Subtracting the wrong piece.** Shaded = whole − cut-out. Don't subtract a full circle when the cut-out is a half circle, and don't subtract radii before squaring.
- **Mixed units.** Inches and feet, or centimeters and meters, in the same question. Convert before you calculate. Square and cubic units convert by 12² or 12³, not by 12.
- **Typing π.** You can't in a student-produced response. If the question says the area is kπ, type only k. If it asks for the actual area, type a decimal.

## Desmos shortcut

Most area and volume questions are quick by hand. Desmos helps when you work backward and the equation is messy.

- **Solve for a length.** Use x for the unknown length and type the whole equation. Desmos draws a vertical line at each solution. For Example 1, type `4/3`, press the right arrow key (→) to leave the fraction, then type `pi x^3=36pi`, and you get the line x = 3. (Typed in one go, `(4/3)pi x^3=36pi` puts everything after the 3 into the denominator and gives an error.) When you get two lines, like x = −6 and x = 6, the length is the positive one.
- **Don't use the letter r.** In Desmos, r means distance from the origin (polar graphs), so `pi r^2=36pi` only gives an error message ("Polar equations must be linear in r") instead of a number. Stick with x.
- **Roots and powers.** Type `cbrt(343)` for a cube root (it gives 7) and `0.8(1.2)^2` to check a scale factor (it gives 1.152).

## Quick checklist

- Did I sketch the figure and label every given number?
- Am I using the radius (not the diameter) and the straight-up height?
- Cone or pyramid? Did I include the 1/3?
- Shaded region: whole minus the cut-out?
- Scaling: lengths × k, areas × k², volumes × k³?
- Did I answer the exact quantity asked, in the right units?
