# Predict the conclusion
The facts point to one careful conclusion.

## Predict "So…"

The blank ends an argument. Predict the conclusion the facts support **before** you read the choices.

```example
Gardeners blamed heavy rain for split tomatoes. But in the driest summer on record, tomatoes split just as often. This suggests that ______
1. Belief: rain splits tomatoes :: Give each sentence a job.
2. Surprise: little rain, same splits :: The fact after "But".
3. So: rain isn't the cause :: Predict before the choices.
4. Answer: rain may not be the main cause :: One summer, so a careful "may".
```

```more
An **inference** is a conclusion the facts support. The text gives a belief, then a fact that surprises, then the blank draws the conclusion. *Suggests* and *may* show a careful conclusion, not a proof.
```

## Predict, then match

```quiz
People thought homing pigeons follow roads home. Yet pigeons released over open sea still flew straight home. This suggests that ______
Which choice most logically completes the text?
* pigeons can find their way without roads.
- pigeons released at sea flew home. :: That repeats a fact. The blank needs the next step.
- roads are useless to pigeons. :: Too strong. They just do not need roads.
> Belief: roads guide them. Surprise: no roads, still home. So roads are not required.
```

# The words before it
The words before the blank set the job.

## Reason or conclusion?

Read the words before the blank. *Since* or *because* wants a reason, not a conclusion.

```example
A café's own customers rated its coffee 9 out of 10. A critic says the score proves little, because ______
1. Words before the blank: "because" :: The blank is a reason.
2. Why does the score prove little? :: Turn those words into a question.
3. Answer: its customers already chose that café :: They are not typical coffee drinkers.
```

```more
The words just before the blank tell you the job. *Because*, *since* and *as* ask for a reason. *Therefore*, *so* and *suggests that* ask for a result. Turn those words into a question, then answer it.
```

## Find the reason

```quiz
On a new park's opening day, 90% of visitors surveyed said they support the park. The council warns that this may overstate the town's support, since ______
- most of the town supports the park. :: That is the claim being doubted, not a reason.
* people who came to the opening probably already liked the park.
- the park cost more than planned. :: Cost is new and does not explain the survey.
> *Since* asks why the number may be too high: opening-day visitors are not the whole town.
```

# Stay inside the text
Same place, same group, no new facts.

## Draw the circle

Draw a circle around what the text covers. A good answer stays inside it.

```visual
inferences--scope
```

```example
A factory opened upstream of Port Avel's bay. Its waste water warmed the bay by 3°C, and the bay's crabs breed poorly in water that warm. The crab catch has fallen. This suggests that ______
1. The text covers one bay, one factory :: Draw the circle there.
2. Out: "factories harm all bays" :: Wider than one bay.
3. Out: "the water has poisons" :: A new fact.
4. Answer: the warm water may be one reason for the smaller catch :: Inside the circle.
```

```more
Wrong choices often leave the circle in two ways. They make it **wider** (one bay becomes all bays) or they add a **new fact** (poisons) that the text never mentions. Keep the same place, the same group and the same facts.
```

## Combine the facts

```quiz
Linguist Mei Tan recorded 40 speakers of a mountain dialect. Speakers under 30 used the older pronunciation of "water" 5% of the time. Speakers aged 30 to 60 used it 35% of the time. Speakers over 60 used it 80% of the time. This suggests that ______
Which choice most logically completes the text?
- older speakers never use the newer pronunciation. :: Too strong. They use the old one only 80% of the time.
* the older pronunciation may be becoming less common among younger speakers.
- younger speakers dislike the older pronunciation. :: New idea. The text says nothing about attitudes.
> Three age groups, one pattern: the old sound is rarest in the young and commonest in the old. So it may be fading.
```

# Match the strength
Strong evidence earns *likely*. Weak evidence, *may*.

## Match verb to evidence

Pick the choice whose strength matches the evidence: one small study, *may*; a fair comparison repeated, *likely*.

```visual
inferences--strength
```

```example
Ecologist Lia Moreno counted seabird nests on 12 islands. Islands with rats averaged 40 nests. Similar islands without rats averaged 210. This suggests that ______
1. Evidence: 12 islands, a fair comparison :: Strong evidence.
2. Too strong: "rats wiped out the seabirds" :: Nests remain on those islands.
3. Against the data: "rats may have no effect" :: 40 versus 210 says otherwise.
4. Answer: rats likely reduce seabird nesting :: The strength matches.
```

```more
A "May" answer is not always right. If a fact rules something out (a fish with no eyes cannot find food by sight), a firm answer is correct. But most questions give evidence that is only strong or weak, so choose *likely* or *may* to match it.
```

## Weak evidence, careful verb

```quiz
In one small study of nine volunteers, a new memory app raised test scores by an average of 6 points after a month. No comparison group used a different app. This suggests that ______
Which choice most logically completes the text?
- the app improves everyone's memory. :: Too strong. Nine volunteers cannot show this.
- the app has no effect on memory. :: The scores rose, so this goes against the text.
* the app may improve some people's memory, but more testing is needed.
> One small study without a comparison group gives weak evidence, so only a careful answer fits.
```

# Be careful with samples
Unusual group studied? Results may not apply elsewhere.

## Spot the unusual group

When the studied group is unusual, the conclusion is often a warning: results may not apply to others.

```example
Much of what scientists know about disease comes from lab mice. But Dr. Ito notes that lab mice are bred to be nearly identical, unlike people. She therefore argues that ______
1. Studied group: lab mice :: Where most results come from.
2. Catch: bred identical, unlike people :: The group is unusual.
3. So: results may not carry over :: Predict a careful warning.
4. Answer: lab-mouse findings should be checked before applying them to people :: Careful, not absolute.
```

```more
Such conclusions often say *researchers should be cautious* or *findings may not apply to*. A good choice warns without going too far: it does not say the studies are useless, only that they need checking in other groups.
```

## Write the warning

```quiz
Much of what scientists know about coral bleaching comes from studies of one fast-growing coral species that tolerates heat unusually well. Marine biologist Tomas Reyes therefore cautions that ______
Which choice most logically completes the text?
- all corals bleach at the same temperature. :: This contradicts the text. The studied species tolerates heat unusually well.
* findings about that species may not hold for slower-growing corals.
- studying that species is a waste of time. :: Too strong. The studies are useful, just limited.
> The studied species is unusual, so its results may not apply to other corals.
```

# Trap: it could be true
Likely isn't enough. Find the sentence that proves it.

## Which sentence proves it?

A sensible story the text never tells is wrong. Ask: which sentence proves this?

```example
A bakery moved next to a train station. Its morning sales tripled. Afternoon sales stayed flat. This suggests that ______
1. Tempting: "the bakery lowered its prices" :: Possible, but no sentence says so.
2. Stated: new spot, mornings tripled :: Use only what is written.
3. Answer: the station likely brought more morning customers :: Every piece is in the text.
```

```more
Two more traps. **Reversed cause:** the text says A goes with B, and the choice says B caused A. **Half supported:** one part of the choice is in the text, but the other part is new. Check every part.
```

## Spot the trap

```quiz
A city put bright lights in one park. Over the next year, evening visits there doubled. At a similar unlit park nearby, evening visits stayed the same. Both parks are on the same bus line. This suggests that ______
Which choice most logically completes the text?
- people avoided the unlit park because of crime. :: Trap: it sounds real, but crime is never mentioned.
- every city park should get lights. :: That is advice, and it stretches one park to all.
* the lights likely drew more evening visitors.
> The unlit park did not change, so the lights explain the jump. No sentence mentions crime.
```
