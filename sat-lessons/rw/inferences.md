## What this question looks like

You get a short text that builds an argument and then stops. The text is on the left of the screen, and its last sentence ends with a blank. On the right, the question is always the same:

- "Which choice most logically completes the text?"

The words just before the blank usually tell you what kind of ending is needed: "This finding suggests that ______", "Therefore, it can be concluded that ______", "…because ______".

The texts are clear academic writing about science, history, economics or the arts, not stories or poems. They are written to be packed with logic: every sentence matters. You never need outside knowledge. Everything you need is in the text.

**How many and where.** Expect about 3 or 4 per test. They are the last questions of the Information and Ideas group, right before the grammar questions start, and they are ordered from easier to harder. Many students find the last one or two the hardest questions in the module.

> **Key idea:** An inference on the SAT is not a guess or a creative idea. It is the small, safe step that the text forces you to take. If you can't point to the sentences that make your answer true, it's the wrong answer.

## The method

1. **Read the whole text, one sentence at a time.** Give each sentence a job: background, a belief or claim, a finding, a contrast. Ask, "What does this sentence add?"
2. **Find the turn.** Most texts set up an idea and then add a fact that breaks it, limits it or explains it, often after *however*, *but* or *yet*. The conclusion lives in that tension.
3. **Read the words right before the blank.** They tell you the job: a conclusion, a reason, a contrast, a condition (see the table below).
4. **Predict the ending in your own words.** Start with "So…" and make it the smallest step that uses all the key facts. Don't add anything new.
5. **Test each choice.** Does it follow from the text with nothing added? Is it about the same people, things and situation? Is it the same strength as the evidence? Does it do the job set by the words before the blank?
6. **Put your choice into the blank and read the last two sentences again.** The right ending sounds like the only sensible way to finish the argument.

> **Tip:** The College Board writes the right answer as a strong, natural conclusion, so you can usually find it by matching your prediction, not just by crossing things out. If your prediction isn't there, look for the choice that says the same thing in other words.

> **Remember:** If a hard inference question is eating your time, pick your best answer, flag it, and do the quicker grammar questions that follow. Come back with the time you have left.

## Rules and formulas you need

### The words before the blank set the job

| Words before the blank | The blank must… |
|---|---|
| therefore, thus, so, it can be concluded that | state what *must* follow from everything above |
| suggests that, implies that, most likely | state what the evidence points to, carefully |
| because, since | give the reason that links the facts |
| however, but, yet, although | go against what came just before |
| for example | give one case of the general idea |
| only if, unless | give the condition |

Watch for negative lead-ins such as "it is unlikely that" or "researchers should not assume that". They flip the direction of the answer.

### Common argument patterns

| Pattern | How the text goes | The ending usually says… |
|---|---|---|
| Ruling out a cause | Two groups differ, but they are the same in factor F | F can't explain the difference; something else does |
| Counterexample | "Trait T is thought to come from C." Then: T found where there is no C | T doesn't always come from C |
| Timeline | Object contains something that didn't exist until year Y | The object dates from Y or later |
| Chain | A leads to B, and B leads to C | A leads to C |
| Hidden third factor | X and Y appear together, but Z causes both | X may not cause Y |
| Puzzle | The known reasons are too weak to explain something | There is probably a reason not yet known |
| Limits of a study | The study tested only one group or one setting | The results may not apply to other groups or settings |

### Match the strength of the evidence

| If the evidence is… | The right ending sounds like… |
|---|---|
| one study, a pattern, a few cases | careful words: may, likely, suggests, some, not necessarily (not proves, always, all, never, exactly) |
| a fact that rules something out completely, such as a date | firm words are fine: cannot, was not, must |

Careful words are not always right, and firm words are not always wrong. The test is simple: is the answer exactly as strong as the logic?

### Must be true, not could be true

The right answer **must** follow from the text. A choice that *could* be true, or is even true in real life, is still wrong if the text doesn't force it. "New information" choices are the easiest way the test catches students who know a lot about the topic.

## Worked examples

Every text below was written for this lesson. The people, places and studies are made up.

### Example 1: Ruling out an explanation

Gardeners in the town of Ostley have noticed that tomatoes grown on the east side of town ripen about a week earlier than tomatoes grown on the west side. Some gardeners believe the reason is that the east side gets more sunlight. However, a survey by the town's gardening club found that gardens on both sides of town receive nearly the same number of hours of sunlight each day. This finding suggests that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

- (A) the difference in ripening time is likely caused by something other than sunlight.
- (B) the east side of town gets more hours of sunlight each day than the west side does.
- (C) gardeners on the east side of town are more experienced than gardeners on the west side.
- (D) the amount of sunlight a tomato plant receives has no effect on how quickly its fruit ripens.

**Solution.** The pattern is "ruling out a cause". Belief: more sun explains the earlier tomatoes. Turn: both sides get the same sun. If the sun is equal, it can't explain a difference. Prediction: *so something other than sunlight is causing it.*

- (A) matches the prediction and stays careful with "likely".
- (B) is the gardeners' old belief, and the survey shows it is wrong. It contradicts the text.
- (C) could be true, but experience is never mentioned. New information.
- (D) is far too broad. The survey shows that sunlight doesn't explain *this* difference, not that sunlight never matters for any tomato.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 2: A counterexample

Many desert plants have very small leaves, which lose less water than large leaves do. For this reason, botanists have often treated small leaves as a sign that a plant is adapted to dry conditions. Botanist Lena Marsh, however, has studied several plant species in cold, wet mountain forests that also have very small leaves. In these forests, Marsh found, small leaves are less likely than large leaves to be torn apart by strong winds. Marsh's findings suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

- (A) a plant's small leaves are not necessarily a sign that the plant is adapted to dry conditions.
- (B) desert plants with small leaves do not actually lose less water than desert plants with large leaves.
- (C) the plants in wet mountain forests are descended from plants that once grew in deserts.
- (D) strong winds are a greater danger to most plants than dry conditions are.

**Solution.** The text sets up a rule: small leaves → adapted to dryness. Then comes a counterexample: plants in *wet* places with small leaves, which help against wind. One clear counterexample means the rule doesn't always hold. Prediction: *small leaves don't always mean the plant is built for dry places.*

- (A) matches, and "not necessarily" is exactly the right strength.
- (B) contradicts the first sentence. Marsh's work never questions how much water small leaves save.
- (C) Ancestry is never discussed. New information.
- (D) The text compares nothing about "most plants". Unsupported and too broad.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 3: A timeline

Divers exploring the harbor of Port Lissan have found the remains of a wooden cargo ship. Old town records describe a storm that sank several ships in the harbor in 1690, so some historians assumed that this ship was lost in that storm. However, the ship's planks are held together with a type of iron nail that was not invented until 1724. If that date for the nail is correct, it can be concluded that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

- (A) the ship was built in 1724.
- (B) the ship sank in a different storm at some point after 1724.
- (C) the town's records of the 1690 storm are not accurate.
- (D) the ship was not one of the ships that sank in the 1690 storm.

**Solution.** "It can be concluded that" asks for something that *must* be true. A ship that contains nails invented in 1724 can't have been lying on the harbor floor since 1690. Prediction: *so this ship didn't sink in the 1690 storm.*

- (A) is too exact. The nails only show the ship was built (or repaired) no earlier than 1724. It could just as well be 1750.
- (B) sounds like the natural next step, but nothing says a storm sank it. It could have been wrecked on rocks, burned or simply abandoned. Could be true, but it doesn't have to be. New information.
- (C) overreaches. The storm may well have happened as recorded; this ship just wasn't one of its victims.
- (D) follows with certainty. Here a firm answer is right, because the logic is firm.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 4: Joining two facts

Psychologists have found that when people face a large number of similar options, they often put off making any choice at all. In 2023, a bakery chain cut the number of bread varieties in its shops from 24 to 8, while keeping its prices, opening hours and advertising the same. Over the next six months, the share of customers who left a shop without buying anything fell by a third. The psychologists' finding could explain this change, because ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

- (A) customers who saw fewer varieties may have found it easier to decide what to buy.
- (B) the 16 varieties that the chain removed were the ones that customers liked least.
- (C) customers bought more bread on each visit after the number of varieties was cut.
- (D) people always buy more when they are offered fewer choices.

**Solution.** The lead-in is "because", so the blank must give the *link* between the psychologists' finding and the bakery's results. Fact 1: many similar options make people put off choosing. Fact 2: fewer options, and fewer customers leaving with nothing. The prices, hours and advertising stayed the same, so they can't explain the change. Prediction: *with fewer options, choosing was easier, so fewer people walked out without buying.*

- (A) joins the two facts and stays careful with "may".
- (B) might be true, but it has nothing to do with the psychologists' finding, which is about *how many* options there are, not which ones. It is also never stated. New information.
- (C) The text measured how many customers left *without buying anything*, not how much each customer bought. It may sound related, but nothing supports it. Wrong measure.
- (D) "Always" is too strong. The psychologists say people "often" put off choosing, and one bakery's results can't prove a rule about everyone.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 5: A hidden third factor

Economist Rafael Duarte studied 40 small towns that built new public libraries between 2005 and 2015. In most of these towns, the number of residents with college degrees rose during the following decade. Duarte points out, however, that towns usually build new libraries only when their populations and tax incomes are already growing, and that growing towns tend to attract college-educated workers whether or not they build a new library. Duarte's observations suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

- (A) college-educated workers prefer to live in towns that have recently built new libraries.
- (B) his study does not show that the new libraries caused the rise in college-educated residents.
- (C) the towns in his study would have gained exactly as many college-educated residents if they had not built new libraries.
- (D) the new libraries helped the towns' tax incomes grow by attracting college-educated workers.

**Solution.** The pattern is a hidden third factor. Libraries and graduates appear together, but *growth* comes first and could cause both: growing towns build libraries, and growing towns attract graduates anyway. Prediction: *so the libraries may not be what brought the graduates.*

- (A) This is exactly the conclusion Duarte warns against. Nothing shows that graduates choose towns because of libraries.
- (B) matches the prediction at the right strength. It doesn't say the libraries had *no* effect; it says the study can't show that they caused the rise.
- (C) "Exactly as many" is much too strong. Duarte says growth *could* explain the rise, not that the libraries made zero difference. This is the most tempting trap: it sounds like the text, but it claims far more.
- (D) gets the order backward. In the text, tax income is already growing *before* towns build libraries. The choice also assumes the very link Duarte questions: that libraries attract graduates.

**Answer:** (B)

## Traps to avoid

- **Too strong:** "proves", "always", "exactly", "all" when the text gives one study or a pattern. Check every strong word against the evidence.
- **Too broad:** the text is about one town, one forest or one kind of plant, and the choice turns it into a rule about everything.
- **New information:** a reasonable idea (experience, ancestry, which bread was removed) that the text never mentions. It *could* be true, but it doesn't *have* to be.
- **Repeats a premise:** a choice that is true according to the text but only restates a fact already given, instead of saying what the facts lead to.
- **Contradicts a premise:** a choice that quietly denies something the text states as fact.
- **Reversed order or cause:** the choice flips what comes first or what causes what (libraries raising tax income when the text says income came first).
- **The conclusion the text argues against:** in "people assumed X, however…" texts, a choice that restates X.
- **True in real life:** something you know from outside the test. If the text doesn't support it, it's wrong here.

## Quick checklist

- What job does each sentence do, and where is the turn?
- What do the words right before the blank ask for: a conclusion, a reason, a contrast?
- Did I predict a "So…" ending before reading the choices?
- Does my choice add nothing new and stay about the same subjects?
- Is it exactly as strong as the evidence?
- Does it still sound right when I read it in the blank?
