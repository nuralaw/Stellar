## What this question looks like

Words in Context questions usually come first in each Reading and Writing module. You get a short passage (often just two or three sentences) and one of two question stems:

- **Fill the blank:** the passage has a blank, and you're asked: "Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?" This is the most common version.
- **Meaning of a word:** a word in the passage is quoted, and you're asked: "As used in the text, what does the word *X* most nearly mean?" This one shows up more in older literary passages.

All four choices are real words, and they usually all fit the grammar of the sentence. So the question isn't "which word sounds okay?" It's "which word does the passage *prove* belongs here?"

> **Key idea:** This is a reading question more than a vocabulary quiz. The passage nearly always contains a clue that tells you what the blank must mean. Your job is to find that clue.

## The method

Use this routine on every question. Aim for about 30–45 seconds each.

1. **Hide the choices.** Don't read them yet. If you read them first, the fanciest-sounding word tends to grab you.
2. **Find the clue.** Read the whole passage, not just the sentence with the blank. Look for the words that describe or explain the blank, and for signal words like *however*, *because*, *for example*, or a colon. (See the clue types below.)
3. **Predict your own word.** Say a simple word or short phrase that fits, like "small," "not important" or "made worse." It doesn't have to be fancy. If you can't think of a word, at least decide the **direction**: positive or negative? more or less? supports or goes against?
4. **Match and eliminate.** Now read the choices. Cross out every word that doesn't match your prediction. If two choices are left, compare their **tone** (positive, negative, neutral) and **strength** (mild or extreme) to the passage.
5. **Check it.** Reread the full passage with your answer in the blank. It should agree with the clue and with the passage's tone.

For "most nearly means" questions, mentally cross out the quoted word and treat it like a blank. Predict a meaning from the clues, *then* look at the choices. The test loves second meanings.

**When you don't know a word in the choices:**

- Eliminate the words you *do* know. If three of them clearly fail, the unknown word is the answer, even if you've never seen it.
- Don't pick the unknown word *because* it's hard. In one count of official practice tests, the hardest-looking choice was right only about a quarter of the time, which is no better than guessing.
- Use word parts as a hint: *in-*, *un-*, *a-* usually mean "not" (*atypical* = not typical); *-able* / *-ible* mean "can be" (*perceptible* = can be noticed).
- Never leave it blank. There's no penalty for guessing, so make your best pick and move on.

## Rules and formulas you need

**The four main clue types**

| Clue type | Signal words | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | however, but, although, yet, despite, while, unlike, rather than | The blank goes the **opposite** way from the other idea |
| Cause / result | because, since, so, therefore, as a result, thus | The blank must make sense as a **cause or effect** of the other idea |
| Restatement | colon (:), dash (—), that is, in other words, *this* / *such* + noun | The passage **repeats** the blank's meaning in other words, usually right after it |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as, including | The examples show the **general quality** the blank names |

Also watch for **continuation** words (*also*, *similarly*, *in fact*, *moreover*): they keep going in the same direction as the idea before them.

> **Tip:** Restatement is one of the most common clues. Look right after the blank for a colon, a dash, or a phrase that starts with "so," "such that" or "meaning." The passage often explains the blank in plain words there.

**High-frequency Digital SAT words**

Most correct answers are ordinary words. The ones below are the harder words that keep showing up as answers *and* as wrong choices. Learn them in the sense given here.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| peripheral | on the edge; not central or important |
| integral | essential; a necessary part of the whole |
| tentative | not certain or final; done as a trial |
| tenuous | weak, thin (an argument, a link) |
| latent | present but hidden or not yet active |
| corroborate | confirm with more evidence |
| buttress | support or strengthen (an idea, an argument) |
| undermine | weaken, often gradually |
| ameliorate | make a bad situation better |
| rectify | fix or correct |
| stymie | block, hold back progress |
| exhaustive | complete; covering everything |
| intricate | very detailed, with many connected parts |
| atypical | not typical; unusual |
| inconsequential | unimportant; makes no real difference |
| negligible | so small it hardly matters |
| perceptible | able to be noticed |
| manifest | to show clearly; clearly visible |
| sanguine | hopeful, optimistic |
| impending | about to happen |
| widespread | found in many places or among many people |
| substantial | large; considerable |
| proponent | a supporter of an idea |
| unassuming | modest; not showy |
| scrutinize | examine very closely |

**Everyday words with test meanings.** These look easy, but the SAT tests a second meaning:

| Word | Test meaning | Example of that sense |
|---|---|---|
| qualify | limit or soften a claim | "She qualified her praise." |
| novel | new, original | "a novel method" |
| temper | moderate, tone down | "temper expectations" |
| check | stop or slow | "check the spread of a disease" |
| yield | produce | "the study yielded data" |
| account for | explain | "account for the results" |

## Worked examples

### Example 1: A contrast clue

For decades, many historians treated letters written by nineteenth-century factory workers as ______ sources, useful mainly for a colorful quote or two. Recent studies, however, have shown that these letters hold detailed records of wages, hours and working conditions that appear in no official document.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) indispensable
- (B) peripheral
- (C) authoritative
- (D) contentious

**Solution.** Hide the choices and find the clue. Two clues work together here. First, a restatement: the letters were seen as "useful mainly for a colorful quote or two." Second, a contrast: "however" tells us the recent view (the letters are full of unique, detailed records) is the *opposite* of the old view. So the old view was that the letters were minor. Prediction: "minor" or "not central."

- (A) *indispensable* means "absolutely necessary." That's the *new* view, so it's the reversal trap.
- (B) *peripheral* means "on the edge, not central." It matches "minor."
- (C) *authoritative* means "reliable, trusted." That's positive, but the old view was dismissive.
- (D) *contentious* means "causing arguments." Nothing in the passage says historians argued about the letters, so it's a topic-related word with no support.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: Cause plus examples

Because the island has been cut off from the mainland for thousands of years, its lizards have developed traits that are ______ elsewhere: a blue-tinted tongue, unusually long toes and a diet made up mostly of flower nectar.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) widespread
- (B) absent
- (C) detrimental
- (D) celebrated

**Solution.** The cause clue is "because the island has been cut off." Being isolated for a long time makes a population *different* from others. The colon then gives examples of odd, unusual traits. So the traits are ones you *don't* find in other places. Prediction: "not found."

- (A) *widespread* means "found in many places." That's the opposite of what isolation produces.
- (B) *absent* means "not present." "Traits that are absent elsewhere" means "found only here," which matches.
- (C) *detrimental* means "harmful." The passage never says the traits hurt anything.
- (D) *celebrated* means "praised." The passage gives no one's opinion of the traits.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 3: A second meaning ("most nearly means")

In her review, the critic praised the novel's vivid setting and sharp dialogue. She was careful, though, to qualify her enthusiasm, pointing out that the final chapters felt rushed and left several plot threads unresolved.

As used in the text, what does the word "qualify" most nearly mean?

- (A) earn
- (B) limit
- (C) certify
- (D) describe

**Solution.** Cross out "qualify" and treat it as a blank: "She was careful, though, to ______ her enthusiasm." "Though" is a contrast clue: after the praise comes a step back. The restatement follows: she pointed out flaws. So she held back some of her excitement. Prediction: "hold back" or "tone down."

- (A) *earn* matches "qualify for a prize," which is the everyday meaning. It makes no sense to earn your own enthusiasm.
- (B) *limit* means "hold back or restrict." That matches the prediction.
- (C) *certify* matches "qualified" as in "a qualified nurse." That's a real meaning of the word, but the wrong one here.
- (D) *describe* is too vague. It misses the idea of pulling back that "though" and the flaws signal.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: Getting the strength right

A survey of 40 coastal towns found that towns near large seagrass meadows also tended to report bigger fish catches. The study's authors were careful not to overstate this result. They argued only that the pattern ______ a link between seagrass and fish populations, since other factors, such as water temperature, could also explain it.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

- (A) confirms
- (B) hints at
- (C) obscures
- (D) rules out

**Solution.** First the direction: the pattern (more seagrass, more fish) *supports* a link, so the blank is positive toward the link. Then the strength: the authors were "careful not to overstate," "argued only," and admitted that other factors could explain the result. So the support is weak. Prediction: "suggests."

- (A) *confirms* is the right direction but far too strong. It's exactly the overstatement the authors avoided.
- (B) *hints at* means "suggests a little." Right direction, right strength.
- (C) *obscures* means "hides or makes unclear." That's the wrong direction, because the pattern points *toward* a link.
- (D) *rules out* means "proves false." That's the opposite.

**Answer:** (B)

## Traps to avoid

- **The reversal.** A choice means the opposite of what the passage needs, usually because you missed a *however* or *although*. This is the most common wrong answer. Once you've found the direction, cross these out first.
- **The everyday meaning.** A choice is a real definition of the word, just not the one used here (*qualify* = "earn" in Example 3). Always test the meaning against the passage, not your memory.
- **Right direction, wrong strength.** "Proves" when the passage only "suggests," "furious" when the person is just "annoyed." Match the passage's hedging words (*may*, *some*, *only*, *tended to*).
- **Right meaning, wrong tone.** Two words can mean nearly the same thing but carry different feelings, like *frugal* (careful with money, positive) versus *stingy* (negative). Pick the one whose feeling matches the passage.
- **On topic but unsupported.** A word that fits the *subject* (a debate word in a history passage) but that nothing in the passage backs up, like *contentious* in Example 1.
- **The fancy-word magnet.** Picking the most impressive word because it "sounds like the SAT." Simple answers like *show*, *reduce* or *absent* are right all the time.
- **"It sounds fine."** Every choice usually fits the grammar, so "it flows" proves nothing. Ask which word the clue *demands*.

> **Trap:** Don't skip the sentence *after* the blank. On the Digital SAT, the clue often comes after the blank, in the next clause or the next sentence.

## Quick checklist

- Did I read the whole passage before looking at the choices?
- Can I point to the exact clue (contrast, cause, restatement or example)?
- Did I predict a word, or at least a direction (positive/negative, more/less)?
- Does my answer match the passage's tone *and* strength, not just its topic?
- For "most nearly means," did I choose the meaning the passage uses, not the everyday one?
- Did I reread the passage with my answer in place?
