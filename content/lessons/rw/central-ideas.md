# Topic + point
The point often follows *but*, *however*, *yet*.

## Topic + point

Main idea = **topic + point**: the whole text in one sentence.

```example
For decades, biologists assumed octopuses sleep in one quiet state. But when Lena Park's team filmed them, the animals sometimes entered an active stage: their skin flashed colors and their arms twitched. What is the main idea?
1. Topic: octopus sleep :: Who or what is it about?
2. "But" → the old view ends, the finding starts :: Find the word that turns.
3. Main idea: octopus sleep also has an active stage. :: Say it before the choices.
```

```more
The **topic** is who or what the text is about. The **point** is what the author says about it. Words like *but*, *however* and *yet* often show where the point starts. Sometimes the point comes in the first sentence instead, and the rest supports it.
```

## Find the point yourself

```quiz
People long said that goldfish forget everything within seconds. Yet in one lab, goldfish learned to push a lever for food and still did it three months later.
Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- Goldfish forget everything within seconds. :: That is the old view, before "Yet".
* Goldfish can remember far longer than people said.
- Lab goldfish pushed a lever for food. :: True, but it is only one detail of the test.
> The point comes after "Yet": the goldfish still remembered three months later.
```

# The right size
Not one detail. Not a rule about everything.

## Same size as the text

The answer covers **every sentence** and nothing more.

```visual
central-ideas--right-size
```

```example
Honeybees do more than make honey. They pollinate apple trees, almond groves and clover fields. Farmers even rent hives to raise their harvests. What is the main idea?
1. "Farmers rent hives." ✗ :: Too narrow: one detail.
2. "Insects are vital to all farming." ✗ :: Too broad: the text is only about honeybees.
3. Honeybees help crops grow, not just make honey. :: Fits all three sentences.
```

```more
**Too narrow:** true, but it covers only one sentence. **Too broad:** it says more than the text says. A good answer is a short summary of the whole text, using the text's own topic.
```

## Pick the right size

```quiz
Painter Rosa Vidal grew up in a fishing town, and her work keeps returning to it. Her early canvases show its harbor at dawn. Her later abstract pieces use the blue-gray of its boats. Even her portraits set people against nets or sails.
Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- Vidal's early canvases show the harbor at dawn. :: True, but it is one of three examples. Too narrow.
- Most painters draw on the places where they grew up. :: The text is only about Vidal. Too broad.
* Vidal's hometown appears throughout her work, in different forms.
> Sentence one makes the claim. The other three are examples of it.
```

# Grant, then point
Authors may admit a point, then give their own.

## Find what they grant

An author can **grant** (admit) a point, then argue for a different one. The main idea is the second.

```visual
central-ideas--grant-point
```

```example
Critics praise the novel's plot. It is not unreasonable to admire the plot, but the book's real strength is its narrator, whose voice grows stranger and more convincing with each chapter. What is the main idea?
1. Grant: "not unreasonable" to admire the plot :: A small agreement.
2. Point: after *but*, the narrator :: The author's own claim.
3. Main idea: the narrator, not the plot, is the strength. :: Keep both, stress the point.
```

```more
"Not unreasonable", "not impossible" and "not inconceivable" are careful ways to say "possible". The author gives this point away, then explains why another point matters more. A choice that keeps only the granted part is a trap.
```

## Keep both halves

```quiz
Many commentators credit the success of 1920s film studios to star power. It is hardly implausible that famous names drew crowds, yet the studios' own ledgers show that audiences also flocked to films with unknown actors, suggesting that the novelty of the medium mattered more.
Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- Audiences in the 1920s preferred films with unknown actors. :: Too strong. The text says they also came to those films.
- Star power was the main reason for the studios' success. :: That is the granted point, not the author's claim.
* Star power may have helped, but the studios' records point to another main cause.
> The author admits star power could matter, then argues the medium's novelty mattered more.
```

# Stated details
Find the line that answers the question.

## Point to the line

*According to the text* asks for one stated fact. Find the sentence, then match it.

```example
The first bicycles had no pedals: riders pushed off the ground with their feet. In the 1860s, pedals were added to the front wheel. According to the text, how did the first riders move?
1. Asked: the *first* riders :: Mark the exact question.
2. Sentence 1: "pushed off the ground with their feet" :: Find its line.
3. They pushed off the ground with their feet. :: Pedals came later.
```

```more
A detail question asks for one fact, so the answer is usually a close copy of one sentence, often in different words. Underline the key word in the question (*first*, *why*, *how*) and look for it in the text.
```

## Find the reason

```quiz
In the 1920s, the town of Halden switched to yellow streetlights. Its fog was so thick that white lamps seemed to vanish, while yellow light carried farther. Years later, tourists praised the yellow glow, and it became the town's symbol.
According to the text, why did Halden switch to yellow streetlights?
* Yellow light was easier to see through the fog.
- Tourists praised the yellow glow. :: True in the text, but it answers a different question.
- The town wanted a symbol. :: It became a symbol later. Nobody planned that.
- White lamps cost too much. :: The text never mentions cost.
> Sentence two gives the reason: white lamps vanished in the fog; yellow light carried farther.
```

## Say it in new words

*Based on the text* allows one small step. The answer rephrases what is written.

```quiz
Veln harbor freezes solid each winter, and by February every fishing boat sits stuck in the ice. Tomas, who owns one of the boats, spends that month repairing his nets and repainting his hull.
Based on the text, why does Tomas do these jobs in February?
* He cannot fish then, so he uses the time for repairs.
- He is preparing to sell the boat. :: The text never says he will sell it.
- The ice damaged his nets. :: The text does not say the ice caused damage.
- Other fishermen asked him to. :: Nobody asks him in the text.
> The boats are stuck in ice, so he cannot fish. Repairs fill the time. It is one small step from the text.
```

# Stories and poems
Actions and word choices show feelings.

## Actions show feelings

Read what a character **does**. If words and actions differ, keep both.

```example
Though Mr. Aldous declared himself perfectly indifferent to the village prize, he was seen to rise before dawn and tend his roses with extraordinary care. When the judges arrived, he discovered urgent business in the far field. What is the main idea?
1. Says: "perfectly indifferent" :: His words.
2. Does: up at dawn, then hides away :: His actions.
3. He pretends not to care about the prize, but he does. :: Keep both halves.
```

```more
Older texts use harder words. *Declared* = said firmly. *Indifferent* = not interested. *Extraordinary* = very great. If a word is new, use what the character does to guess the feeling. Poems work the same way: ask what the speaker feels, and which words show it.
```

## Read Nora

```quiz
When the letter came, Nora read it twice, folded it small and said, "Well, that's settled." She made dinner as usual. Only after everyone had gone to bed did she sit by the window, turning the letter over and over in her hands.
Which choice best describes how Nora responds to the letter?
- She is openly furious. :: Too strong. Nothing she says or does is angry.
- She is relieved that it is settled. :: That ignores what she does once she is alone.
* She acts calm but is privately troubled.
> Calm in front of others, restless alone at night. The answer keeps both halves.
```

# Trap: one word too strong
*Some* is not *all*. *May* is not *proves*.

## Check each word's strength

One word the text does not support makes a choice wrong.

```example
In a survey of 40 gardeners, most preferred clay pots. The author says the result might reflect local habits. Which statement does the text support?
1. "Gardeners always prefer clay pots." ✗ :: "Always": the text says "most" of 40.
2. Most of the surveyed gardeners preferred clay pots. :: Same strength as the text.
```

```more
Words like *all*, *every*, *always*, *never*, *proves* and *only* are very strong. Words like *some*, *most*, *may*, *suggests* and *slightly* are weaker. Choose the choice whose strength matches the text's own words.
```

## Spot the strong word

```quiz
In a small study, students who took a ten-minute walk before a math quiz scored slightly higher, on average, than students who sat quietly. The researcher says a short walk may help some students focus.
Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
- A study proves that a walk raises every student's quiz score. :: Trap: "proves" and "every". The text says "may" and "some".
* A small study hints that a short walk may help some students.
- Walking is the best way to prepare for a quiz. :: The study compared walking only with sitting.
> The text is careful: "small", "slightly", "may", "some". The answer must be just as careful.
```
