# Support a claim
Predict what you'd see, then match it.

## Predict, then match

Find the **claim**. Ask: **if it is true, what should we see?** Pick the choice that shows exactly that.

```example
Hypothesis: traffic noise makes city birds sing higher. Which finding supports it?
Choices: City birds lay more eggs. / When noise was played in a quiet forest, birds there sang higher.
1. Claim: noise → higher pitch :: Two ideas, one link.
2. Prediction: add noise, pitch goes up :: If true, what should we see?
3. (A) is about eggs, not pitch :: Same topic, wrong link. Cross it out.
4. Answer: (B) :: Noise added, pitch rose. It matches.
```

```more
A **hypothesis** is an idea that scientists test. A question says *if true* because you imagine the finding is real, then ask: does it support the idea? You do not have to prove anything. Just ask what the finding would show.
```

## Support the goby claim

```quiz
Biologist Rafa Lim claims that gobies, a small fish, recognize themselves in a mirror. He painted a dot on each goby's throat, a spot a goby can see only in a mirror.
Which finding, if true, would most directly support Lim's claim?
- Lim's mirrors were about the size of a goby. :: That is the method, not a result.
* Gobies ignored the dot until they got a mirror, then rubbed their throats on rocks.
- Many fish species have natural throat spots. :: That is about other species, not gobies and mirrors.
> If a goby knows the reflection is itself, the mirror should make it notice its own dot.
```

# Weaken a claim
Find what the claim says you should not see.

## Flip the prediction

Ask what the claim says you should **not** see. A finding that shows it weakens the claim.

```example
Claim: cats purr only when they are content. Which finding weakens it?
Choices: Adult cats purr more loudly than kittens. / Cats in pain at the vet often purr.
1. Claim: purring → content :: Two ideas, one link.
2. Should not see: purring without content :: Flip the prediction.
3. (A) is about loudness :: Same topic, wrong link. Cross it out.
4. Answer: (B) :: Purring in pain breaks the link.
```

```more
**Weaken** and **undermine** mean the same: make the claim less likely to be true. The best weakener is a finding that is the opposite of what the claim predicts. A finding that only changes the topic does nothing.
```

## Weaken the bridge argument

```quiz
Millbrook tripled in size from 1850 to 1870. Historian Clara Osei argues that the main cause was a bridge built in 1851, which let farmers reach the town's market.
Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken Osei's argument?
- Millbrook's market sold mostly grain and wool. :: What was sold does not say why the town grew.
- The bridge's stone came from a nearby quarry. :: The stone's source says nothing about growth.
* Millbrook grew just as fast in the twenty years before the bridge.
> Same growth with no bridge, so the bridge is not needed to explain it.
```

# Chains and rivals
Test the end of a chain, or compare two rivals.

## Test the last link

A long hypothesis is a chain. Shrink it to its **end result**, then ask what would break it.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--chain
```

```example
Ecologist Pia Gomes proposes that mangrove roots slow water, so mud settles around them, and the mud slowly raises the coast. Which finding, if true, would most directly undermine her hypothesis?
Choices: Mangrove roots are thicker in muddy bays. / Coasts with mangroves rose no faster than similar coasts without them.
1. Chain: roots → mud settles → coast rises :: Three links in a row.
2. End result: mangroves raise the coast :: Shrink the chain to its end.
3. (A) fits the chain; it breaks no link :: Roots can be thick and the chain holds.
4. Answer: (B) :: No extra rise breaks the result.
```

```more
A finding that only fits the chain (thicker roots) breaks no link, so it does not weaken the hypothesis. Breaking any link weakens a chain. Testing the end result is usually the most direct check. *Directly* in the question is a hint.
```

## Support A over B

```quiz
Biologists disagree about why frogs vanished from the valley's ponds. Some blame pollution. Others blame a fungus that infects frogs' skin.
Which finding, if true, would most directly support the fungus explanation over the pollution explanation?
- Polluted ponds had fewer frogs than clean ponds even before the decline. :: That supports the pollution explanation.
- The fungus dies at high temperatures. :: It does not say why the frogs vanished.
* Frogs vanished from clean ponds as well as polluted ones, and the fungus was found in all of them.
> Pollution cannot explain missing frogs in clean ponds, but the fungus can.
```

# Quotations
The quote must show every part of the claim.

## Match every part

Put each quotation in plain words. It must show **every part** of the claim.

```visual
command-of-evidence-textual--match-words
```

```example
Claim: in the story, Mr. Hale is generous but hides it. Which quotation illustrates this?
Choices: "He gave freely to every neighbor in need." / "He left the coins at night, so none knew the giver."
1. Claim: generous + hides it :: Split the claim into two parts.
2. (A) = he gives openly :: Generous only. Half the claim.
3. (B) = he gives in secret :: Both parts.
4. Answer: (B) :: Every part shown.
```

```more
Split the claim at *and*, *but* or a comma. Then look for words in the quotation that match each part. *Freely* matches generous. *At night, so none knew* matches hides it. If one part has no matching words, cross the choice out.
```

## Find both feelings

```quiz
"Night Train to the City" is a poem by Isabel Varga. The speaker feels both wonder at the city and loneliness there: ______
Which quotation best illustrates the claim?
- "A thousand windows burn like captured stars, / And every street is humming with a song." :: Wonder only. That is half the claim.
* "How bright the towers rise, how proud, how tall! / And yet among their lights I know no one."
- "I walk among the crowds as if through rain, / And not one face turns toward me as I pass." :: Loneliness only. That is half the claim.
> Bright, proud towers: wonder. "I know no one": loneliness. Both parts in one quotation.
```

# Old words, figures
Translate old words and comparisons first.

## Translate the quote

Old or figurative lines say one thing and mean another. Put each choice in plain words, then match the claim.

```example
Claim: in "London" (1794), William Blake's speaker sees sadness in the people he passes. Which quotation best illustrates the claim?
(A) "I wander thro' each charter'd street"
(B) "And mark in every face I meet / Marks of weakness, marks of woe"
1. *mark* = notice; *marks* = signs; *woe* = deep sadness :: Translate first.
2. (A) = he walks the streets :: No sadness. Cross it out.
3. (B) = he notices signs of sadness in every face :: Matches the claim.
4. Answer: (B) :: Sadness in the people he passes.
```

```more
**Figurative** language compares one thing to another, such as "burn like stars". Older English uses words like *thou* (you), *thy* (your), *thro'* (through) and *charter'd* (chartered). Remember too that a character's words are not always the author's own view.
```

## Match the comparison

```quiz
In Sonnet 73, Shakespeare's speaker compares his old age to a tree in late autumn.
Which quotation from the poem best illustrates the claim?
* "That time of year thou mayst in me behold / When yellow leaves, or none, or few, do hang"
- "In me thou see'st the twilight of such day" :: This compares him to the end of a day, not a tree.
- "As after sunset fadeth in the west" :: This is also a day ending. There is no tree.
> *Thou mayst behold* = you may see. Yellow leaves on a tree mean autumn. The others describe a day ending.
```

# Trap: the opposite job
Support or weaken? Reread the verb.

## Reread the verb

Reread the question's verb: **support** or **weaken**? A strong supporter is wrong when the job is weaken.

```example
Claim: a later school start raises grades. Which finding weakens it?
Choices: Grades rose at every school that started later. / Grades fell at schools that started later.
1. Job: weaken :: Read the verb first.
2. (A) = later start, grades up :: This supports the claim. Cross it out.
3. Answer: (B) :: Later start, lower grades. Weakens.
```

```more
Many students pick the choice that matches the claim best, then forget the question asked to weaken it. Circle the verb in the question (*support*, *weaken*, *undermine*, *illustrate*) before you read the choices.
```

## Weaken, don't support

```quiz
Researcher Lena Brandt concluded that a reading app makes children read faster. In her study, 60 children aged 8 to 10 used the app daily for a month, and their reading speed rose by 12% over that month.
Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken her conclusion?
- Children who used the app for two months improved even more. :: Trap: that supports her. The job is weaken.
- Most children said they enjoyed the app. :: Enjoying the app is not the same as reading faster.
* Similar children who did not use the app improved by about the same amount over the month.
> If children improve equally without the app, something else explains the gain.
```
