## What this question looks like

These questions show lines that cross, parallel lines, triangles, or polygons (closed shapes with straight sides). Angle measures may be numbers like 48° or expressions like (3x + 12)°. Typical stems:

- "In the figure, line ℓ is parallel to line m. What is the value of x?"
- "What is the measure, in degrees, of angle BAC?"
- "Triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF. What is the length of DE?"
- "Which additional piece of information is sufficient to prove that the two triangles are congruent?"
- "Each interior angle of a regular polygon measures 150°. How many sides does the polygon have?"

How figures show information: small arrow marks on two lines mean the lines are parallel, small tick marks on sides mean the sides are equal, and a small square in a corner means a right angle (90°). Figures are drawn to scale unless the question says otherwise, but a picture is never a reason by itself. Every equal angle or parallel line must come from the given information or from a rule.

Expect about one or two of these questions per test, in both modules. Questions about similar and congruent triangles are very common: one tutor who compared the official Bluebook practice tests found one on every recent test.

The main sub-types:

- **Angle chasing:** lines that cross, and parallel lines cut by a **transversal** (a line that crosses both).
- **Triangle angles:** the 180° sum, outside (exterior) angles, isosceles and equilateral triangles.
- **Similar triangles:** same shape, different size. You set up a proportion.
- **Congruent triangles and "what proves it" questions.**
- **Polygons:** sums of the inside and outside angles.

## The method

1. **Sketch the figure on scratch paper,** big and clear. Mark parallel lines with arrows, equal sides with ticks and right angles with a small square. Write every given angle on your sketch.
2. **Fill in every angle you can.** For each new angle, name the rule in your head: "vertical angles," "straight line = 180," "parallel lines," "triangle = 180." Then write the number on the sketch.
3. **If the angles are expressions, write one equation from one rule.** Equal angles: set them equal. Angles on a straight line, or same-side interior angles: add them and set the sum equal to 180.
4. **For similar triangles, match the vertices first.** Write the small triangle's letters above the big triangle's letters, in matching order. Then write one ratio with two matching sides. Use whole sides, not pieces.
5. **Answer the exact question.** They may want x, an angle measure, a different angle than the one with x in it, or a side length.
6. **For "which information is sufficient" questions,** test each choice. Does it add a new fact that completes a test like AA or SAS? A fact that is always true, like vertical angles, adds nothing.

> **Key idea:** Every fact you use must come from a rule or from the given information. Never assume lines are parallel, or that two sides are equal, just because they look that way.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Angles at a point and on a line

- Angles on a straight line add up to 180°. Two angles side by side on a line are a **linear pair**.
- Angles all the way around a point add up to 360°.
- **Vertical angles** (the opposite angles where two lines cross) are equal.
- **Complementary** angles add up to 90°. **Supplementary** angles add up to 180°.

### Parallel lines cut by a transversal

When a transversal crosses two parallel lines, it makes 8 angles but only two different sizes.

| Pair of angles | Where they are | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Corresponding | same position at each crossing (for example, both above the line and left of the transversal) | equal |
| Alternate interior | between the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal (a Z shape) | equal |
| Alternate exterior | outside the parallel lines, on opposite sides of the transversal | equal |
| Same-side interior | between the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal (a C shape) | add up to 180° |

Shortcut: all the "big" angles are equal, all the "small" angles are equal, and big + small = 180°.

It also works backward: if corresponding angles or alternate interior angles are equal, then the lines **are** parallel. Vertical angles and linear pairs are true for any two crossing lines, so they never prove that lines are parallel.

**A bent path between parallel lines.** If a path goes from one parallel line to a point between the lines and then to the other line, draw a third line through that point, parallel to the other two. This splits the angle at the point into two pieces, and each piece is an alternate interior angle that you can find.

### Triangles

- The three angles add up to 180°. In a right triangle, the two smaller angles add up to 90°.
- **Exterior angle:** extend one side of the triangle. The outside angle equals the sum of the two inside angles that are not next to it.
- **Isosceles triangle:** two equal sides. The angles opposite those sides are equal. This works backward too: two equal angles mean the sides opposite them are equal.
- **Equilateral triangle:** all three sides are equal and all three angles are 60°.
- The longest side is opposite the largest angle, and the shortest side is opposite the smallest angle.
- **Triangle inequality:** any two sides add up to more than the third side. If two sides are 5 and 9, the third side is more than 9 − 5 = 4 and less than 9 + 5 = 14.
- Area = ½ × base × height, where the height is perpendicular to the base.

### Polygons

For a polygon with n sides:

- The interior angles add up to (n − 2) × 180°.
- In a **regular** polygon (all sides equal and all angles equal), each interior angle is (n − 2) × 180° ÷ n.
- The exterior angles, one at each corner, always add up to 360°. In a regular polygon, each one is 360° ÷ n.
- At each corner, interior angle + exterior angle = 180°.

| Polygon | Sides | Sum of interior angles | Each angle if regular |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triangle | 3 | 180° | 60° |
| Quadrilateral | 4 | 360° | 90° |
| Pentagon | 5 | 540° | 108° |
| Hexagon | 6 | 720° | 120° |
| Octagon | 8 | 1,080° | 135° |

### Similar triangles

**Similar** triangles have the same shape. All three angles match, and every side is multiplied by the same number k. We write triangle ABC ∼ triangle DEF.

- **The order of the letters tells you which parts match.** A matches D, B matches E, and C matches F. So AB/DE = BC/EF = AC/DF.
- **AA:** if two angles of one triangle equal two angles of the other, the triangles are similar. This is the test you will use most often.
- **SSS similarity:** all three pairs of sides have the same ratio. **SAS similarity:** two pairs of sides have the same ratio, and the angles between those sides are equal.
- **A segment parallel to one side** cuts off a small triangle that is similar to the big one. If DE ∥ BC, with D on AB and E on AC, then triangle ADE ∼ triangle ABC, so AD/AB = AE/AC = DE/BC.
- **Pieces shortcut:** the parallel segment also cuts the two sides in the same ratio, so AD/DB = AE/EC. This works only for the two sides that are cut. For DE and BC, always use whole sides.
- **Bow-tie shape:** two triangles that meet at one point share a pair of vertical angles. If one more pair of angles is equal (for example, because the other two sides are parallel), the triangles are similar.
- **Shadows:** a pole and its shadow, and a person and their shadow at the same moment, make similar right triangles.
- With a scale factor k, sides and perimeters are multiplied by k, areas by k², and the angles don't change.

### Congruent triangles

**Congruent** triangles have the same shape and the same size: all matching sides and all matching angles are equal. The symbol is ≅, and the order of the letters matters here too.

| Test | What you need |
|---|---|
| SSS | three pairs of equal sides |
| SAS | two pairs of equal sides and the angles between them |
| ASA | two pairs of equal angles and the sides between them |
| AAS | two pairs of equal angles and one pair of equal sides that are not between them |
| HL | two right triangles with equal hypotenuses and one pair of equal legs |

Not enough: AAA (equal angles prove only that the triangles are similar) and SSA (the angle is not between the two sides).

> **Remember:** Read a congruence or similarity statement letter by letter. If triangle JKL ≅ triangle PQR, then KL = QR and angle L = angle R, even when the figure shows the second triangle turned or flipped.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Parallel lines and the angle they ask for

In the figure, line ℓ is parallel to line m, and line ℓ is above line m. Line t crosses both lines. Where t crosses ℓ, the angle above ℓ and to the left of t measures (5x − 20)°. Where t crosses m, the angle above m and to the left of t measures (3x + 16)°. What is the measure, in degrees, of the angle below m and to the left of t?

- (A) 18
- (B) 70
- (C) 95
- (D) 110

**Solution.** Both given angles sit in the same position (above the line, left of t), so they are corresponding angles, and corresponding angles are equal: 5x − 20 = 3x + 16. Then 2x = 36, so x = 18. Each of those angles measures 3(18) + 16 = 70°. The angle below m and to the left of t forms a straight line with the 70° angle above m, so it measures 180° − 70° = 110°. Choice (A) is x, not an angle. Choice (B) is the angle above m, not the one asked for. Choice (C) comes from adding the two given angles to make 180°, which is the rule for same-side interior angles, not corresponding angles.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 2: An isosceles triangle inside a triangle

In triangle ABC, point D lies on side BC so that AD = BD. The measure of angle ABC is 34°, and the measure of angle ACB is 58°. What is the measure of angle DAC?

- (A) 34°
- (B) 54°
- (C) 68°
- (D) 88°

**Solution.** Because AD = BD, triangle ABD is isosceles. The angles opposite the equal sides are equal: angle BAD is opposite side BD, and angle ABD is opposite side AD. So angle BAD = 34°. The whole angle BAC is 180° − 34° − 58° = 88°. So angle DAC = 88° − 34° = 54°. Check another way: angle ADC is an exterior angle of triangle ABD, so it equals 34° + 34° = 68°, and then in triangle ADC, angle DAC = 180° − 68° − 58° = 54°. Choice (A) is angle BAD, choice (C) is angle ADC, and choice (D) is the whole angle BAC. Each one is a step on the way, not the answer.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 3: Angles of a regular polygon

Each interior angle of a regular polygon measures 160°. How many sides does the polygon have?

*Student-produced response: enter your answer.*

**Solution.** Use the exterior angles, because they always add up to 360°. Each exterior angle is 180° − 160° = 20°. So the number of sides is 360 ÷ 20 = 18. Check with the interior formula: (18 − 2) × 180° ÷ 18 = 16 × 10° = 160°. It matches.

**Answer:** 18

### Example 4: A segment parallel to one side

In triangle ABC, point D lies on side AB and point E lies on side AC, so that segment DE is parallel to side BC. AD = 5, DB = 10, and AE = 6. What is the length of EC?

- (A) 3
- (B) 9
- (C) 12
- (D) 18

**Solution.** Because DE ∥ BC, triangle ADE ∼ triangle ABC: they share angle A, and the parallel lines make angle ADE equal to angle ABC. Match small to big with whole sides: AD/AB = AE/AC. The whole side AB is 5 + 10 = 15, so 5/15 = 6/AC, and AC = 18. Then EC = AC − AE = 18 − 6 = 12. The pieces shortcut gives the same result: AD/DB = AE/EC, so 5/10 = 6/EC, and EC = 12. Choice (D) is the whole side AC, not EC. Choice (A) flips one of the two ratios (5/10 = EC/6). Choice (B) mixes a piece with a whole side (10/15 = 6/EC).

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 5: What proves the triangles are similar?

Segments AD and BE intersect at point C. Point C lies between A and D, and between B and E, so triangles ABC and DEC meet only at point C. Which additional piece of information is sufficient to prove that triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEC?

- (A) The measure of angle BAC is equal to the measure of angle EDC.
- (B) AC = DC
- (C) AB is twice as long as DE.
- (D) The measure of angle ACB is equal to the measure of angle DCE.

**Solution.** Angles ACB and DCE are vertical angles, so they are always equal. That gives one pair of equal angles for free. The AA test needs one more pair. Choice (A) supplies it: angle A matches angle D. With two pairs of equal angles, triangle ABC ∼ triangle DEC. Choice (D) only restates the vertical angles, which were already true, so it adds nothing. Choices (B) and (C) each describe just one pair of sides. Similarity from sides needs two side ratios with the angle between them (SAS similarity) or all three ratios.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 6: Similar triangles that overlap

In triangle ABC, point D lies on side AC. The measure of angle ABD is equal to the measure of angle ACB. If AB = 6 and AC = 9, what is the length of AD?

- (A) 3
- (B) 4
- (C) 6
- (D) 13.5

**Solution.** Compare the small triangle ABD with the big triangle ACB. They share angle A, and the question says angle ABD = angle ACB. By AA, triangle ABD ∼ triangle ACB, in exactly this order: A matches A, B matches C, and D matches B. Sketch the two triangles side by side, turned so that matching angles line up. Now pair the sides in the same order: AB (small) goes with AC (big), and AD (small) goes with AB (big). So AD/AB = AB/AC, which is AD/6 = 6/9. Then AD = 36/9 = 4. Choice (D) pairs the wrong sides, and it can't be right anyway, because AD is part of AC and must be shorter than 9. Choice (A) is just 9 − 6. Choice (C) assumes AD = AB without a reason.

**Answer:** (B)

## Traps to avoid

- **Trusting the picture.** Lines that look parallel and sides that look equal are not, unless the question says so or a rule proves it.
- **Equal vs supplementary.** Corresponding and alternate interior angles are equal. Same-side interior angles add up to 180°. Mixing them up gives a wrong x every time.
- **Stopping at x.** Many questions ask for an angle measure, or for a different angle than the one that contains x.
- **Pieces instead of whole sides.** With a segment parallel to one side, compare a side of the small triangle with a whole side of the big triangle. The pieces shortcut works only on the two sides that are cut.
- **Wrong matching in similar triangles.** Use the letter order of the similarity statement, not the position on the page. Overlapping triangles are often turned or flipped.
- **The wrong pair of equal angles.** In an isosceles triangle, the equal angles are the ones opposite the equal sides.
- **Choices that are always true.** In "sufficient to prove" questions, vertical angles or a side the triangles share add no new information.
- **AAA for congruence.** Equal angles prove that triangles are similar, not congruent. Congruence needs at least one pair of equal sides.

## Quick checklist

- Did I redraw the figure and mark parallel lines, equal sides and right angles?
- Did I name a rule for every angle I wrote down?
- Parallel lines: equal or supplementary?
- Similar triangles: did I match vertices by letter order and use whole sides?
- Did I answer the exact question: x, an angle, or a side?
- "Sufficient" questions: does the choice add new information?
