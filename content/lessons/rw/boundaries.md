# Sentence or fragment?
Subject plus verb, and it makes sense alone.

```visual
boundaries--clause-test
```

## Key ideas

Every boundary question starts with one move: **cut the text at the blank and read each side alone.**

A **full sentence** (the official SAT term is *independent clause*) has a subject, a verb, and makes sense on its own: *The ferry left.* A **fragment** is missing a piece: *After the ferry left.* *Leaving the dock at dawn.*

Ask about each side:
- **Left side a full sentence? Right side a full sentence?**
- **Yes and yes:** two sentences meet at the blank. They need a period, a semicolon, or a comma with a FANBOYS word, or a colon or dash when the second explains the first (see Colon and dash). A bare comma or no mark glues them wrongly.
- **One side is a fragment:** it must hook onto the other side. No period, no semicolon.

Beware an opening phrase: *Built into the cliff, the temple has survived.* The phrase alone is a fragment, but the whole line is a full sentence, because *the temple has survived* carries the subject and the verb.

```example
The harbor froze in January ______ the ferries stopped running.
Choices: January. The / January, the / January the
1. Left: "The harbor froze in January" :: Subject harbor, verb froze: a full sentence.
2. Right: "the ferries stopped running" :: Subject ferries, verb stopped: also a full sentence.
3. Answer: January. The :: Two full sentences need a period. A comma or nothing glues them.
```

```theory
**Subject:** who or what the sentence is about. **Verb:** what the subject does or is. A clause needs both, and the verb must be able to stand alone as the main verb: *The ferry left* works, but *the ferry leaving* does not, because *leaving* needs a helper (*was leaving*).

Four common fragments:
- **No verb:** *The old lighthouse on the point.*
- **A verb form that cannot be the main verb:** *Waiting for the tide.* *To reach the island.*
- **A subordinating word in front:** *Because the tide was low.* (More on these later in the lesson.)
- **A relative word in front:** *Which the crew had repaired.*

Subjects hide. A subject is never inside a phrase that starts with *of, in, on, to, with*. In *The books on the top shelf belong to Maya*, the subject is *books*, not *shelf*. Long subjects still count: *That the old lighthouse survived the storm surprised everyone* is a full sentence. The subject is the whole *that* clause and the verb is *surprised*.

Two names appear in official explanations. A **comma splice** is two full sentences joined by only a comma. A **run-on** (fused sentence) is two full sentences with nothing between them. Both are fixed the same way, which the next subtopic covers.

On the SAT the prompt reads "conforms to the conventions of Standard English." The choices usually differ only in the mark, so the whole question is: which mark fits what stands on each side?

**Trap:** do not choose a mark because you would pause there when reading aloud. Choose it from the grammar of the two sides.
```

# Join two sentences
Period, semicolon, or comma plus FANBOYS. Never a bare comma.

```visual
boundaries--fanboys
```

## Key ideas

Two full sentences need a real boundary. Three are safe:
- **Period:** *The harbor froze. The ferries stopped.*
- **Semicolon** (it works like a period): *The harbor froze; the ferries stopped.*
- **Comma + FANBOYS:** *The harbor froze, so the ferries stopped.*

**FANBOYS** are the seven joining words: **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o.

Wrong every time: a comma alone (a **comma splice**) or no mark at all (a **run-on**).

Two traps:
- A comma goes before FANBOYS only when a full sentence follows. *The harbor froze and stopped the ferries* has one subject with two verbs: no comma.
- Between two sentences, never put a period or semicolon right before a FANBOYS word: *The harbor froze; and the ferries stopped* is wrong.

A semicolon also separates list items that already contain commas: *Oslo, Norway; Lima, Peru; and Perth, Australia.*

```example
Our flight was delayed two hours ______ we missed the last train.
Choices: hours, / hours, so / hours so
1. Left: "Our flight was delayed" :: A full sentence.
2. Right: "we missed the last train" :: Also a full sentence.
3. Answer: hours, so :: Comma plus FANBOYS joins two sentences. A bare comma splices them.
```

```theory
A **comma splice** is two full sentences joined by a comma alone. A **run-on** is two full sentences with no mark and no joining word. Both are errors, and the SAT often puts both in the choices as wrong answers.

**What each FANBOYS word does.** *For* means because. *And* adds. *Nor* means and not. *But* and *yet* contrast. *Or* gives a choice. *So* gives a result. On the SAT, *and*, *but* and *so* are the common ones; *or* and *nor* are rare.

**Semicolon rules.** A semicolon must have a full sentence on both sides, and the two ideas should be closely related. Think of it as a period that keeps two sentences together. Anything that is good for a semicolon is also good for a period, so the SAT will not offer both as correct choices.

**Sneaky FANBOYS.** Look for a long second half that has no subject: *Nina studied the map for an hour and planned the whole route.* One subject (Nina), two verbs: no comma before *and*. If the second half has its own subject (*and she planned the route*), the comma is needed.

**Lists with semicolons.** When each list item already has commas, semicolons separate the items, and a semicolon plus *and* is correct before the last item: *We visited Oslo, Norway; Lima, Peru; and Perth, Australia.* This is the one case where "; and" is fine.

**Related fix.** Colons and dashes can join two sentences too, when the second explains the first. That is the next subtopic.

**Trap:** *so that* is not the FANBOYS *so*. *The bus waited so that we could board* is one sentence with no comma.
```

# Colon and dash
Full sentence first, then the explanation or list.

```visual
boundaries--colon-dash
```

## Key ideas

A **colon** (:) or a **single dash** (—) says: *here comes the detail.* Two rules:
- **Left side: a full sentence.**
- **Right side:** a list, a phrase, or another sentence that explains or proves the first.

*She packed two things: a map and a compass.*
*The plan had one flaw—it cost too much.*

Because the left side is a full sentence, a colon often joins two sentences when the second **explains** the first. If a question's choices have a colon but no period or semicolon, test the colon first.

Never use a colon or dash after an unfinished phrase:
- after a verb: *The kit includes: tape and glue* (wrong)
- after a preposition or *such as*, *like*, *including*: *places such as: Rome* (wrong)

A dash used this way is a single dash. It acts like a colon.

```example
The detective noticed one problem ______ the clock had stopped at noon.
Choices: problem, / problem: / problem and
1. Left: "The detective noticed one problem" :: A full sentence. It promises a detail.
2. Right: "the clock had stopped at noon" :: A full sentence that names the problem.
3. Answer: problem: :: The colon says: here is the explanation.
```

```theory
A colon works like a signpost. Everything before it must be a complete thought, and what comes after it must explain, list or illustrate what was promised: *The recipe has three steps: soak, boil, and serve.*

**What follows can be:**
- a list (*soak, boil, and serve*),
- a single word or phrase (*one answer: yes*),
- a full sentence that explains (*The plan had one flaw: it cost too much*).

**What precedes cannot be a fragment.** *The three steps: soak, boil, serve* is wrong, because *The three steps* is not a sentence. Likewise *The kit includes: tape* is wrong, because *includes* needs its object right away.

**A single dash does the same job as a colon.** *The plan had one flaw—it cost too much.* Do not confuse it with two dashes, which mark extra information in the middle of a sentence (a later subtopic).

**Colon or semicolon?** A semicolon joins two sentences of equal weight. A colon is used when the second sentence explains, proves or details the first. If the second sentence only adds a related idea, it is a semicolon or a period.

**Why the SAT hides the period.** When a colon is correct, a period and a semicolon would also be grammatical, so the SAT rarely offers them next to a correct colon. Do not choose the colon only because it is the odd one out. Check that the first part is a full sentence and the second explains it.

**Trap:** a colon after *such as*, *including*, *like*, or a form of *to be* (*is*, *are*, *was*) almost always cuts a phrase in half.
```

# However and therefore
Not FANBOYS. Semicolon before, comma after.

```visual
boundaries--however-slot
```

## Key ideas

Words like *however, therefore, moreover, instead, otherwise, for example, in fact* are **transitions, not FANBOYS.** A comma before them cannot join two sentences.

Between two full sentences, use:
- a **semicolon** before and a comma after: *The museum was free; however, the line was long.*
- or a **period** before and a comma after: *The museum was free. However, the line was long.*

Never *free, however, the line...* (comma splice) or *free however the line...* (run-on).

The word can also move:
- **Inside one sentence:** two commas. *The line, however, was long.*
- **At the end of the first clause:** a comma before it and a semicolon after. *The museum was free, however; the line was long.* Ask which sentence the word contrasts with.

Quick test: cover the word. If two full sentences remain, treat the spot as a sentence boundary.

```example
The museum was free on Sundays ______ the line was two hours long.
Choices: Sundays, however, / Sundays; however, / Sundays however
1. Left: "The museum was free on Sundays" :: A full sentence.
2. Right: "the line was two hours long" :: Also a full sentence.
3. Answer: Sundays; however, :: Semicolon between the sentences, comma after however.
```

```theory
These words are called **conjunctive adverbs** or transitions. The SAT does not ask for the term, but it asks for the punctuation. They connect ideas like conjunctions do, but they are not conjunctions, so they cannot do the joining themselves. The joining is done by the period or the semicolon.

**More members:** *therefore, thus, consequently, moreover, furthermore, otherwise, meanwhile, then, instead, nevertheless, similarly, likewise*. Phrases behave the same way: *for example, for instance, in fact, on the other hand, in other words*. Even *though* and *rather* can be used like this: *The forecast promised storms. The route was safe, though; the hikers still turned back.*

**Compare with FANBOYS.** *But* is a FANBOYS word: *The museum was free, but the line was long.* *However* means almost the same, yet the punctuation changes: *The museum was free; however, the line was long.*

**Where does the word belong?** In a semicolon spot, the transition is usually the first word of the second sentence. Sometimes the SAT moves it to the end of the first sentence, as in *free, however;*. The deciding question: does the word contrast with the idea before or the idea after? If it only makes sense with the sentence before, it ends that sentence.

**Inside one sentence.** If the words around *however* form one single sentence, use two commas: *The first flight, however, went perfectly.* Check by removing the word; if only one sentence remains, it is an interrupter.

**Trap:** a comma before *however* and nothing after it. *Free, however the line was long* is both a splice and a missing comma.
```

# Dependent clauses
Because, although, which: they need a main sentence.

```visual
boundaries--dependent
```

## Key ideas

A **dependent clause** has a subject and a verb, but one extra word makes it unable to stand alone. That word is:
- a **subordinating conjunction:** because, although, though, while, whereas, when, if, since, after, before, unless, until
- a **relative word:** which, who, where, that

*Although the bridge was old* is not a sentence. It needs a main clause.

Three patterns:
1. **Opener first:** *Although the bridge was old, it held.* A comma after the opener.
2. **Main clause first:** *The bridge held because the steel was new.* Usually no comma.
3. **Extra tail with which or -ing:** *The bridge held, which surprised everyone.* A comma.

Never put a period, semicolon, or colon between a dependent clause and its main clause: that leaves a fragment. And use one joining word per pair: *Although it rained, but we played* is wrong.

```example
Because the mountain pass was closed ______ the bus took the coast road.
Choices: closed. The / closed; the / closed, the
1. Left: "Because the mountain pass was closed" :: Subject and verb, but "because" leaves it unfinished.
2. Right: "the bus took the coast road" :: A full sentence.
3. Answer: closed, the :: Opener, comma, main sentence.
```

```theory
**Why the comma after an opener.** The dependent clause is only a lead-in. The comma tells the reader where the main clause starts: *When the tide went out, the boats leaned over.*

**Main clause first, no comma.** *The boats leaned over when the tide went out.* The *when* clause finishes the sentence and is needed, so it takes no comma. A comma is used before **contrast** words (*although, though, while, whereas*) to show a shift: *The test was easy, although I ran out of time.*

**Which and that.** A **which** clause adds extra information and follows a comma: *The wall, which was built in 1901, still stands.* A **that** clause is essential and takes no comma: *The wall that was built in 1901 still stands.* The blank often sits right before *which* or *that*.

**-ing and with phrases.** A phrase that begins with a participle (*-ing* word) or with *with* attaches to a sentence with a comma: *The river flooded, destroying two bridges.* A period before it leaves the phrase as a fragment.

**Double conjunction trap.** One joining word per pair of clauses: *Although the road was icy, the bus ran* (right). *Although the road was icy, but the bus ran* (wrong).

**Cutting the sentence.** With a long sentence, find the first full main clause, then check whether the rest is another full sentence or only a tail. In *Having left the dock at dawn, the ship set a record*, the main clause is *the ship set a record*; the opening phrase only decorates it.

**Trap:** a semicolon before *because* or *which*: *The bridge held; because the steel was new* leaves a fragment.
```

# Extra information: pairs
Remove it and a sentence survives. Mark both ends.

```visual
boundaries--pairs
```

## Key ideas

**Extra information** can be removed and the sentence still works. Mark it on both ends with a **matching pair**:
- commas: *My cousin, a pilot, lives in Oslo.*
- dashes: *My cousin—a pilot—lives in Oslo.*
- parentheses: *My cousin (a pilot) lives in Oslo.*

Rules:
1. **Same mark on both ends.** A comma never closes a dash.
2. **Remove test.** Delete the extra part. A full sentence must remain.
3. **Parentheses:** no comma before the opening bracket.
4. **At the end of a sentence,** one mark is enough: *She called Dr. Lin, a surgeon.*

**Names:** if the name is needed to know who is meant, use no commas: *the poet Maya Angelou*. If the name only adds to a unique person, use two commas: *My sister, Rosa, lives in Oslo.* One comma alone in the middle of a sentence is always wrong.

```example
My neighbor, a retired ______ waters the garden every morning.
Choices: pilot / pilot, / pilot—
1. Remove the detail: "My neighbor waters the garden every morning." :: A full sentence still stands.
2. The detail opened with a comma :: Close it with a comma too.
3. Answer: pilot, :: Two commas make the pair.
```

```theory
**Essential or extra?** *Essential* information tells which one you mean; take it away and the meaning changes. *Extra* (nonessential) information only adds detail. In *The poet Maya Angelou wrote it*, the name is essential because *The poet* alone does not say who. In *Maya Angelou, a poet, wrote it*, the phrase *a poet* is extra.

**The matching rule.** The SAT tests that the opening and the closing mark match. Pairs: comma + comma, dash + dash, bracket + bracket. A mixed pair (*, ... —*) is always wrong, and so is a single mark in the middle (*My cousin, a pilot lives in Oslo*).

**Brackets.** Never put a comma before the opening bracket, and never close a bracket with a comma or dash that was supposed to match. After the closing bracket, put whatever the sentence needs: *The city (population 40,000) has two hospitals.* A bracket can sit inside a dash pair: *three territories—New Granada (now Colombia), Venezuela, and Quito (now Ecuador)—won independence.* The bracket closes first, then the dash closes the pair.

**One mark at the end.** When the extra information is last, only one mark is needed, because the period closes it: *She called Dr. Lin, a surgeon.* A single dash is also fine in that spot. Do not add a second comma before the period.

**Phrases that interrupt.** Extra information can be an appositive (a noun phrase renaming the noun), a *which* or *who* clause, a phrase starting with *-ing*, or an aside. All use the same pairs.

**Trap:** a comma that separates a name from its title when the name is needed (*the botanist, Lena Marsh*) or only one comma in the middle (*Lena Marsh, a botanist led the team*).
```

# Trap: needless marks
No marks between partners. Question marks: direct questions only.

```visual
boundaries--no-marks
```

## Key ideas

Some words belong together. Put **no mark** between:
- **subject and verb:** *The books on the top shelf belong to Maya.*
- **verb and its object:** *Lena collected ferns.*
- **preposition and its object:** *in the forest*
- **a needed title and the name:** *the botanist Lena Marsh*

A long subject or a modifier in between does not change this. Ignore it and find the real verb.

**End marks.** A **direct question** has the verb before the subject and ends with a question mark: *How did they escape?* An **indirect question** sits inside a statement, keeps normal word order (subject, then verb), and ends with a period: *The team asked how they escaped.*

If the sentence tells **what someone asked, wondered, or tested**, it is a statement.

```example
The ranger asked how many hikers were on the trail ______
Choices: trail. / trail? / trail!
1. Frame: "The ranger asked" :: A statement that reports a question.
2. Order: "how many hikers were" :: Subject before verb: indirect question.
3. Answer: trail. :: A statement ends with a period.
```

```theory
**Why no mark between subject and verb?** A mark is a boundary or an interruption. Subject and verb are one team: nothing should split them. The SAT hides this by making the subject long (*The books on the top shelf of the library*) and offering a comma, dash, or colon at the end of it.

**Other pairs that stay together:** an adjective and its noun, a verb and its object (*had collected more than four hundred species*), a preposition and its object, and a *that*-clause and the word it completes. After *such as*, *including* and *like*, a colon is also wrong.

**When no mark is not the answer.** If a real boundary or an interruption exists, a mark is needed. The "no mark" choice is right when the words simply belong together.

**Direct and indirect questions.** A direct question stands alone: *What was her favorite book?* An indirect question is part of a longer statement: *She asked what her favorite book was.* The signal is a framing verb such as *asked, wondered, wanted to know, investigated, determined*, followed by *how, what, why, whether*. After that verb the subject comes first and the verb second.

**SAT wording.** In the choices you see the same words with a question mark, a period, or an exclamation mark, and with different word orders. First decide which order (*could these penguins become* or *these penguins could become*) the sentence needs. Then match the end mark.

**Exclamation marks** are rarely right on the SAT. **Quotation marks** mostly appear around titles or direct speech, and the comma before a direct quote follows the same logic: it separates the speaker from the words.

**Trap:** a question that is only reported, not asked. If the writer reports what someone asked, the sentence is a statement and ends with a period.
```
