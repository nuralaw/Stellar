# Trap: one main verb
One subject, one main verb. Count them first.

```visual
form-structure-sense--verb-slot
```

## Key ideas
Every sentence needs a **main verb**: a verb with a tense that can stand as the action of a full sentence (*opens*, *opened*, *has opened*). Count the main verbs before you look at the choices.

- **A main verb is already in the sentence?** Then the blank takes a form that only describes: an *-ing* word, *to* + verb, or an *-ed* word that means "that was ___".
- **No main verb yet?** Then the blank must be one.
- *-ing* alone and *to* + verb can **never** be the main verb. An *-ed* form can be either, so look at the rest of the sentence.
- Two main verbs for one subject, joined by *and*, take the same form: *would raise ... and create*.

Choices that mix *opens*, *opening* and *to open* are testing exactly this.

```example
The telescope ______ in 1990 still sends back images.
Choices: launched / launches / launching
1. Main verbs: *sends* :: One is already there, after the blank.
2. Blank = a describing form :: A second main verb would break the sentence.
3. Not *launching* :: It says the telescope did the launching.
4. Answer: launched :: It means "that was launched in 1990".
```

```theory
**Finite and nonfinite.** A *finite* verb has a tense and a subject: *opens, opened, has opened, is opening, will open*. It can be the main verb. The describing forms are *nonfinite*: an *-ing* word (*opening*), *to* + verb (*to open*), and a participle (*opened*, *taken*) used as an adjective. The College Board calls these forms verbals.

**Why the blank matters.** Two main verbs for one subject, with only a comma between them, make a comma splice. No main verb at all makes a fragment. The SAT hides the verb: *The bridge, which connects the two islands, ______ in 1932.* *Connects* belongs to the *which* clause, so the sentence still needs its own main verb: *opened*.

**The -ed form does two jobs.** *The bridge opened in 1932* uses *opened* as the main verb. *The bridge opened in 1932 is still in use* uses it to describe the bridge, and *is* is the main verb. Count the verbs in the whole sentence to decide.

**A describing phrase after a full sentence.** A comma plus a describing phrase is correct: *The crew sailed at dawn, their ship reaching port by noon.* A main verb in that spot would be a comma splice.

**-ing as a subject** (*Swimming is good exercise*) is fine, but the sentence still needs a main verb after it.

**Method.** Cross out the blank, find the main verbs left. None: choose a main verb. One: choose a describing form.
```

# Subject and verb match
Find the true subject. Ignore the extras.

```visual
form-structure-sense
```

## Key ideas
The verb must match its **subject** in number, not the noun nearest to the blank. Cross out what cannot hold the subject, then read what is left.

- **Prepositional phrases** (*of, in, with, for, among*) never hold the subject. Neither do extras between commas or dashes, even *along with* or *not X*.
- **Each, every, one, either, neither** are singular, even before *of the...*.
- **A group** (*team, board, committee, jury, class*) is singular.
- **A and B** is plural. With *A or B* and *neither A nor B*, the nearer noun decides.
- **An -ing phrase as subject** is singular.
- **Flipped order:** after *there is/are* or a place phrase, the subject comes after the verb. Put it first to hear the verb.

```example
One of the oldest maps in the museum's collection, drawn in 1540, ______ torn at the edges.
Choices: is / are
1. Cross out: *of the oldest maps in the museum's collection* :: A prepositional phrase never holds the subject.
2. Cross out: *drawn in 1540* :: An extra between commas.
3. Left: *One ______ torn* :: *One* is singular.
4. Answer: is
```

```theory
**Subject and verb.** The subject is who or what the clause is about. In the present tense a singular subject takes *is, has* or a verb ending in -s (*the map is, it has, she walks*), and a plural takes *are, have* and no -s. In the past only *was* and *were* change, so a past-tense question usually offers those two.

**What is never the subject.** Anything inside a prepositional phrase (*of, in, on, with, for, among, between, by*), a phrase with *along with* or *as well as*, and anything between a pair of commas or dashes. Cross these out first. A *who, which* or *that* clause, or an *-ing* phrase right after a noun, is also an extra: *The person watering the plants **lives** here.*

**Words that fool.** *Each, every, one, either, neither, anyone, everyone* are singular, even in *each of the maps*. *The number of* is singular, *a number of* is plural. With *some, most, all, half* or a percent, the noun after *of* decides: *Half of the maps **are** faded; half of the water **is** gone.*

**Groups.** The SAT counts *team, board, committee, jury, class, staff, company* as one thing and wants a singular verb. The pronoun that follows is *it* or *its*, never *they*.

**Joined subjects.** *A and B* is plural. In *either A or B* and *neither A nor B*, the noun closer to the verb decides. *Not X* and *rather than X* are extras.

**Flipped order.** After *there is/are*, *here is/are*, or an opening such as *Beside the lake* or *Among the findings*, the subject follows the verb: *Among the leaders **was** Ruffin.* Move the subject to the front and the right verb is easy to hear.
```

# Tense and consistency
Time clues and neighboring verbs set the tense.

```visual
form-structure-sense--timeline
```

## Key ideas
Choose the tense that fits the **time clues** and the **verbs around the blank**.

- **Match the neighbors.** Read the other verbs in the passage. A story told in the past stays in the past; a general fact stays in the present. Switch only when a clue says so.
- *Since 2010, for years, so far* → **present perfect**: *has/have* + past participle. It runs from then until now.
- *By the time* + a past event → **past perfect**: *had* + past participle. It is the earlier event.
- *In 1990, last year* → simple past. *Every day*, general facts, and what a book or film says → present. *Next year* → future.
- **Take the simplest tense that fits.** Choose *had* or *has* only when a clue demands it.

```example
Since 2010, the museum ______ over a million visitors.
Choices: welcomes / has welcomed / welcomed
1. Clue: *Since 2010* :: From the past up to now.
2. Now is included :: Not a finished past event.
3. Answer: has welcomed :: *Has* + past participle.
```

```theory
**The forms.** Present: *walks*. Simple past: *walked*. Future: *will walk*. Present perfect: *has/have walked*. Past perfect: *had walked*. The **past participle** is the form after *has, have* or *had* (*walked, taken, gone*). Forms like *is walking* show an action in progress and are rarely right unless the sentence says *at that moment*.

**Perfect tenses.** The present perfect joins the past to now: *since 2010, for decades, so far, ever since*. The past perfect shows the earlier of two past events: *By the time the storm hit, the crew **had moved** the ship.* *By the time* is the one dependable signal. After *before* and *after* either the simple past or the past perfect can be right, so the SAT will not ask you to choose between them.

**Consistency.** Read the verbs around the blank, in the same sentence and the sentences before it. A passage in the past keeps the past. A summary of a book, film or general fact stays in the present: *Elizabeth dislikes Darcy at first, but she later changes her mind.* The verbs next to the blank will confirm it. A shift is correct only when a time word signals it: *last year* is past, *next year* is future, *today* is present.

**The simplest tense wins.** Long forms like *will have been studying* or *would have studied* are almost never the answer unless the time words ask for them. If one choice is the plain tense and nothing signals an earlier or later time, take it.

**Common traps.** A past date after the blank (*in 1990*) rules out *has/have*. *Would* does not replace a plain past event. Tense questions also hide agreement (*has* or *have*), so fix the tense first, then check the subject.
```

# Pronouns: agree and case
Find the noun it replaces, then match.

```visual
form-structure-sense--pronoun-arrow
```

## Key ideas
A **pronoun** replaces a noun, its **antecedent**. Find the antecedent, then match it.

- **Number.** A singular noun takes *it, its, he, she*. A plural takes *they, their, them*. A company, a city, a brain or a piece of writing is *it*.
- **Stay consistent.** Do not hop between *one, you, we* and *they*. Use the one the noun needs.
- **Clear reference.** If two nouns could be the antecedent, or none, the pronoun is wrong.
- **Case.** *I, he, she, they, who* do the action. *Me, him, her, them, whom* receive it, or follow a preposition (*between you and **me***). Test: *who* = *he*, *whom* = *him*.
- **Who** is for people, **which** for things, *whose* for both.

The nearest plural noun is often the trap. It is not always the antecedent.

```example
Visitors praised the new trail map, saying ______ is much clearer than the old one.
Choices: they / it / those
1. Ask: what is clearer? :: The map, not the visitors.
2. map = one thing :: A singular noun needs a singular pronoun.
3. Answer: it :: *They* would mean the visitors.
```

```theory
**Terms.** A pronoun stands in for a noun. The noun it replaces is the **antecedent**. *Number* is singular or plural. *Person* is who is speaking: *I/we* (first), *you* (second), *he, she, it, they, one* (third). Do not shift person inside one sentence: *When customers wait, **you** get bored* is wrong. Use *they*.

**Agreement.** Count the antecedent, not the nearest noun: *The scientists' report was clear, and **it** led to new rules* refers to the report. Companies, schools, cities, boards and books are single things: *it, its*. Pronouns for the noun after *of* are tricky: *one of the maps lost **its** color* keeps the singular.

**Clear reference.** *When Maya met Jo, she smiled* could mean either person, so the SAT treats it as an error. The right choice usually names the noun or rewrites the sentence. A pronoun with no noun at all (*Last year they raised prices*) is also wrong.

**Case.** A subject pronoun does the action, an object pronoun receives it. Two tricks. Cross out the extra person: *Maria and (I/me) went* becomes *I went*; *between you and (I/me)* is *between you and me* because *between* is a preposition. For who/whom, use the he/him test: if *he* fits the blank, use *who*; if *him* fits, use *whom*. A quick shortcut is to look at the next word (a verb usually means *who*; a noun or pronoun, as in *whom the judges chose*, usually means *whom*), but it can fail: in *a ceramicist who the judges said was original*, the next word is a noun and *who* is still correct. After a preposition, *whom* is correct: *to whom*, *with whom*.

**Relative pronouns.** *Who* and *whom* refer to people, *which* to things, *that* to either. *Whose* shows ownership for people or things, and never has an apostrophe.

**Reflexives.** *Themselves, itself* are used only when the same noun is also the subject: *Barnacles attach **themselves** to shells.*
```

# Plural or possessive
Count the owners, then place the apostrophe.

```visual
form-structure-sense--apostrophes
```

## Key ideas
Three jobs, three spellings. Decide which job the word does.

- **Plural** (more than one, nothing owned): *girls*. No apostrophe.
- **One owner:** add *'s*. *The girl's book.*
- **Several owners, plural ends in s:** add *'* after the s. *The girls' books.* A plural without an s adds *'s*: *children's*.
- **Owning pronouns** never take an apostrophe: *its, their, whose, hers, ours*.
- **Contractions** do, because letters are missing: *it's* = *it is* or *it has*; *they're* = *they are*; *who's* = *who is*.

**Test:** read the contraction in full. *The library reopened it is rooftop garden* makes no sense, so use *its*.

Two nouns in a row (*the poet's early work*) often mean the first owns the second.

```example
The two ______ lockers are on the second floor.
Choices: students / student's / students'
1. "Two" :: More than one owner.
2. The lockers belong to them :: Owning needs an apostrophe.
3. Answer: students' :: Several owners: apostrophe after the s.
```

```theory
**Plural, possessive, contraction.** A plural noun just means more than one (*two maps*). A possessive noun shows ownership (*the map's edge*). A contraction joins two words and drops letters (*it's* is *it is*). The same sounds appear in all three, which is why the SAT tests them together.

**Where the apostrophe goes.** Say *of the...* to find the owner: *the books of the girls* is *the girls' books*. One owner puts the apostrophe before the s, several owners ending in s put it after the s. Plurals that don't end in s (*children, women, people*) work like singulars: *the children's books*.

**Separate or joint.** If each owner has its own thing, both nouns are possessive: *rock's and rap's ratios* (each genre has its own). If they share one thing, only the last noun is possessive: *Priya and Dev's lab*.

**Look for two nouns together.** When one noun directly follows another (*the brain's neurons, the school's lockers*), the first is usually the owner. If the choices drop an apostrophe in one slot, check each blank separately: *the photographer's early photos* has an owner (apostrophe) and a plain plural (none).

**Look-alike words.** *Its/it's, their/there/they're, whose/who's, your/you're.* The owning forms (*its, their, whose, your*) have no apostrophe. And pronouns agree with their noun: one library, *its*; two libraries, *their*.

**Plain plurals take no apostrophe**, even for numbers and acronyms: *the 1990s*, *two CEOs*.
```

# Modifiers in place
The opening phrase describes the subject next to it.

```visual
form-structure-sense--modifier
```

## Key ideas
A **modifier** adds detail and must sit next to what it describes. A phrase at the start of the sentence, before a comma, describes the **subject** that comes right after the comma.

Ask: **who or what** is doing what the phrase says?

- If the subject after the comma can't be that, the modifier **dangles**.
- *Reading the letter, a smile spread across Maya's face.* A smile can't read. Fix: *Reading the letter, Maya began to smile.*
- A **possessive** is not the subject. In *Raised in Chile, the poet's work is...*, the subject is *work*, and work wasn't raised.
- The subject may start with a possessive if it is the right thing: *Named a landmark in 1999, **Julian's synthesis** led to...* The synthesis was named.
- Keep *only, just* and describing phrases next to the word they describe.

In the choices, look at what comes first after the comma. That decides it.

```example
Founded by a retired nurse in 1987, ______
Choices: the clinic now treats 3,000 patients each year. / 3,000 patients now visit the clinic each year. / a new wing was added to treat 3,000 patients each year.
1. Ask: what was founded? :: A clinic, not patients or a wing.
2. The noun after the comma must be the clinic :: It is the thing that was founded.
3. Answer: the clinic now treats 3,000 patients each year.
```

```theory
**Terms.** A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes something. An **introductory phrase** comes before the first comma: *Reading the letter,* or *After a long drought,* or *A former sailor,*. A **dangling modifier** is one whose describing target is missing or wrong. A **misplaced modifier** is in the right sentence but in the wrong spot.

**The rule.** The subject of the main clause must come right after the comma, and it must be what the opening phrase describes. This is the most dependable fix on the SAT: look at what the choices put first after the comma and ask whether that thing can do the action.

**How the SAT hides it.** The wrong choices put an object, a date, a place or an idea there (*1891 and 1909 were the years...*). Passive voice also hides the real doer: *Hoping to attract visitors, a festival was launched* has no one hoping. And when a choice begins with a possessive (*the poet's early work*), the real subject is the noun after it, *work*. If that noun is not what the phrase describes, the choice is wrong. The possessive itself does not rescue it.

**Appositives.** *A former sailor, Dana runs the dock* works: the noun right after the comma renames the opening phrase. *A former sailor, the dock is run by Dana* does not.

**Misplaced modifiers.** *Only, just, nearly* and describing phrases belong next to the word they affect. *She nearly drove four hours* means she did not drive at all.

**Other modifier traps.** A phrase beginning with *who* or *which* describes the noun just before it. If the noun before it is wrong, the choice is wrong.
```

# Parallel and logical comparisons
Compare like with like. Keep pairs in one form.

```visual
form-structure-sense--parallel
```

## Key ideas
**Parallel structure:** items in a list, or joined by *and, or* or *but*, take the **same form**.

- Same verb form: *The lock would increase the salinity and **create** a barrier* (not *creates* or *creating*).
- Same type: all nouns, all *-ing* words, or all *to* verbs: *to read, to write, and to edit*.
- Pairs (*both ... and, either ... or, not only ... but also, neither ... nor*) need the same form after each half.

**Compare like with like.** A comparison must set two things of the same kind side by side.

*The population of Oslo is larger than **that of** Bergen.* A population compares with *that* (a population), not with *Bergen* (a city). Use *that of* for one thing and *those of* for several.

- *Fewer, many, number* go with plural nouns you can count. *Less, much, amount* go with singular ones.
- *-er* compares two things, *-est* three or more.

```example
The winters in Oslo are colder than ______ in Madrid.
Choices: those / Madrid / it
1. Compare: *winters* with ... :: Two things of the same kind.
2. Winters (plural) vs. winters :: *Madrid* alone is a city.
3. Answer: those :: A plural stand-in for *winters*.
```

```theory
**Why parallel.** The SAT wants a sentence in balance. After *and*, *or* or *but*, the reader expects the same kind of word or phrase as before. A list is built the same way: *She collected rocks, sorted them, and labeled them* uses three past-tense verbs. *She collected rocks, sorting them, and labels them* breaks the pattern. When two verbs share a helping word (*would increase ... and create*), the second one takes the base form.

**Pairs.** *Both ... and, either ... or, neither ... nor, not only ... but also, not ... but* join the same kind of thing. *Not only cheap but also **durable*** is parallel; *not only cheap but also it lasts* is not. Also: *rather than* and *than* join equal forms (*to walk rather than to drive* or *walking rather than driving*).

**Logical comparisons.** Every *than*, *compared to* and *unlike* sets two items side by side, and they must be the same kind of item. *The population of Oslo is larger than **that of** Bergen* works. *The population of Oslo is larger than Bergen* compares a number to a city. Possessives also work: *larger than **Bergen's***. Look at what sits right after *Compared to that of X,* in a choice: it must be the matching thing, such as *silica glass's atomic arrangement*.

**Adjective or adverb.** Adjectives describe nouns (*a quick study*); adverbs describe verbs, adjectives and other adverbs (*she studied quickly*). *Good* is an adjective, *well* the adverb.

**Less and fewer.** *Fewer* + a countable plural (*fewer maps*). *Less* + an uncountable singular (*less water*). The same for *number/amount* and *many/much*.

**Method.** Find the earlier part of the pair, list or comparison. Your blank must copy its form.
```
