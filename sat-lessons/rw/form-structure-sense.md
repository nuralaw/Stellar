## What this question looks like

The screen shows a short text containing a single blank, plus this question, word for word, every time:

*Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?*

Punctuation questions (the Boundaries skill) use the same wording, so the choices are your only clue to which skill you're facing. In Boundaries, the words stay the same and the marks move. Here, the **words change shape**. Typical sets:

- one verb in four forms: *measures / measure / measuring / to measure*, or *measured / measures / will measure / had measured*
- four pronouns: *it / they / this / these*, or *its / their / it's / they're*
- one noun with apostrophes in different places: *painters / painter's / painters'*
- four long choices made of the same words in different orders, sitting right after an opening phrase and a comma

**How many.** College Board puts Standard English Conventions at about 26% of the scored Reading and Writing questions, or 11 to 15 over both modules. Roughly half of those test this skill, so plan on about 5 to 8 per test. In the official practice tests, two types lead by a wide margin, each making up about a quarter of these questions: verb forms (is there a real main verb?) and subject-verb agreement. Verb tense comes next, then modifier placement, then pronouns and possessives. Newer official questions sometimes mix two verb rules in one set of choices, such as number and form, so check every difference.

**Where.** The conventions block sits in the middle of each module, after the reading questions and before the Transitions and notes questions. Grammar and punctuation questions are shuffled together inside it, from easiest to hardest.

**The six things College Board tests here.**

| Sub-type | In plain words |
|---|---|
| Subject-verb agreement | One subject takes a singular verb; more than one takes a plural verb. |
| Pronoun-antecedent agreement | A pronoun matches the noun it replaces. |
| Verb finiteness | Each clause has a working main verb, and describing phrases use *-ing*, *-ed* or *to* forms. |
| Verb tense and aspect | The verb shows the right time, and whether the action is finished, ongoing or habitual. |
| Subject-modifier placement | A describing phrase sits next to the noun it describes. |
| Plurals and possessives | Apostrophes, plus *its / it's* and *their / they're / there*. |

Some classic grammar topics are missing from that list and never came up in the eight official practice tests we went through: *who / whom*, *I / me*, spelling pairs like *affect / effect*, and idioms. Skip them when you study for this test.

> **Key idea:** The blank is never decided by the blank alone. Somewhere else in the text sits the word that decides it: the subject, the noun behind a pronoun, a time clue, or the noun an opening phrase describes. Track that word down before you judge any choice.

## The method

1. **Spot the difference.** Before reading the text closely, look at what separates the four choices: number, verb form, tense, pronoun, apostrophe or word order. The table below names the rule for each. When two things differ, plan to check two rules.
2. **Hunt for the decider.** Each rule has its own: the subject decides singular or plural verbs; the rest of the clause decides main verb or describing form; time words and nearby verbs decide tense; the replaced noun decides a pronoun; the word after a noun decides its apostrophe; the opening phrase decides word order.
3. **Bracket the extras.** Put mental brackets around phrases that begin with *of*, *in*, *on*, *with*, *along with* or *such as*, around *who*, *which* and *that* clauses, and around anything between commas or dashes. Read the sentence without the bracketed parts, and the decider is easy to see.
4. **Name the answer type before you choose.** Say it in a few words, such as "plural verb," "needs a main verb," "one owner," or "the blocks must come first."
5. **Knock out, then compare what's left.** Remove every choice that clashes with your answer type. If two survive, they differ in some second way, usually tense or a pronoun. Decide that too.
6. **Reread with the sentence before.** Put your choice in and read from the start of the previous sentence. Watch for anything new that's broken: a second main verb, a missing main verb, a time jump, or a phrase describing the wrong noun.

**Matching the choices to the rule.**

| If the choices differ like... | you're being tested on... | and the decider is... |
|---|---|---|
| *measures / measure*, *was / were*, *has / have* | subject-verb agreement | the true subject |
| *measures / measuring / to measure / having measured* | verb finiteness | whether the clause already has its main verb |
| *measured / measures / will measure / had measured* | tense and aspect | time words and the other verbs |
| *it / they / this / these*, *its / their* | pronoun agreement | the noun the pronoun replaces |
| apostrophes appearing, disappearing or moving | plurals and possessives | whether something is owned, and by how many |
| *its / it's*, *their / they're / there*, *whose / who's* | possessive or contraction | whether *it is*, *they are* or *who is* fits |
| long choices with the same words reordered | modifier placement | what the opening phrase describes |

> **Tip:** Agreement choices often come as three of one number and one of the other, and the lone choice is frequently right. That's a hint, not a method. Confirm it with the subject every time.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Subject-verb agreement

Match the verb to its subject: one person or thing takes a singular verb, two or more take a plural verb. Present-tense verbs reverse the pattern you know from nouns. With nouns, the *-s* means more than one. With verbs, the *-s* means one.

| One | More than one |
|---|---|
| The kettle **whistles**. | The kettles **whistle**. |
| The road **is** icy. | The roads **are** icy. |
| The choir **has** sung. | The choirs **have** sung. |
| The tide **was** turning. | The tides **were** turning. |

Most past-tense verbs don't change with number (*whistled*), except *was / were*. So agreement questions lean on present-tense verbs and helping verbs, and every choice carries a number. In *explains / has explained / explain / are explaining*, the first two are singular and the last two are plural.

**Finding the true subject.**

1. Take the verb in the blank and ask what is doing it.
2. Bracket phrases that start with a preposition: *of, in, on, with, from, between, among, for*.
3. Bracket *along with*, *as well as*, *together with* and *in addition to*. Only *and* makes a subject plural; these phrases don't.
4. Bracket *who*, *which* and *that* clauses, and anything between commas or dashes.
5. The noun left in front of the verb is the subject.

| Subject type | Verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A noun followed by an *of* phrase | matches the noun before *of* | The basket of ripe mangoes **was** on the table. |
| *one of the...*, *each*, *every*, *either*, *neither* | singular | One of the paintings **is** a fake. |
| Two subjects linked by *and* | plural | The violin and its case **were** sold together. |
| *or*, *nor*, *either...or*, *neither...nor* | matches the closer subject | Neither the mayor nor her advisers **were** at the meeting. |
| Group nouns: *team, committee, family, jury, audience, company, government, series, collection* | singular | The jury **has** reached a verdict. |
| An activity ending in *-ing* | singular | Learning a new language **takes** patience. |
| A *that* or *whether* clause | singular | Whether the plan works **depends** on funding. |
| An amount seen as one total | singular | Ten kilometers **is** a long walk. |
| *the number of* / *a number of* | singular / plural | The number of bike lanes **has** doubled. A number of riders **have** asked for more. |
| *many*, *several*, *few*, *both* | plural | Many **arrive** early. |
| *some*, *all*, *most*, *half* followed by *of* | matches the noun after *of* | Most of the soup **is** cold. Most of the plates **are** clean. |
| A title or a name | singular | *The Birds* **is** a short story. |

> **Remember:** British English often treats group nouns as plural ("the team are"). The SAT follows American English, where group nouns take singular verbs and the pronoun *it*.

**When the verb comes first.** In some sentences the subject follows its verb. Turn the sentence around to check.

- *There* **are** *two bakeries on this street.* (Two bakeries are on this street.)
- *Among the finds* **was** *a gold coin.* (A gold coin was among the finds.)
- *Beneath the ice* **lie** *two hidden lakes.* (Two hidden lakes lie beneath the ice.)

> **Trap:** In speech, people say "there's two bakeries." On the test, go by the subject that comes after the verb, not by what sounds normal in casual speech.

**Distance.** Harder questions pile up nouns and verbs of the other number between the subject and the blank. In "The *guides* who lead the tour of the old fortress **are** trained historians," neither *tour* nor *fortress*, nor even the verb *lead*, changes anything: *guides* is plural.

### Pronoun-antecedent agreement

**Pronouns** are short words that replace nouns: *it, its, they, them, their, this, these, that, those, he, she, one*. The noun a pronoun replaces is its **antecedent**.

- One thing: *it*, *its*, *this*, *that*. More than one: *they*, *them*, *their*, *these*, *those*.
- Companies, organizations, schools, cities, countries, teams and committees count as one: *The museum reopened its east wing.*
- Two nouns linked by *and* count as more than one: *Ana and her brother shared their notes.*
- *This* and *these* also work right in front of a noun. Match that noun: *this letter*, *these letters*.
- *This* can refer back to an entire event or idea, which counts as one thing: *The river changed course in 1850. This forced the town to move.*

**Finding the antecedent.**

1. Ask what the pronoun refers to, and read the previous sentence too, since the antecedent may sit there.
2. Don't trust closeness. The noun nearest the pronoun is a favorite trap.
3. **Swap it in.** Replace the pronoun with your candidate noun. If the sentence still says what the writer means, you've got it: "Although *its* shell is hard, a turtle can still feel touch through it" becomes "Although *the turtle's* shell is hard..."
4. A pronoun can come before its noun: "Although *they* look fragile, butterfly wings are tough."

**Pronouns must also be clear and steady.**

- A pronoun has to point to one noun. If *it* or *this* could mean two different things, or a word like *some* doesn't fit a group of only two, the choice is wrong.
- Keep the same point of view. A text about *customers* shouldn't suddenly speak to *you* or about *one*.
- You won't be asked to choose between *he or she* and singular *they* for one person.

### Verb tense and aspect

**Tense** places an action in time: past, present or future. **Aspect** describes its shape: a single event (simple), an action in progress (progressive), or an action completed before some other point (perfect).

| Form | Example | Used for |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | *paints* | facts, habits, how something works, what a novel, painting or study shows |
| Simple past | *painted* | a completed action at a past time: *in 1920*, *last year* |
| Simple future | *will paint* | an action still to come |
| Present perfect | *has painted*, *have painted* | from the past up to now, or a past time left unnamed: *since 2010*, *for decades*, *recently* |
| Past perfect | *had painted* | completed before another past event or date: *by 1900*, *before the war began* |
| Future perfect | *will have painted* | completed before a future point: *by 2040* |
| Present progressive | *is painting* | in progress right now |
| Past progressive | *was painting* | in progress at a past moment |
| *would* + verb | *would paint* | the future as seen from the past: *She promised she would paint the mural.* |

**Choosing the tense.**

1. **Collect the time words:** dates, *today*, *currently*, *now*, *in 1890*, *by 1900*, *since 2015*, *for ten years*, *before*, *after*, *later*.
2. **Line up with the other verbs** in the sentence and the sentences around it, unless a clue shows the time really shifts.
3. **Facts and habits take the simple present:** *Salmon return to the stream where they hatched.*
4. **Books, paintings and research findings take the present:** *In the novel, the narrator returns to her village.* *The study shows that sleep improves memory.*
5. **"By" plus a past date** points to the past perfect: *By 1900, the city had doubled in size.*
6. **A named past time blocks the present perfect:** *In 1969, astronauts landed on the Moon*, not *have landed*.
7. **Keep the progressive for actions in progress.** To describe how a wind turbine works in general, write *the blades spin*, not *the blades are spinning*.

> **Tip:** If nothing in the text calls for a complicated form, the simple past or the simple present is usually the answer. Forms like *would have been* or *will have had* need a clear reason in the text.

### Verb forms: is there a working main verb?

This is one of the two most common sub-types, and it catches fluent readers because the wrong forms often sound smooth.

A **finite verb** is a verb that can run a clause. It shows time and teams up with a subject: *paints, painted, will paint, has painted, is painting, can paint*.

A **nonfinite form** (a verbal) shows no time of its own and can't run a clause by itself:

| Form | Example | Its usual job |
|---|---|---|
| *to* + verb (infinitive) | *to paint* | a goal (in order to), or after words like *decided*, *able*, *used* |
| *-ing* form | *painting* | describes a noun, or adds a result or a simultaneous action |
| *-ed* / *-en* form with no helper | *painted*, *built*, *written* | describes something that was acted on |
| *having* + *-ed* form | *having painted* | an action completed before the main one |

**Rule 1: every clause needs a finite main verb.** That goes for the main clause and also for each *which / who / that* clause and each *because / when / although* clause.

- Wrong: *The telescope, launched in 2021, detecting infrared light.* (nothing runs the clause)
- Correct: *The telescope, launched in 2021, detects infrared light.*

> **Trap:** In that example, *launched* looks like the main verb, but it belongs to a describing phrase inside commas. The clause still needs its own main verb later.

**Rule 2: one subject, one main verb.** If the clause already has its main verb, the blank needs a nonfinite form. A single subject can run two finite verbs only when a joining word such as *and*, *or* or *but* links them.

- Wrong: *The team, hopes to confirm the result, repeated the test.*
- Correct: *The team, hoping to confirm the result, repeated the test.*
- Correct: *The team hoped to confirm the result and repeated the test.*

Putting a finite verb at the start of a describing phrase creates a second sentence, and with only a comma in front of it, that's a comma splice: *Visitors admired the old violin, is made almost entirely of maple* is wrong. *Made* fixes it.

The same goes for a describing phrase that has its own noun (an absolute phrase): *The ship left at dawn, its sails filled with wind.* Here *filled* must stay a verbal. *Its sails were filled with wind* would be a second sentence after a comma.

**Rule 3: choose the right verbal.**

- *-ing*: the noun is doing the action. *the scientists studying the fossils* (the scientists study).
- *-ed*: the action was done to the noun. *the fossils studied by the scientists* (the fossils were studied).
- *to* + verb: a goal. *She moved to Lima to study music.* When the sentence describes a result rather than an aim, *to* + verb is wrong: "The pools dry up, to force the animals to move" makes the pools sound like they have a plan.
- After *can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must* and *do, does, did*, use the bare verb: *would cut*, *can reduce*, *did not survive*.
- Two verbs linked by *and* that share one subject or one helping verb take matching forms: *The new bridge would cut travel time and reduce traffic.*

| When the sentence... | the blank needs... | Example |
|---|---|---|
| has a subject but nothing running the clause yet | a finite verb | In 2019, the team **announced** its results. |
| is already run by a main verb, and the blank opens a describing phrase | an *-ing* or *-ed* form | The team, **hoping** for funding, repeated the test. |
| states a goal (in order to) | *to* + verb | The team repeated the test **to confirm** the result. |
| has *can*, *will*, *would* or *should* just before the blank | the bare verb | The new bridge would **cut** travel time. |
| has *which*, *who* or *that* just before the blank | a finite verb | The dam, which **supplies** power to the city, was rebuilt. |

### Plurals and possessives

- **Plural** (more than one), no apostrophe: *painters*, *cities*, *theories*.
- **Singular possessive** (one owner), add *'s*: *the painter's brush*, *the city's mayor*, *the bus's door*.
- **Plural possessive** (several owners), put the apostrophe after the *s*: *the painters' brushes*, meaning brushes that belong to several painters.
- Plurals without a final *s* add *'s*: *children's*, *people's*, *women's*, *men's*.
- *People* is already plural. *Peoples* means separate nations or ethnic groups, and it's rarely the answer.

**Does it own something?** Check the word that follows the noun. If that word names something that belongs to the noun, you can rewrite it with *of* (*the novel's hero* is "the hero of the novel"), and the noun needs an apostrophe. If the next word is a verb, a preposition or anything else that isn't owned, write a plain plural or singular.

**How many owners?** Count with the clues in the text: *two*, *each*, *several*, *a series of*, or a plural verb later in the sentence.

> **Tip:** These questions often hide two nouns in one blank, such as *letters written by the poet's sister*. Judge each noun separately: one or many, owner or not. Only one choice gets both nouns right.

**Possessive pronouns versus contractions.** Possessive pronouns never use an apostrophe.

| Word | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *its* | belonging to it | The turtle pulled in its head. |
| *it's* | it is, or it has | It's raining. It's been a long day. |
| *their* | belonging to them | The birds left their nests. |
| *they're* | they are | They're nesting early this year. |
| *there* | a place, or *there is / there are* | There are three nests over there. |
| *whose* | belonging to whom or which | the artist whose work won |
| *who's* | who is, or who has | the artist who's famous |
| *your* / *you're* | belonging to you / you are | Your map is here. You're late. |

**Expand it to check.** Read the word as *it is*, *they are* or *who is*. If the sentence still works, the contraction is right. If not, use the possessive, then check number: *its* for one owner, *their* for several.

### Modifier placement: dangling and misplaced modifiers

A **modifier** is a word or phrase that describes something. The test cares most about the **opening modifier**: a describing phrase at the start of a sentence, followed by a comma.

- *Built in 1890,* ...
- *Known for her detailed maps,* ...
- *After studying the data,* ...
- *A former teacher,* ...
- *To survive the winter,* ...
- *Unlike most frogs,* ...

**The rule:** whatever comes right after the comma has to be the thing the opening phrase describes. If some other noun lands there, the phrase is left "dangling," and the sentence claims something absurd. *Built in 1890, engineers still inspect the bridge every year* says the engineers were built in 1890.

**Steps.**

1. Ask the opening phrase its question: who or what was built, is known, studied, survived?
2. Look for the choice that puts **that exact noun** first after the comma.
3. Possessives fool people. In *the author's novel*, the main noun is *novel*. So "Raised in a small fishing village, the poet's early work describes the sea" says the work was raised in a village.
4. So do *of* phrases. In *the population of the city* or *the shape of the shell*, the main noun is the first one: *population*, *shape*.
5. Choices that begin with *there is*, *it is*, or a different topic put no fitting noun after the comma at all.
6. Once the right noun is in place, check the rest of that choice for agreement and sense.

**Comparisons at the start.** *Like* and *unlike* openers are modifiers too, and they must compare things of the same kind. "Unlike most frogs, the skin of the glass frog is see-through" compares frogs with skin. "Unlike most frogs, the glass frog has see-through skin on its belly" compares frogs with a frog.

**Misplaced modifiers.** A describing phrase in the middle or at the end of a sentence should also sit beside what it describes. "She bought a bike from her neighbor with a broken chain" makes it sound as if the neighbor has a broken chain. "From her neighbor, she bought a bike with a broken chain" puts the chain on the bike.

> **Trap:** A dangling sentence can seem fine because you already know the topic from the sentence before. The test ignores what you could guess and checks the noun that actually sits right after the comma.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Two nouns, one blank

Marie Curie recorded her experiments with radioactive elements in a set of notebooks. More than a century later, the ______ are still radioactive, so researchers who want to read them must wear protective clothing.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) notebook's pages
- (B) notebooks pages
- (C) notebooks' pages
- (D) notebooks' page's

**Solution.** The choices differ only in apostrophes, so judge the two nouns one at a time.

*Notebooks*: the first sentence mentions a set of notebooks, so there are several. The pages belong to them ("the pages of the notebooks"), which makes this a plural possessive: *notebooks'*.

*Pages*: there are many pages, and the word after it is the verb *are*, not something the pages own. So it takes no apostrophe: *pages*.

(A) treats *notebook's* as one notebook, but there are several. (B) is missing the apostrophe that links the pages to the notebooks. (D) gives *pages* an apostrophe even though the pages own nothing here.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: A past moment and an earlier action

When Dutch explorers reached Rapa Nui (Easter Island) in 1722, the island's people ______ hundreds of giant stone statues, known as moai, over the previous centuries.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) carve
- (B) will carve
- (C) have carved
- (D) had carved

**Solution.** Four tenses of one verb, so collect the time words. The explorers arrived in 1722, a past moment. The carving took place "over the previous centuries," earlier than that moment. An action completed before another past event takes the past perfect: *had carved*.

(A) is present tense, which clashes with a scene set in 1722. (B) moves the carving into the future. (C), the present perfect, reaches up to the present, so it can't sit next to a named past moment like 1722.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: A subject far from its verb

The bright bands of red, yellow, and black on the eastern coral snake, which lives in the southeastern United States, ______ predators that the snake is venomous.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) warns
- (B) warn
- (C) warning
- (D) has warned

**Solution.** Two things vary: number (*warns* or *warn*) and form (*warning*). Bracket the extras first: "of red, yellow, and black," "on the eastern coral snake," and "which lives in the southeastern United States." What remains is "The bright bands ______ predators." The subject, *bands*, is plural, so the verb must be plural.

The clause also has nothing running it yet (*lives* belongs to the *which* clause), so the blank must supply a finite verb.

(A) and (D) are singular; they match the nearby singular words *snake* and *lives* instead of the subject. (C) *warning* can't run a clause, so the sentence would be left with no main verb. (B) is plural and finite.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: Whose rooms?

Built inside a mountain on a remote Norwegian island, the Svalbard Global Seed Vault serves as a backup bank for the world's crop diversity. Because ______ storage rooms are cut deep into permanently frozen rock, the vault would stay below freezing even if the power failed.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) its
- (B) their
- (C) it's
- (D) they're

**Solution.** The choices mix one and many, possessive and contraction, so settle both questions.

Whose storage rooms? Swap in the candidates. The text mentions a mountain, an island, the vault and the world's crop diversity, and only the vault can own storage rooms. The main clause confirms it: "the vault would stay below freezing." The vault is one thing, so the pronoun is singular. The rooms belong to it, so the word must be possessive, not a contraction.

(B) is plural. It can look right because *rooms* is plural, but a possessive pronoun matches its owner, not the thing owned. (C) means "it is," which gives "Because it is storage rooms are cut...," a sentence that falls apart. (D) means "they are" and fails the same way. (A) is singular and possessive.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 5: The clause already has its main verb

During the dry season in Namibia's Etosha National Park, most of the shallow pools dry up completely, ______ elephants, zebras, and lions to share the few waterholes that remain.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) forcing
- (B) to force
- (C) forces
- (D) forced

**Solution.** One verb in four forms, so ask whether the clause is already running. It is: "most of the shallow pools **dry up** completely." The blank opens a phrase that adds a result of the drying, so it needs a verbal, and the *-ing* form is the one that adds a result: the pools dry up, and that forces the animals together.

(C) is a finite verb with no subject of its own after the comma, so it adds a second main verb. (D) is either a past-tense main verb (the same problem, plus a jump into the past) or an *-ed* describing form meaning "the elephants were forced," which doesn't fit the sentence. (B) means a goal: it says the pools dry up *in order to* push the animals together, as if the pools had a plan.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 6: What does the opening phrase describe?

Cut to fit together so closely that almost no gap shows between them, ______

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) the Inca laid the granite blocks of Machu Picchu's Temple of the Sun without any mortar.
- (B) Machu Picchu's builders laid the granite blocks of the Temple of the Sun without any mortar.
- (C) the Temple of the Sun at Machu Picchu is made of granite blocks that the Inca laid without any mortar.
- (D) the granite blocks of the Temple of the Sun at Machu Picchu were laid by the Inca without any mortar.

**Solution.** Long choices built from the same words in different orders, right after an opening phrase, mean modifier placement. Ask the phrase its question: what was cut to fit together so closely that almost no gap shows between them? The granite blocks. So *the granite blocks* need to sit directly after the comma.

(A) places *the Inca* there, which says the people were cut to fit together. (B) opens with a possessive, and the main noun of "Machu Picchu's builders" is *builders*, so the builders were cut to fit. (C) places *the Temple of the Sun* there, but the temple is one building, not pieces cut to fit together; the blocks inside it are. (D) puts *the granite blocks* right after the comma.

**Answer:** (D)

## Traps to avoid

- **Agreeing with the neighbor.** The verb matches a noun that happens to sit right before the blank (*of the islands*, *which lives*) instead of the true subject. Bracket the extras first.
- **Treating "along with" like "and".** *Along with*, *as well as* and *together with* leave the subject's number unchanged. Only *and* makes it plural.
- **Group nouns as plural.** *Team*, *committee*, *company* and *city* take singular verbs and *it / its* on this test, even though many people belong to them.
- **The smooth -ing word.** An *-ing* word sounds like action, so a clause with no main verb can still sound complete. Look for a verb that actually runs the clause.
- **The extra main verb.** If a main verb is already in place, putting a finite verb in a describing phrase (*is made*, *they use*) adds a second one and creates a comma splice.
- **A goal that isn't there.** *To* + verb means "in order to." Choose it only when the text describes an aim, never a plain result.
- **The unexplained time jump.** A choice moves to a different time for no reason. Line up with the time words and the verbs nearby.
- **Present perfect next to a date.** *Has* or *have* + verb can't sit beside a finished past time such as *in 1722* or *last year*.
- **The apostrophe on a plain plural.** *Three scientist's shared the prize* is wrong, because nothing belongs to the scientists. No owner, no apostrophe.
- **"It's" where "its" belongs.** If "it is" doesn't fit, the answer is *its*. The same goes for *they're* and *their*.
- **The nearest-noun pronoun.** A pronoun matches the closest noun instead of the one it really replaces. Swap your candidate in to confirm.
- **The possessive dangler.** After an opening phrase, *the author's novel* puts the novel, not the author, in the spotlight.
- **Fixing one rule and missing another.** A choice can have the right number but the wrong form, or the right form but the wrong tense. Check every difference between the choices.

## Quick checklist

- What separates the four choices, and which rule does that point to?
- With the extras bracketed off, what is the true subject?
- Is every clause run by exactly one finite verb, unless *and* links two?
- Do the time words and the nearby verbs support my tense?
- Does my pronoun match the noun it replaces, and is that noun clear?
- Possessive or plural: is something owned, and by how many owners?
- After an opening phrase, is the very next noun the thing the phrase describes?
