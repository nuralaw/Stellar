> **Key idea:** An author can only "answer" with what's in their own text. Apply that author's ideas to the other text's claim, never your own opinion or outside facts.

## What this question looks like

This is the only Reading and Writing question with **two texts**, labeled Text 1 and Text 2. Both are short (about 50–90 words) and cover the same topic, often a scientist's idea and then another researcher's finding about it. Expect about one per module.

The stems look like this:

- "Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1?"
- "Based on the texts, how would [researcher from Text 1] most likely respond to the findings in Text 2?"
- "Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?"

That gives two kinds of question:

- **Respond:** one author reacts to the other's claim or finding.
- **Agree:** you find the idea both authors share.

## The method

Use the same six steps on every cross-text question.

1. **Read the question first.** Is it respond or agree? Who is responding, and to what: the whole text, an underlined claim, or one finding?
2. **Sum up Text 1's claim** in a few words, like "trout are small because of genes." If something is underlined, that's the claim that matters.
3. **Sum up Text 2's claim** the same way, and note its attitude toward Text 1's idea. Text 2 may never mention Text 1; new evidence that points elsewhere still counts as a response.
4. **Name the relationship** using the table below: disagree, another explanation, qualify, complicate, or support.
5. **Predict the answer** in one sentence: "Author 2 would say ___, because ___." For agree questions, predict the broadest idea both texts back.
6. **Check every choice against both texts.** Cross out any choice that fails either one (the usual ways are listed in Traps to avoid).

> **Tip:** The key lines are usually the last sentence of Text 1, where its claim lands, and the first sentence or two of Text 2, where the response starts. Read both texts fully, then reread those lines before you predict.

## Rules and formulas you need

Almost every pair of texts fits one of five relationships, and each has a typical right-answer wording.

### The five relationships

| Relationship | What the response does | Right answer often says |
|---|---|---|
| Disagree | calls the claim wrong | "the claim is mistaken" |
| Another explanation | same facts, different cause | "better explained by…" |
| Qualify ("yes, but") | accepts part, adds a limit | "holds only when…" |
| Complicate | adds evidence that raises doubt | "may be premature" |
| Support / extend | agrees and adds evidence | "evidence for…" |

**Qualify** is the one students miss most. When an author partly agrees, any choice that says "completely wrong" or "fully correct" is out.

### Agree questions: find the shared floor

Ignore what the authors argue *about* and look for what they both take for granted. The right answer is usually modest and general, like "the problem is real" or "context matters." Every word of it must be backed by both texts.

### The question can point either way

Some stems flip direction and ask how the Text 1 author would respond to Text 2. The method is the same; just check which name is in the stem and speak for that author.

### Match each author's caution

SAT authors usually make careful claims with words like *mainly*, *may* or *in most cases*. A correct answer keeps that same level of caution.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Another explanation

**Text 1**

Visitors to Lake Orrin often notice that its trout are much smaller than the trout in nearby lakes. Biologist Hana Reyes argues that the difference is genetic. In her view, the lake's trout descend from a small group of naturally small fish stocked there in the 1950s, and those fish passed their size on to later generations.

**Text 2**

Biologist Samuel Okoye moved young trout from Lake Orrin into a nearby lake with more food, and the fish grew to normal size within two years. Young trout moved the other way, into Lake Orrin, stayed small. Okoye concludes that the trout's size depends mainly on how much food the lake provides.

Based on the texts, how would Okoye most likely respond to Reyes's explanation?

- (A) He would argue that it is supported by his finding that trout moved out of Lake Orrin stayed small.
- (B) He would point out that his results suggest the trout are small mainly because of the lake's conditions, not their ancestry.
- (C) He would agree that the trout are small but suggest that they were stocked in a different decade.
- (D) He would claim that genes have no influence on the size of any fish.

**Solution**

Text 1 says small because of **genes**. Text 2 says small because of **food**: the same fish grow big when moved, and outside fish shrink when moved in. Relationship: another explanation. Prediction: "Okoye would say it's the lake, not the bloodline."

- (A) gets his result backward. The trout moved *out* grew to normal size.
- (B) matches the prediction and keeps his word *mainly*.
- (C) The stocking date never comes up in Text 2. That's outside information.
- (D) is too extreme. He talks about these trout and says *mainly*, not "no influence on any fish."

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: "Yes, but" (qualify)

**Text 1**

A growing number of schools now ban phones during the school day. Supporters say the policy clearly works: **students at these schools pay closer attention in class.**

**Text 2**

Education researcher Priya Nand studied 40 schools that banned phones. At most of them, classroom focus did improve, but only where teachers enforced the rule consistently. At schools with loose enforcement, students simply hid their phones, and focus stayed about the same.

Based on the texts, how would Nand most likely respond to the underlined claim in Text 1?

- (A) She would reject it, arguing that phone bans have no effect on students' focus.
- (B) She would fully accept it, since she found that bans improved focus at every school she studied.
- (C) She would say that it can be true but that the benefit depends on whether the ban is actually enforced.
- (D) She would argue that the claim is true only for younger students.

**Solution**

Text 1 says bans make students focus better. Text 2 says yes, at most schools, **but** only with strict enforcement. That's a classic qualify. Prediction: "Partly right; it depends on enforcement."

- (A) is too far in one direction. She found focus *did* improve at most schools.
- (B) is too far in the other direction, and false: at loosely run schools, focus didn't change.
- (C) captures both the "yes" and the "but."
- (D) Student age is never discussed.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 3: Both authors agree

**Text 1**

Some architects argue that new buildings in historic neighborhoods should copy the style of the older buildings around them. Designer Clara Moss disagrees: she thinks new buildings should look clearly modern. Even so, she insists they should match their neighbors' height and spacing so that the street still feels unified.

**Text 2**

Architect Daniel Ibe favors new buildings that closely imitate the historic styles nearby. A building that looks out of place, he argues, can break the character of an entire street, so designers have a duty to think about how their work fits with what surrounds it.

Based on the texts, Moss and Ibe would most likely agree with which statement?

- (A) A new building's design should take its surroundings into account.
- (B) New buildings in historic neighborhoods should copy older architectural styles.
- (C) Modern-looking buildings strengthen the character of historic streets.
- (D) Historic neighborhoods should not allow new construction.

**Solution**

They disagree on **style**: modern (Moss) vs. imitation (Ibe). Look underneath that. Moss wants new buildings to match their neighbors' height and spacing so the street feels unified. Ibe wants designers to consider how a building fits its surroundings. Shared floor: "think about the neighbors."

- (A) is modest, and both texts back it.
- (B) is Ibe's view only. Moss rejects it.
- (C) Moss might lean this way, but Ibe clearly wouldn't, and even Moss never says modern design *strengthens* the street.
- (D) Neither author opposes new buildings. They're arguing about how to design them.

**Answer:** (A)

## Traps to avoid

Most wrong choices fail one of these checks.

- **One-text answer.** It describes one text well but gets the other wrong or ignores it. Every part must fit both.
- **Swapped authors.** It gives Text 1's idea to Text 2, or flips a finding (Example 1, choice A). Keep each claim tied to its author.
- **All-or-nothing.** It says "completely rejects" or "fully accepts" when the real response is "yes, but."
- **Too extreme.** *Always*, *never*, *only*, *no effect*, *any species* go beyond what careful authors say, unless an author really said it.
- **Outside information or your own opinion.** It brings in a fact, cause or group neither text mentions, or what *you* think about the topic. Sounding reasonable isn't enough.
- **Same topic doesn't mean same view.** Two texts on one subject can still disagree. And a contrast word in Text 2 doesn't always mean it rejects Text 1; it may just add a limit.
- **True but off-target.** Both texts support it, but it doesn't answer the specific claim the stem points to.

## Quick checklist

- Respond or agree? Who's responding, and to which exact claim?
- Did I sum up each text's claim in a few words?
- Did I name the relationship?
- Did I predict the response before reading the choices?
- Does every part of my answer fit both texts, with the authors in the right places?
- Is my answer as cautious as the authors are?
