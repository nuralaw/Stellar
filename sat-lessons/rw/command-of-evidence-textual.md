## What this question looks like

A short text on the left of the screen gives you a **claim**: a hypothesis, an argument, a conclusion or a statement about a character. On the right, the question asks you to choose the evidence that fits it. The question comes in two main forms.

**1. Finding questions (science, social science, history).** The text describes what a researcher or historian thinks. The four choices are possible research results.

- "Which finding, if true, would most directly support the team's hypothesis?"
- "Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken Osei's argument?"
- "Which finding, if true, would most strongly support Nwosu's hypothesis over Lind's?"

**2. Quotation questions (literature).** The text names a poem, novel, story or play and makes a claim about a character, the narrator or the speaker. It ends with a colon and a blank. The four choices are quotations from the work.

- "Which quotation from the poem most effectively illustrates the claim?"

Poems are quoted with a slash ( / ) where each line ends. Now and then the quotations come from a person instead, such as a participant in a study or a scholar. Treat them the same way: the quotation must show the claim.

**How many and where.** Expect about 3 or 4 of these per test. They sit in the Command of Evidence part of the Information and Ideas group, mixed in with the table and graph questions and ordered from easier to harder. They come after the main idea questions and before the "most logically completes the text" questions.

> **Key idea:** The findings are imaginary ("if true"). You never judge whether a finding really happened, only whether it would matter for this exact claim. Most wrong choices are on the right topic but do nothing for the claim, or push it the wrong way.

## The method

1. **Find the claim and the job.** In a finding question, the claim is usually the sentence with *hypothesizes*, *argues*, *claims*, *concludes* or *suggests*, often near the end. In a quotation question, it is the sentence that ends with the colon and the blank. Highlight it. Then note the job word: **support**, **weaken** or **illustrate**.
2. **Break the claim into parts.** Who or what exactly is it about? What is the relationship: X causes Y, X is bigger than Y, X is used for Y, a character feels X? Are there limits or a second part, like *only*, *mainly*, *at night*, *both … and*, *at first … later*?
3. **Predict what the evidence must show.** Support: "If the claim is true, we would expect to see …" Weaken: "If the claim is true, we would *not* expect to see …", or "something else could explain it." Quotation: "I need a line where this person shows this feeling, plus the second part."
4. **Translate each choice into plain words.** Treat every finding as true. Turn every quotation into a simple modern sentence before you judge it.
5. **Check fit and direction.** Does the choice deal with the same subjects and the same relationship as the claim? Does it cover every part? Does it push toward the claim, away from it, or nowhere? A good test: would this change the mind of someone who doubts the claim?
6. **Re-read the job word, then choose.** The most common mistake is picking a strong supporter when the question asks you to weaken (or the reverse).

> **Tip:** Write the job word on your scratch paper (S, W or I) before you read the choices. It takes one second and stops the opposite-job mistake.

## Rules and formulas you need

### How a finding connects to a claim

| Kind of claim | Example claim | Supports it | Weakens it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cause | "Traffic noise makes city birds sing higher." | Add noise and pitch rises; take it away and pitch drops; more noise goes with higher pitch | Birds sing just as high with no noise; pitch was high before the traffic came |
| Comparison | "Species A dives deeper than species B." | A dives deeper than B on the same measure, in the same conditions | B dives as deep as A, or deeper |
| Purpose | "The bright tail helps the lizard escape birds." | Birds grab the tail and the lizard gets away; lizards with bright tails escape more often | Lizards with dull tails escape just as often |
| Main cause of a change | "The bridge caused the town to grow." | The town grew only after the bridge, faster than similar towns without one | The town was already growing before the bridge, or towns without bridges grew as fast |
| One explanation over another | "A explains the pattern, not B." | A result that A predicts and B does not | A result that B predicts and A does not |
| A rule about a whole group | "All desert frogs can survive drought." | Many different desert frogs survive drought | One desert frog that can't (one is enough against "all") |

### Rules for finding questions

- **"If true" means accept it.** Never reject a choice because it seems unlikely in real life.
- **Same subjects.** Evidence about a different species, group, place or time does not test the claim, unless the text links them.
- **Cause needs a fair comparison.** The strongest support for "X causes Y" changes X while keeping other things the same, and Y changes with it.
- **New, not repeated.** The right finding adds new information that connects to the claim through one step of reasoning. A choice that only repeats the claim or the background is not new evidence.
- **"Most directly" means fewest extra steps.** If a choice needs you to imagine extra facts before it matters, a better choice exists.
- **Personal opinions are weak evidence in science.** "Many visitors said they felt calmer" is weaker than a measured result.

### Rules for quotation questions

- **You don't need to know the book or poem.** Everything you need is in the claim and the four quotations.
- **Split two-part claims.** "Both wonder and loneliness" needs both. "Changes from hope to doubt" needs the change.
- **Show, don't mention.** A quotation that repeats a word from the claim ("city", "fear") is not automatically right. It must show the idea.
- **Right person.** The claim is about one character or the speaker. A great quotation about someone else is wrong.
- **What a character says is not always the whole truth.** A character's words show what that character thinks, or wants others to think. What the character does, or how the narrator describes them, can show something different.
- **Translate picture language.** In harder questions the quotation shows the claim through an image or an action rather than saying it outright.

## Worked examples

Every text, finding and quotation below was written for this lesson. The people, places, species and works are made up.

### Example 1: Support a cause

Male songbirds of one species sing at a higher pitch in cities than in forests. Ecologist Maya Ferreira hypothesizes that loud, low-pitched traffic noise causes the birds to raise the pitch of their songs so that other birds can still hear them.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support Ferreira's hypothesis?

- (A) City birds of this species have slightly longer beaks than forest birds of the species do, and they also tend to weigh a little more.
- (B) When recorded traffic noise was played in a quiet forest, the birds there sang at a higher pitch, then returned to their usual pitch once it stopped.
- (C) Forest birds of this species sing more often in the early morning than in the afternoon, and their songs are longer in spring than in autumn.
- (D) City birds of this species that live in quiet parks sing at the same high pitch as city birds that live next to busy roads.

**Solution.** The claim is a cause: traffic noise → higher pitch. The job is support. Prediction: *add noise and the pitch should rise; take it away and the pitch should fall.*

- (A) Beak length and body weight have nothing to do with noise or pitch. No bearing.
- (B) Noise is added, pitch rises; noise is removed, pitch returns. The same birds are tested, so other differences between cities and forests can't explain the change. This directly supports the claim.
- (C) How often and how long the birds sing says nothing about noise or pitch. No bearing.
- (D) Birds in quiet parks sing just as high, so the pitch does *not* depend on noise. This weakens the claim. Opposite job.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: Weaken an argument about a cause

Between 1850 and 1870, the population of Millbrook, a small river town, tripled. Historian Clara Osei argues that the main cause of this growth was a toll-free bridge built across the river in 1851, which allowed farmers from the far bank to bring their goods to Millbrook's market.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken Osei's argument?

- (A) Several other river towns in the region that built toll-free bridges in the 1850s also grew quickly over the following years.
- (B) The stone used to build the bridge came from a quarry just outside Millbrook.
- (C) Farmers from the far bank sold much more grain at Millbrook's market after 1851 than they had before.
- (D) Millbrook's population had been growing just as quickly for twenty years before the bridge was built.

**Solution.** The claim: the bridge was the *main cause* of the growth after 1851. The job is weaken. Prediction: *show that the town grows the same way without the bridge, or that something else explains the growth.*

- (A) Towns with bridges grew too. That fits the claim, so it supports it. Opposite job.
- (B) Where the stone came from has no effect on why people moved there. No bearing.
- (C) More trade across the bridge is exactly how the bridge would cause growth. It supports the claim. Opposite job.
- (D) If the town was already growing just as fast before the bridge existed, then the bridge is not needed to explain the growth. This weakens the argument.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: A quotation for a two-part claim

"Night Train to the City" is an early 1900s poem by Isabel Varga. In the poem, the speaker expresses both wonder at the city and a feeling of loneliness there: ______

Which quotation from "Night Train to the City" most effectively illustrates the claim?

- (A) "A thousand windows burn like captured stars, / And every street is humming with a song."
- (B) "I walk among the crowds as if through rain, / And not one face turns toward me as I pass."
- (C) "How bright the towers rise, how proud, how tall! / And yet among their lights I know no one."
- (D) "At dawn the market wagons rumble in / With apples, bread and milk from distant farms."

**Solution.** The claim has two parts: **wonder** and **loneliness**. Translate each quotation.

- (A) The windows shine like stars and the streets sing: wonder only. Half the claim.
- (B) Nobody looks at the speaker in the crowd: loneliness only. Half the claim.
- (C) "How bright … how proud, how tall!" is amazement. "Among their lights I know no one" is loneliness. Both parts, joined by "And yet".
- (D) Wagons bring food in the morning. This describes the city but shows neither feeling.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 4: A quotation where the link is indirect

*The Linden House* is a 1908 novel by Clara Penhallow. In the novel, the narrator, Rose, suggests that her Aunt Delphine's harsh words often hide real care for her: ______

Which quotation from *The Linden House* most effectively illustrates the claim?

- (A) "'Sit up straight,' Aunt Delphine told me at every meal, 'or the whole town will think you were raised in a barn.' She said it so often that I stopped hearing it."
- (B) "When I won the spelling prize, Aunt Delphine hugged me in front of the whole school and did not let go for a long time. 'My clever girl,' she kept saying."
- (C) "'That coat is a disgrace,' she said. That night I heard her sewing by the fire, and in the morning the coat lay mended on my chair. She never mentioned it."
- (D) "The neighbors said that Aunt Delphine had once been the best dancer in the county, though none of us children could picture her dancing, or even laughing, at a party."

**Solution.** The claim has two linked parts: **harsh words** and **care hidden behind them**.

- (A) Harsh words, repeated often, but nothing shows care. Half the claim.
- (B) Care, but shown openly with a hug and praise, and there are no harsh words. This is the tempting one: it is about affection, but the claim is about affection that is *hidden*.
- (C) She insults the coat (harsh words), then mends it at night and never mentions it (hidden care). Both parts, and the care is clearly hidden.
- (D) This is about her past as a dancer and how serious she seems now. It shows no harsh words to Rose and no care.

The link in (C) comes through actions, not a direct statement like "she loved me". That is what harder quotation questions look like.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 5: Choosing between two hypotheses

Each morning, male ridge-tailed lizards, a desert species, turn dark blue, and the color fades by midday. Biologist Amara Nwosu hypothesizes that the dark color helps the lizards absorb the sun's heat quickly after cold nights. Her colleague Tomas Lind instead hypothesizes that the color is a signal that warns rival males to stay away.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support Nwosu's hypothesis over Lind's?

- (A) Males that turn the darkest blue win more fights against rival males than other males do.
- (B) Female ridge-tailed lizards, which do not compete with rivals, stay pale even on the coldest mornings.
- (C) The blue color is produced by tiny crystals in the lizards' skin that reflect light.
- (D) Males kept alone, with no other lizards nearby, still turn dark blue each morning, and they turn darker on colder mornings.

**Solution.** There are two explanations, so predict what each one expects. **Nwosu (heat):** the color should follow temperature, and it should appear even when no rivals are around. **Lind (signal):** the color should follow rivals, and it should matter in contests. The job is to support Nwosu *over* Lind, so we need a result that fits heat and not a signal.

- (A) Darker males win more fights. That fits the signal idea. Supports Lind.
- (B) Females face the same cold mornings. If the color were for warming up, they should use it too. They don't, which fits Lind better. Opposite job.
- (C) This explains *how* the color is made, not *why* the lizards use it. No bearing on either hypothesis.
- (D) With no rivals present, a warning signal would be pointless, yet the males still turn blue, and the color tracks the cold. This fits heat and not a signal.

**Answer:** (D)

## Traps to avoid

- **Opposite job:** a choice that strongly supports the claim when the question says weaken, or the reverse. Check the job word last.
- **On topic, no bearing:** the choice mentions the same animals, town or poem but not the relationship in the claim (beak length, where the stone came from, how a color is made).
- **Half the claim:** for a two-part claim, a quotation or finding that covers only one part.
- **Wrong subject:** evidence about a different species, group, time or character than the claim names.
- **Keyword match:** a quotation that repeats a word from the claim but doesn't show the idea. Judge the meaning, not the shared words.
- **Fits both explanations:** in "A over B" questions, a finding that both hypotheses would expect can't help you choose between them.
- **Real-world doubt:** rejecting the right finding because it sounds surprising. "If true" means you accept it.
- **Open versus hidden:** in literature, a quotation that shows the right feeling in the wrong way (shown openly when the claim says hidden, or at the wrong moment when the claim describes a change).

## Quick checklist

- Did I find the exact claim and the job word: support, weaken or illustrate?
- Did I split the claim into its parts, including limits like *only* or *both*?
- Did I predict what the evidence should show before reading the choices?
- Does my choice match the claim's subjects and every part of it?
- Does it push in the right direction?
- For quotations: did I translate each one into plain words first?
