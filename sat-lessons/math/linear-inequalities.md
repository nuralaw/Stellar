## What this question looks like

An *inequality* compares two sides with <, >, ≤ or ≥ instead of =. Its solution is usually a whole range of numbers, not one number. For example, x > 3 means "every number bigger than 3".

On the Digital SAT, inequality questions come in a few forms:

- **Solve it.** One variable, like 5 − 2(x + 4) < 3x + 7. The choices look like x > −2 and x < −2, so the direction of the sign is the whole question.
- **Write it from words.** A story with a limit: a budget, a weight limit, a minimum score. Words like "at least", "at most", "no more than" and "fewer than" tell you which symbol to use.
- **Greatest or least whole number.** "What is the greatest number of tickets she can buy?" You solve, then round in the right direction.
- **Which point works?** You get one inequality or a system of two inequalities (or a shaded graph) and pick the point (x, y) that is a solution.
- **Read or match a graph.** A boundary line, solid or dashed, with one side shaded. Which inequality is it?
- **A system from a story.** Two limits at once, like money and space. Which system fits, or what is the most you can make?
- **What a number means.** In 12x + 8y ≤ 400, what does the 8 stand for?

About 13 to 15 of the 44 math questions are algebra, and prep sites estimate that 2 or 3 of them are inequality questions (their counts vary). Inequalities also show up as one step inside other questions. They come both as multiple choice and as student-produced response, where you type the answer yourself.

## The method

1. **Find the target.** Is the answer a range of x, a single greatest or least whole number, a point, a graph, or a system?
2. **Name the variables, with units.** "t = number of ride tickets", "h = hours worked".
3. **Translate the words into a symbol** (table below), then build the inequality the way you would build an equation: a fixed amount plus rate × quantity on one side, the limit on the other.
4. **Solve it like an equation, with one extra rule.** When you multiply or divide both sides by a negative number, flip the sign at that step. You can avoid the flip by moving the variable to the side where its coefficient is positive.
5. **For a whole-number answer**, round toward the allowed side: down for an upper limit, up for a lower limit. Then test your number and the next one in the story.
6. **For points and graphs**, put each point into every inequality. For a graph, solve for y: y > means above the line, y < means below it, a solid line means ≤ or ≥, a dashed line means < or >.
7. **Check with an easy value.** Zero is usually fastest. Put it into the original inequality and see whether it should be part of your answer.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Solving one-variable inequalities

| This move | Does the sign flip? |
|---|---|
| Add or subtract any number on both sides (even a negative one) | No |
| Multiply or divide both sides by a positive number | No |
| Multiply or divide both sides by a negative number | Yes |
| Distribute a negative inside one side, like −2(x + 4) = −2x − 8 | No |
| Swap the two sides | The symbol turns with them: 3 < x is the same as x > 3 |

- *Compound inequality*, like −3 < 2x + 1 ≤ 9: do each step to all three parts. Subtract 1 to get −4 < 2x ≤ 8, then divide by 2 to get −2 < x ≤ 4.
- If the x-terms cancel, read what is left. A true statement (like 3 < 5) means every number is a solution. A false one (like 5 < 3) means no number is.

### Words into symbols

| Words in the question | Symbol |
|---|---|
| at least, no less than, a minimum of, not under | ≥ |
| at most, no more than, a maximum of, cannot exceed, up to | ≤ |
| more than, greater than, exceeds, over, above | > |
| less than, fewer than, under, below | < |
| between 3 and 7, inclusive | 3 ≤ x ≤ 7 |

> **Trap:** "5 less than x" is the expression x − 5, not an inequality. "5 is less than x" is the inequality 5 < x. Look for a verb such as "is" or "must be".

### Greatest or least whole number

| The situation | Example | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| An upper limit (budget, weight limit, "at most") | t ≤ 11.23 | round down: 11 |
| A lower limit (goal, minimum, "at least", "more than") | w > 36.67 | round up: 37 |
| The boundary is a whole number and the sign is strict | n < 12 | the greatest whole number is 11, not 12 |

### Two-variable inequalities and graphs

- The *boundary line* is the inequality with = in place of the symbol.
- Solid line: ≤ or ≥, so points on the line count. Dashed line: < or >, so points on the line don't count.
- Solved for y: y > mx + b (or ≥) is shaded above the line; y < mx + b (or ≤) is shaded below it.
- Solving for y can mean dividing by a negative. Example: 2x − 3y > 6 becomes −3y > −2x + 6, and dividing by −3 flips the sign: y < (2/3)x − 2. So this one is shaded below its line.
- Vertical boundary: x > 4 is shaded to the right of the line x = 4, and x < 4 to the left.
- Test point: if the line doesn't pass through (0, 0), put (0, 0) into the inequality. If the result is true, the side with the origin is shaded.

### Systems of inequalities

- The solution is the overlap: the part of the plane shaded by every inequality. A point is a solution only if it makes every inequality true.
- A corner where two boundary lines meet is a solution only if both inequalities allow "equal" (both lines solid).
- Near a boundary, don't trust your eyes. Substitute the point.

### What the parts of a model mean

A shop buys x shirts at $12 each and y caps at $8 each, and it can spend at most $400: 12x + 8y ≤ 400. Here 12 is the price of one shirt, 12x is the money spent on shirts, 8 is the price of one cap, 400 is the most the shop can spend, and ≤ says "at most".

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Solve and watch the sign

Which of the following describes all solutions to the inequality 5 − 2(x + 4) < 3x + 7?

- (A) x < −2
- (B) x > −2
- (C) x > 6/5
- (D) x < 6/5

**Solution.** Distribute the −2 to both terms in the parentheses: 5 − 2x − 8 < 3x + 7, which simplifies to −3 − 2x < 3x + 7.

Keep x on the right, where its coefficient stays positive. Add 2x to both sides and subtract 7 from both sides: −10 < 5x. Divide by 5, a positive number, so no flip: −2 < x. That means x > −2.

If you move x to the left instead, you get −5x < 10. Dividing by −5 flips the sign: x > −2. Same answer.

Check with 0: the left side is 5 − 2(4) = −3 and the right side is 7, and −3 < 7 is true, so 0 must be a solution. That rules out (A) and (C). Check with −3: the left side is 5 − 2(1) = 3 and the right side is −2, and 3 < −2 is false, so −3 is not a solution. That rules out (D).

Why the others are wrong: (A) forgets to flip when dividing by −5. (C) multiplies −2 × 4 as +8, which leads to 6 < 5x. (D) makes both mistakes.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 2: The least whole number

A school club needs to raise more than $2,000 for a trip. It already has $350, and it earns $45 for each car it washes. What is the least number of cars the club must wash to reach its goal?

- (A) 36
- (B) 37
- (C) 45
- (D) 53

**Solution.** Let w = the number of cars. "More than" means >, so 350 + 45w > 2,000.

Subtract 350: 45w > 1,650. Divide by 45: w > 36.67 (rounded to two decimals).

The club can't wash part of a car, so w is a whole number bigger than 36.67. Round up: 37.

Test: 36 cars give 350 + 45(36) = 1,970, which is not enough. 37 cars give 350 + 45(37) = 2,015, which is more than 2,000.

Why the others are wrong: (A) rounds down. (C) forgets the $350 the club already has (45w > 2,000 gives 45). (D) adds the $350 to the goal instead of subtracting it (45w > 2,350 gives 53).

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 3: The greatest whole number in a budget

Lena has $85 to spend at a fair. Entry costs $12, and each ride ticket costs $6.50. What is the greatest number of ride tickets she can buy?

*Student-produced response: enter your answer.*

**Solution.** Let t = the number of ride tickets. She can spend at most $85, so 12 + 6.5t ≤ 85.

Subtract 12: 6.5t ≤ 73. Divide by 6.5: t ≤ 11.23 (rounded to two decimals).

She can't buy part of a ticket, and she can't go over her budget, so round down: 11.

Test: 11 tickets cost 12 + 6.5(11) = 83.50 dollars, which fits. 12 tickets cost 12 + 6.5(12) = 90 dollars, which is over $85.

Common mistakes: forgetting the entry fee (85 ÷ 6.5 = 13.08 gives 13), or rounding 11.23 up to 12.

**Answer:** 11

### Example 4: Which point is in the solution set?

y ≥ 3x − 4

2x + y < 6

Which point (x, y) is a solution to the system of inequalities above?

- (A) (2, 2)
- (B) (0, 3)
- (C) (3, 1)
- (D) (−1, 9)

**Solution.** Put each point into both inequalities. Cross a point out as soon as one inequality fails.

- (A) (2, 2): first, 2 ≥ 3(2) − 4 = 2 is true, because "equal" is allowed. Second, 2(2) + 2 = 6, and 6 < 6 is false. Out.
- (B) (0, 3): first, 3 ≥ −4 is true. Second, 0 + 3 = 3 < 6 is true. Both work.
- (C) (3, 1): first, 1 ≥ 5 is false. Out.
- (D) (−1, 9): first, 9 ≥ −7 is true. Second, −2 + 9 = 7 < 6 is false. Out.

Choice (A) is the trap. (2, 2) is the corner where the two boundary lines meet, so on a graph it seems to sit on the edge of the shaded region. But the second boundary is dashed (strict <), so points on it are not solutions.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 5: Match the graph

In the xy-plane, the graph of an inequality has a dashed boundary line that passes through the points (0, 3) and (4, 0). The region below the line is shaded. Which inequality does the graph show?

- (A) 3x + 4y < 12
- (B) 3x + 4y ≤ 12
- (C) 3x + 4y > 12
- (D) 4x + 3y < 12

**Solution.** Find the line first. The slope is (0 − 3)/(4 − 0) = −3/4, and the line crosses the y-axis at 3, so the line is y = −(3/4)x + 3.

Shaded below a dashed line means y < −(3/4)x + 3. Multiply every term by 4: 4y < −3x + 12. Add 3x to both sides: 3x + 4y < 12.

Check with the origin: (0, 0) is below the line, so it must be a solution. In choice (A), 3(0) + 4(0) = 0 and 0 < 12 is true.

Why the others are wrong: (B) uses ≤, which needs a solid line. (C) is shaded above the line, and the origin fails it (0 > 12 is false). (D) swaps the intercepts: its boundary 4x + 3y = 12 passes through (3, 0) and (0, 4).

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 6: Two limits at once

A bakery makes small cakes and large cakes. A small cake uses 2 eggs and 3 cups of flour. A large cake uses 5 eggs and 4 cups of flour. Today the bakery has 60 eggs and 64 cups of flour, and it must make at least 4 small cakes for an order. What is the greatest number of large cakes it can make today?

- (A) 10
- (B) 11
- (C) 12
- (D) 13

**Solution.** Let s = the number of small cakes and L = the number of large cakes. Write one inequality for each limit:

- Eggs: 2s + 5L ≤ 60
- Flour: 3s + 4L ≤ 64
- Order: s ≥ 4

To make as many large cakes as possible, make as few small cakes as allowed: s = 4. Every extra small cake uses eggs and flour that large cakes could use.

- Eggs: 8 + 5L ≤ 60, so 5L ≤ 52 and L ≤ 10.4.
- Flour: 12 + 4L ≤ 64, so 4L ≤ 52 and L ≤ 13.

Both limits must hold, so the smaller one decides: L ≤ 10.4. Round down: 10 large cakes. Check: 4 small and 10 large cakes use 2(4) + 5(10) = 58 eggs and 3(4) + 4(10) = 52 cups of flour, so both fit. With 11 large cakes the bakery would need 8 + 55 = 63 eggs, which is too many.

Why the others are wrong: (B) rounds 10.4 up. (C) ignores the order for 4 small cakes (with s = 0 the eggs allow 12). (D) uses only the flour limit.

**Answer:** (A)

## Traps to avoid

- **Forgetting to flip.** Dividing or multiplying by a negative flips the sign. Moving x to the side where its coefficient is positive avoids the problem.
- **Flipping when you shouldn't.** Subtracting a negative number or distributing a negative never flips the sign. Only multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative does.
- **Mixing up "at least" and "at most".** At least means ≥ (a floor). At most means ≤ (a ceiling). "No more than" is ≤, and "no fewer than" is ≥.
- **"More than" versus "at least".** "More than 2,000" means 2,000 itself does not count, so the symbol is >.
- **Rounding the wrong way.** Budgets and capacities round down; goals and minimums round up. Then test the number you picked in the story.
- **Picking the boundary in a strict inequality.** If n < 12 exactly, 12 is not allowed. The greatest whole number is 11.
- **Trusting the picture at a boundary.** A point on a dashed line is not a solution. Substitute it to be sure.
- **Shading the wrong side after dividing by a negative.** 2x − 3y > 6 becomes y < (2/3)x − 2, which is shaded below its line, even though the original has a ">" in it.
- **Checking only one inequality in a system.** A point must make every inequality true.
- **Mixing up a fee and a rate.** A fee paid once is added on its own. A price per item multiplies the variable: 12 + 6.5t, not 6.5 + 12t.

## Desmos shortcut

Inequalities are easy to type in Desmos, and the shading shows you the "which side" answer.

**Typing.** Type < and > directly. Type `<=` for ≤ and `>=` for ≥; Desmos turns them into the symbols. Desmos then draws the boundary dashed for < and >, solid for ≤ and ≥, and shades the side where the inequality is true.

**One-variable inequality.** Type it as it is, for example `5 - 2(x + 4) < 3x + 7`. Desmos shades everything to the right of a dashed vertical line at x = −2, so the answer is x > −2. This is a quick check on the direction of the sign.

**Greatest or least whole number.** Graph the two sides of the story as two lines and click where they cross. For Example 3, type `y = 12 + 6.5x` and `y = 85`. The crossing point is (11.23077, 85), so the boundary is about 11.23. Then decide the rounding yourself: a budget means round down, so 11.

**Which point works.** Type both inequalities, for example `y >= 3x - 4` and `2x + y < 6`. The overlap of the two shaded regions is darker. Then type each answer choice as a point, like `(0, 3)`. A point inside the darker region is a solution. If a point sits on a boundary line, as (2, 2) does in Example 4, don't decide from the picture: substitute it.

**Other letters.** Desmos graphs only x and y. If a question uses s and L, type x for the first and y for the second, and write down which is which.

> **Tip:** Typing the inequality from a word problem into Desmos only checks your algebra. Desmos can't tell you whether "at least" means ≥, so translate the words on paper first.

## Quick checklist

- Did I translate every limit word (at least, at most, more than, fewer than) into the right symbol?
- When I multiplied or divided by a negative number, did I flip the sign?
- For a whole-number answer, did I round toward the allowed side and test my number and the next one?
- For a point, did I check it in every inequality, with "equal" allowed only for ≤ and ≥?
- For a graph: solid or dashed? Above or below? Did I flip if I divided by a negative while solving for y?
- Did I test 0 (or another easy number) in the original inequality?
