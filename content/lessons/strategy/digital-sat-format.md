# Shape of the test
Two sections, four timed modules.

```visual
digital-sat-format--shape
```

## Key ideas

The digital SAT has **two sections**, and each section is split into **two modules** with separate timers. You take Reading and Writing (R&W) first, then a short break, then Math.

| Section | Questions per module | Minutes per module |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading and Writing | 27 | 32 |
| Math | 22 | 35 |

That is 98 questions in 134 minutes (2 hours 14 minutes), not counting the break.

Every question stands alone. In R&W, one short passage (25 to 150 words) comes with one question, and there are no question sets. All R&W questions and about three quarters of the Math questions are multiple choice with four options. The other quarter of Math is **student-produced response**: no options, you type the answer.

A calculator is allowed on the whole Math section, and Bluebook has the Desmos calculator built in. R&W has no calculator.

**Trap:** each module has its own clock. Time you save in one module is gone when it ends.

```theory
A **section** is one of the two big parts of the test, each scored from 200 to 800. A **module** is one separately timed block of questions, and each section has two. **Bluebook** is the College Board app that delivers the digital SAT. A **discrete** question can be answered without any other question.

In a **multiple-choice** question, exactly one option is correct (the **key**). The three wrong options, called **distractors**, are built from common mistakes, so a wrong option often looks reasonable. A **student-produced response (SPR)** has no options. About 11 of the 44 Math questions are SPR, and each asks you to type one answer.

R&W questions belong to four content domains: Craft and Structure, Information and Ideas, Standard English Conventions, and Expression of Ideas. Math has four too: Algebra and Advanced Math (about 35 percent each), Problem-Solving and Data Analysis, and Geometry and Trigonometry (about 15 percent each). About 30 percent of Math questions are word problems, and most of those are 50 words or fewer. The rest are pure math.

R&W graphics are tables, bar graphs and line graphs, and no R&W question asks you to calculate. Math graphics also include scatterplots, graphs of functions and geometric figures.

Average time per question: 64 minutes for 54 R&W questions is about 1.19 minutes (71 seconds). 70 minutes for 44 Math questions is about 1.59 minutes (95 seconds). These are averages, not deadlines: some questions take 20 seconds and some take three minutes.
```

# Scores and guessing
Sections out of 800, total out of 1600.

```visual
digital-sat-format--score
```

## Key ideas

Each section gets its own score from **200 to 800**, in steps of 10. The **total score** is the sum of the two section scores, so it runs from **400 to 1600**.

Your section score depends on which questions you answered correctly, not only how many. College Board does not publish the exact formula, so do not try to calculate it.

**There is no penalty for a wrong answer.** A wrong answer and a blank both give zero points, so answer every question, even if you have to guess.

**Not every question counts.** In each module, 2 questions are unscored: College Board is trying them out for future tests. They look like every other question, and you cannot tell which ones they are. Treat every question as scored.

```example
Reading and Writing: 640. Math: 710. What is the total score?
1. 640 + 710 :: Add the two section scores.
2. 1350 :: Between 400 and 1600, as it should be.
```

```theory
The **section score** is reported from 200 to 800 and the **total score** from 400 to 1600, both in 10-point steps. The score report shows these totals, a percentile and College Board's readiness benchmarks.

A **percentile** is the percent of students who scored at or below you. A score at the 80th percentile beats about 80 percent of students. The **benchmarks** are 480 for Reading and Writing and 530 for Math: College Board's estimate of the scores that give a good chance of success in entry-level college courses.

If you test more than once, **Score Choice** lets you decide which test dates colleges see. Some colleges also **superscore**: they take your best Reading and Writing score and your best Math score, even from different dates. Each college sets its own policy.

Older versions of the SAT subtracted a fraction of a point for wrong answers. The digital SAT does not. That is why a blank is never better than a guess: a guess has some chance to earn a point, and a blank has none. You will use this in the pacing lesson.

The 2 unscored questions per module are also called experimental. Their answers help College Board measure how hard a question is before it appears on a real test. They are not marked in any way. A question that feels strange is not necessarily one of them, so give every question your honest effort.
```

# Module 2 adapts
Module 1 decides how hard module 2 is.

```visual
digital-sat-format--adaptive
```

## Key ideas

In each section, **module 1** is a broad mix of easy, medium and hard questions. When you finish it, the test picks your module 2: **harder** if you did well, **easier** if you did not. (Each is harder or easier on average, so both still contain a mix.)

The test adapts **once per section**, after all 27 or 22 questions. A slow start cannot drop you into a spiral of easy questions. Reading and Writing and Math are routed **separately**: a hard R&W module 2 says nothing about Math.

Inside a module you can move freely: skip, flag and change answers. But when a module ends, you cannot go back to it, and you cannot return to R&W once Math begins.

Prep books report that the easier module 2 limits how high your section score can go, and the harder one is the route to scores near 800. College Board does not publish cutoffs. Either way, **module 1 matters most. Do not rush it.**

```theory
The digital SAT uses **multistage adaptive testing**. The earlier paper SAT was **linear**: everyone saw the same questions in the same order. An adaptive test changes during the test, based on how you are doing. The first module of a section is the **routing module**, because its result routes you to the next one.

Why adapt only once? College Board chose a simple two-stage design so that everyone starts at the same place, with a medium-difficulty module, and everyone gets a long chance (27 or 22 questions) to show what they know before anything changes. One early mistake cannot decide the rest of the test. Adapting also lets the test be shorter, about two hours instead of three, while still measuring your level accurately.

"On average" matters. The harder module 2 has some medium questions, and the easier one has some hard questions. Both modules are built to be fair, so two students with the same skill get comparable scores even if they see different questions.

You cannot tell which module 2 you received, and you should not try. A module 2 that feels harder usually means module 1 went well, but guessing about it only wastes attention. Use the same effort and the same pacing for every question.

The navigation rules are strict. Within a module, you may go back and forth. After time runs out, the module closes for good.
```

# Question order
Math climbs. R&W follows four groups.

```visual
digital-sat-format--order
```

## Key ideas

**Math:** each module runs from the easiest question to the hardest, whatever the topic. The early questions are quick, so earn time there. The last few are the toughest.

**Reading and Writing:** every module follows the same four groups, in this order:

| Order | Group | Questions per section (both modules) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Craft and Structure | 13 to 15 |
| 2 | Information and Ideas | 12 to 14 |
| 3 | Standard English Conventions | 11 to 15 |
| 4 | Expression of Ideas | 8 to 12 |

The counts are for the whole section (54 questions). Each module has 27 questions, so a module holds about half of each count.

Questions on the same skill sit together (all the Words in Context questions in a row, for example). Conventions is the exception: its skills are mixed.

Difficulty in R&W rises only **roughly**. College Board says R&W questions are not strictly ordered by difficulty, so a hard question can be followed by an easy one.

**Trap:** never assume that the next R&W question is harder or easier. If one question stalls you, guess, flag it and move on.

```theory
A **content domain** is a big area of the test. Each domain holds several **skills**, and each question tests one skill.

Craft and Structure covers Words in Context, Text Structure and Purpose, and Cross-Text Connections. Information and Ideas covers Central Ideas and Details, Command of Evidence (textual and quantitative), and Inferences. Standard English Conventions covers Boundaries, and Form, Structure and Sense. Expression of Ideas covers Rhetorical Synthesis and Transitions. The exact number of questions per skill changes from test to test.

The order helps you pace. College Board says Information and Ideas questions usually take the longest, Standard English Conventions questions the least time, and the other two fall in between. Seeing a block of Conventions questions is a chance to move quickly and bank time for the longer reading questions that came before.

In Math, the order is stricter. College Board says each module goes from easiest to hardest, regardless of domain. So working from the start to the end is a sensible plan: by question 18 or so, expect questions that are hard for most students. "Easy" and "hard" are relative to the typical student, not to you, so a topic you know well can feel easy even late in the module.

Some Math questions are word problems and take longer to read than pure equations, so a word problem early in the module is not necessarily slow.
```

# Bluebook tools
What the testing app lets you do.

```visual
digital-sat-format--tools-by-section
```

## Key ideas

Bluebook is the app that delivers the digital SAT. Learn its tools before test day. The names in your version may differ slightly, so learn what each tool does.

**Both sections**
- **Flag** a question to find it again. A flag is a bookmark, not an answer.
- **Cross out** answer choices you have ruled out.
- **Show or hide the timer.** It comes back when 5 minutes remain.
- **Review screen** at the end of a module: it lists flagged and unanswered questions and lets you jump to any question.
- **Zoom** in and out, and open the **test directions**.

**Reading and Writing only**
- **Annotate**: highlight text and add notes in the passage.

**Math only**
- The built-in **Desmos graphing calculator**. You may bring your own approved calculator instead.
- The **reference sheet** of formulas.

**Not part of Bluebook**
- **Scratch paper** is allowed for the whole test, in both sections. It is paper, not an app tool, so use it for working things out in Reading and Writing as well as Math.

```theory
Bluebook has two modes you can use before test day. **SAT Test Preview** is an untimed walk-through with sample questions, made to let you try the tools and the answer entry. **Full-length practice tests** are adaptive like the real test, so they also teach you how the modules feel.

Crossing out works on multiple-choice options only, and it is for you: it only marks the option so you can see what is left. Use it before you guess.

The timer can be hidden, which helps some students feel calmer, but it always returns at 5 minutes remaining. The review screen is part of Check Your Work, which appears when you reach the end of a module while there is still time. You can jump from it to any question in that module, and you can change answers until the clock ends.

The Math reference sheet lists common formulas: area and volume formulas, the Pythagorean theorem, special right triangles, and the number of degrees (360) or radians (\(2\pi\)) in a circle. It does not give formulas such as the quadratic formula, so learn those.

R&W has no calculator and no reference sheet, and questions there never ask you to calculate. Check College Board's calculator policy if you want to use your own device.

Colour contrast is set in your device's own settings before you start the test.
```

# Typed answers
No choices: type what the box can read.

```visual
digital-sat-format--typed
```

## Key ideas

About one Math question in four (roughly 11 of 44) is a **student-produced response (SPR)**: no options, you type the answer into a box. The box reads plain numbers:

- **One answer only.** If a question has two correct answers, enter either one.
- **No symbols.** No `$`, `%`, commas or units. Type `68132`, not `68,132`. Type `0.45`, not `45%`.
- **No mixed numbers.** For \(3\frac{1}{2}\), type `7/2` or `3.5`. The entry `3 1/2` is rejected, and `31/2` is read as thirty-one halves.
- A **negative** answer uses a minus sign.

Bluebook shows an **Answer Preview** under the box: how it read your entry. Compare it with what you meant.

All equivalent forms count: `7/2`, `3.5` and `3.50` are the same answer.

```example
Your work gives \(2\frac{3}{4}\). What can you type?
1. \(2 \cdot 4 + 3 = 11\), so \(\frac{11}{4}\) :: Turn the mixed number into one fraction.
2. Type `11/4`, or the decimal `2.75` :: Both are accepted.
3. Check Answer Preview :: It should show 11/4.
```

```theory
An SPR question has no options, so you cannot cross anything out. That does not make guessing useless: there is no penalty, so type your best number instead of leaving the box empty.

The box accepts digits, a decimal point, a slash for fractions and a minus sign. It rejects symbols such as a dollar sign, percent sign or comma, and it rejects words and units. If a question asks for money, type the number. If it asks for a percent, type the number without the percent sign. Check the question's last sentence for how the answer should be given: "in dollars", "in meters", "what is p" and so on.

A mixed number is the main trap. Write it as an improper fraction: whole number times the denominator, plus the numerator, over the denominator. Or use a decimal. The danger of typing `2 3/4` is that Bluebook rejects it, and the danger of typing `23/4` is that it reads twenty-three fourths, a different number.

Answer Preview is your safety net. It shows how Bluebook understood the entry. If you type `31/2` meaning three and a half, Preview shows thirty-one halves. Equivalent forms are all marked correct, so extra zeros do no harm, and a leading zero (`0.5` or `.5`) is fine.

Some questions have more than one correct answer, such as two solutions of an equation. You still type just one of them. Write your final answer on scratch paper so you can copy it carefully.
```

# Fill the answer box
Fractions that fit, decimals that fill.

```visual
digital-sat-format--decimal
```

## Key ideas

The answer box holds **5 characters**, or **6 for a negative answer**, because the minus sign counts. The decimal point and a leading zero count too.

Choose the form in this order:

1. **Does the fraction fit?** Then type it: `2/3`, `-1/3`, `7/12`.
2. **Fraction too long?** Type the decimal instead.
3. **A decimal that never ends must fill the box**, rounded or cut off at the fourth digit. For 2/3, `.6666`, `.6667`, `0.666` and `0.667` are accepted. `.66`, `.67` and `0.66` are rejected: too short.

A decimal that ends, such as `0.5` or `3.75`, is fine as it is.

```example
The answer is \(\frac{125}{37}\), about 3.378378... What do you type?
1. `125/37` has 6 characters :: Too long for 5 spaces.
2. The decimal does not end :: So fill the box.
3. Type `3.378` :: Exactly 5 characters.
```

```theory
The answer grid is small on purpose: it has room for 5 characters in a positive answer, and 6 in a negative one. College Board's rule is simple. If a fraction fits, enter it. If a fraction does not fit, enter the decimal. If a decimal does not fit, truncate or round it at the fourth digit.

For a decimal that never ends, accepted answers are rounded or cut off at the fourth digit and must fill the box. For 2/3 that gives `.6666` (cut off), `.6667` (rounded), and `0.666` and `0.667` (the same two with a leading zero). The entries `.66`, `.67`, `0.66` and `0.67` are rejected, because they are rounded too early and leave a space empty.

For a negative answer, the minus sign takes one of the 6 characters: `-.3333`, `-0.333`. For a decimal that ends, no rounding is needed: 0.25 and 0.375 are exact. A decimal such as 0.0625 has 6 characters, too many for a positive answer, but you can drop the leading zero and type `.0625` (5 characters).

Why does this matter? A tiny rounding difference can turn a correct solution into a wrong entry. Whenever you can type the exact fraction, do it: `7/9` is exact, while `.7778` is only close. Use the decimal only when the fraction is too long.

Always check Answer Preview before you move on.
```
