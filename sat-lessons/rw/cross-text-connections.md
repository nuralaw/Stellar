## What this question looks like

This is the only question on the test with **two texts**, labeled Text 1 and Text 2. Together they are at most about 150 words, and they are always about the same topic. Usually Text 1 presents a claim, a finding, a theory or an approach, and Text 2 brings in another researcher or writer who sees it differently: a new study, a different explanation, or a limit.

Typical question stems:

- "Based on the texts, how would [the author of Text 2, or a researcher named in Text 2] most likely respond to [the claim, finding, view or approach] in Text 1?" This is the most common form.
- "Based on the texts, how would [a person from Text 1] most likely respond to [the finding] in Text 2?" Here the direction is reversed.
- "Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?"
- "Based on the texts, how would [someone in Text 2] most likely describe [the view] in Text 1?"

The choices are usually full sentences, such as "By arguing that the evidence in Text 1 is incomplete" or "It explains part of the pattern but overlooks the role of diet."

**How many?** About 1–3 per test, at the end of the Craft and Structure questions in a module. College Board's own skill descriptions place this skill among the harder ones, mostly because the texts are dense, not because the logic is complicated. It's fine to spend a little longer here, about 75–90 seconds.

> **Key idea:** The two texts share a topic, but they almost never disagree about everything. Your job is to find the exact point where they meet (the *point of contact*) and decide what Text 2 does to that one point.

## The method

1. **Read the question first and note the direction.** Who is responding, and to what? Write it down as "2 → 1" or "1 → 2". Also note exactly which idea is named: "the hypothesis", "the conventional view", "the approach".
2. **Read Text 1 and shrink that idea to one short line.** For example: "T1: traffic noise makes city birds sing higher." Skip background details.
3. **Read Text 2 and find what it says about the same point.** That is the point of contact. Does Text 2 test the same claim? Question the evidence? Look at a different group or place? Explain the same facts another way? Write one line: "T2: quiet cities, same high songs."
4. **Name the relationship.** Agree, disagree, partly agree ("yes, but…"), extend (adds something new that fits), or different focus. Quick test: can both texts be fully true at the same time? If they can, it isn't a full disagreement.
5. **Predict the response in Text 2's voice,** including the reason: "Noise can't be the main cause, because birds in quiet cities sing high too." A prediction without a reason ("she disagrees") is not enough, because several choices will disagree.
6. **Match and eliminate.** The right choice matches both the relationship and the reason. Cross out choices that are too extreme, go in the wrong direction, describe only one text, bring in outside ideas, or have the right attitude for the wrong reason.

**For "both agree" questions:** look for a fact or idea that both texts accept, usually something modest in the background, not the point they argue about. Then test each choice by asking, "Would *each* author sign this sentence?" A statement only one author makes is wrong.

> **Tip:** Text 2 often never mentions Text 1. A new study that points somewhere else still counts as a response. Ask: "If the Text 2 author read Text 1, which sentence would they object to, and why?"

## Rules and formulas you need

### The five relationships

| Relationship | What Text 2 does | Signals in Text 2 | Typical right answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agree | supports or confirms Text 1's point | "likewise", "consistent with", new data pointing the same way | "would see the finding as further support for…" |
| Disagree | says Text 1's claim or explanation is wrong | "however", "contrary to", "found no", "in fact" | "would argue that the explanation is not supported, since…" |
| Partly agree (qualify) | accepts part of it but adds a limit or condition | "but only", "may", "in some cases", "overlooks", "not necessarily" | "would agree that X, but caution that it holds only when…" |
| Extend | builds on it with a new example, cause or effect | "also", "further", "this suggests" | "would add that…" |
| Different focus | discusses another side of the topic, with no real conflict | a different question, group or time | "would point out that X is only part of the picture" |

Disagreement and partial agreement are the most common relationships on the Digital SAT, and partial agreement produces the most traps: choices that say "completely agrees" or "completely rejects" look tempting when the real answer is "yes, but…".

### Where the texts meet: points of contact

| Point of contact | What Text 2 might say about Text 1 |
|---|---|
| The claim or conclusion | "That conclusion is wrong" (or right). |
| The evidence | "That evidence is too thin, too indirect or not representative." |
| The method | "That kind of test can't show what happens in real conditions." |
| The scope | "That's true for this group, place or time, but not for all of them." |
| The explanation of the same facts | "The pattern is real, but something else causes it." |
| The proposed next step | "The finding is sound, but the suggested solution won't work." |

> **Remember:** Agreeing about the facts while disagreeing about the explanation is a very common pattern. When Text 2 confirms Text 1's observation, don't jump to "agree". Check what Text 2 says about the *reason*.

### Wording in the choices

- **Extreme words** to distrust unless the text is just as strong: *completely, entirely, always, never, only, all, proves, disproves, refutes, worthless, impossible*.
- **Measured words** that often appear in right answers: *may, likely, partly, some, insufficient, premature, overstated, incomplete, does not fully account for*.
- **Direction check:** in a "2 → 1" question, the right answer gives Text 2's view of Text 1's idea. A choice that states Text 1's own view, or swaps who said what, goes the wrong way.
- **Reversed direction:** when Text 1's author responds to Text 2, remember that this author never read Text 2. Apply Text 1's reasoning to Text 2's idea: "Given what Text 1 believes, what would its author say about this?"

## Worked examples

The texts below were written for this lesson. The people and studies in them are invented for practice, though the topics are real.

### Example 1: Responding to an explanation

**Text 1**

Birds in cities often sing at a higher pitch than birds of the same species in the countryside. Many ecologists explain this with traffic noise: because the roar of engines is low-pitched, a higher song is easier for other birds to hear over it.

**Text 2**

Biologist Mira Okafor recorded great tits in nine European cities. Three of the cities had strict limits on car traffic and were nearly as quiet as the countryside around them. Yet the birds in these quiet cities sang at the same high pitch as birds in the noisiest cities, a result that Okafor says other features of city life must explain.

Based on the texts, how would Okafor (Text 2) most likely respond to the explanation offered by the ecologists in Text 1?

- (A) She would accept the explanation, since her recordings confirm that city birds sing at a higher pitch than birds in the nearby countryside.
- (B) She would claim that city birds and country birds actually sing at the same pitch.
- (C) She would argue that traffic noise cannot be the main explanation, since birds sing at a high pitch even in cities with little traffic noise.
- (D) She would suggest that the explanation is correct only for cities outside Europe.

**Solution.** Direction: Okafor (2) → the ecologists' explanation (1). T1: "high songs because of traffic noise." T2 on the same point: in quiet cities the songs are just as high, so noise isn't what causes them. The point of contact is the *cause*, not the pattern: both texts agree that city birds sing higher; they disagree about why. Prediction: "Noise can't be the reason, because quiet cities have high songs too."

- (A) The trap. Okafor does confirm the pattern, but the question asks about the explanation, and her quiet-city result goes against it.
- (B) Her own recordings show the opposite: city birds sing higher.
- (C) Matches the relationship (disagreeing about the cause) and the reason (the quiet cities).
- (D) Nothing in Text 2 is about cities outside Europe. An invented limit.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: Responding to an argument

**Text 1**

When a large software company let its customer-service staff work from home, the number of calls each worker handled rose by about 10 percent. Economists who studied the change argue that the results show remote work can make employees more productive, since workers at home take fewer breaks and face fewer interruptions from coworkers.

**Text 2**

Organizational psychologist Daniel Reyes accepts that remote workers often get more routine work done. But his surveys of engineers at several firms found that new hires who worked mostly from home learned their jobs more slowly than new hires who worked in an office, largely because they had fewer chances to watch and ask experienced colleagues.

Based on the texts, how would Reyes (Text 2) most likely respond to the economists' argument in Text 1?

- (A) By rejecting it, since his surveys show that remote workers complete less work than office workers
- (B) By arguing that the economists' results can't be trusted, because customer-service work is easier to count than the work engineers do
- (C) By agreeing that remote work benefits all employees equally, including new hires
- (D) By accepting that remote work can raise output on routine tasks while warning that it may slow how quickly new employees learn

**Solution.** Direction: Reyes (2) → the economists' argument (1). T1: "remote work makes workers more productive." T2: "accepts" that remote workers get more routine work done (the yes), "But" new hires learn more slowly at home (the but). Relationship: partly agree. Prediction: "True for routine output, but new hires learn more slowly from home."

- (A) Too extreme, and it misreports his data. He accepts that remote workers get more routine work done; his surveys measured how fast new hires learn, not how much work gets done.
- (B) Reyes never doubts the economists' results: he "accepts" that remote workers get more routine work done. The complaint about counting work sounds reasonable, but the text doesn't make it.
- (C) The opposite of his finding about new hires.
- (D) Matches both halves: the yes and the but.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: What both authors would accept

**Text 1**

The ancient city of Mohenjo-daro, in what is now Pakistan, was largely abandoned nearly 4,000 years ago. Some archaeologists blame a sudden shift in the course of a major river: as the water moved away, they argue, farming near the city collapsed, and residents had little choice but to leave.

**Text 2**

Historian Lena Varga points out that Mohenjo-daro's decline was slow. Over several centuries, its buildings were repaired less carefully and its population shrank, long before the city finally emptied. She argues that a gradual loss of trade with distant regions, not a single sudden event, best explains why residents left.

Based on the texts, the archaeologists in Text 1 and Varga in Text 2 would most likely agree with which statement?

- (A) Mohenjo-daro was eventually abandoned by most of its residents.
- (B) A change in a river's course caused farming near Mohenjo-daro to fail.
- (C) Mohenjo-daro declined gradually over several centuries.
- (D) The loss of long-distance trade was the main reason residents left Mohenjo-daro.

**Solution.** The two texts argue about *why* people left and *how fast*. So look for what both take for granted: that people did leave. Text 1 says the city was "largely abandoned"; Text 2 says it "finally emptied". Prediction: "The city was eventually abandoned."

- (A) Both texts state it. Modest, shared and correct.
- (B) This is only Text 1's explanation. Varga argues that no single sudden event explains the decline, so she wouldn't sign this.
- (C) This is only Text 2's view. Text 1 describes a sudden cause.
- (D) This is only Text 2's explanation.

**Answer:** (A)

### Example 4: A response that never mentions the claim

**Text 1**

Skeletons from an early farming village in the Jordan Valley are, on average, several centimeters shorter than skeletons from hunter-gatherer sites in the same region. A team of archaeologists argues that the switch to farming harmed people's health: diets built on a few grain crops, the team says, gave villagers less protein and fewer nutrients than hunter-gatherers ate.

**Text 2**

Anthropologist Samir Haddad notes that nearly all of the village skeletons studied so far come from a single burial ground next to the village's grain stores, where workers who did the heaviest labor appear to have been buried. At other villages from that period, he points out, wealthier people were usually buried in a separate cemetery, and no such cemetery has yet been excavated at this village.

Based on the texts, how would Haddad (Text 2) most likely respond to the team's argument in Text 1?

- (A) He would argue that hunter-gatherers in the region were actually shorter than early farmers.
- (B) He would warn that the team's conclusion may be premature, because the skeletons studied may not represent the village as a whole.
- (C) He would agree that grain-based diets were low in protein but insist that farming improved health in other ways.
- (D) He would argue that the team's height measurements were inaccurate and should be repeated.

**Solution.** Direction: Haddad (2) → the team's argument (1). T1: "farmers were shorter, so farming harmed health." Text 2 never mentions diet or health. It talks about *which* skeletons were studied, so the point of contact is the evidence. If the sample comes mostly from the hardest-working laborers, it may not show what was typical for the whole village. Relationship: a doubt about the evidence, not a counter-claim. Prediction: "Your sample may be one-sided, so don't draw that conclusion yet."

- (A) Haddad gives no information about hunter-gatherer height. An outside claim.
- (B) Matches the point of contact (the sample) and the strength: Haddad raises a doubt; he doesn't prove the team wrong.
- (C) Haddad says nothing about diet or about any health benefits of farming. Invented.
- (D) Right attitude (doubt about the evidence), wrong reason. He questions which skeletons were studied, not how they were measured.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 5: When Text 1 does the responding

**Text 1**

Critics often call the late novels of Ada Lindqvist "plain," and they are right. Compared with her early books, the sentences are short and the descriptions are thin. Some readers will say this simplicity was intentional, but an author's intention does not change the experience of reading: a flat page is a flat page, whatever the writer hoped it would be.

**Text 2**

Letters discovered in 2019 show that Lindqvist stripped down her late style on purpose. Writing to her editor, she explained that she wanted her books to reach factory workers with little schooling, readers she felt she had ignored early in her career. Seen in this light, the plainness of the late novels is not a failure of skill but a choice.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 1 most likely respond to the conclusion in Text 2?

- (A) By accepting that the plain style was deliberate while insisting that this does not make the late novels more rewarding to read
- (B) By arguing that the letters are probably not genuine, since Lindqvist never meant to write simply
- (C) By agreeing that the late novels are Lindqvist's best work once her purpose is understood
- (D) By agreeing that the plainness was a choice and concluding that critics should stop calling the late novels plain

**Solution.** The direction is reversed: Text 1's author → Text 2's conclusion. Text 1's author never saw the letters, so apply Text 1's own reasoning. Text 1 already expects the argument "it was intentional" and answers it: intention "does not change the experience of reading". Text 2 proves the intention, which is exactly the point Text 1 says doesn't matter. Prediction: "Fine, she meant it. The books are still flat."

- (A) Matches. It grants the fact (the style was deliberate) and keeps Text 1's judgment.
- (B) Text 1 never doubts that the plainness could be intentional; it says intention is beside the point. Nothing suggests the letters are fake.
- (C) The opposite of Text 1's judgment.
- (D) Half right. Text 1 could grant that the plainness was a choice, but it says the critics are "right" to call the novels plain, so it would never tell them to stop.

**Answer:** (A)

## Traps to avoid

- **Agreeing with the pattern, not the point.** Text 2 may confirm Text 1's facts while rejecting its explanation (Example 1, choice A). Ask what exactly is being judged.
- **All or nothing.** "Completely agrees" or "completely rejects" when Text 2 really says "yes, but…" (Example 2, choice A).
- **Wrong direction.** The choice gives Text 1's view of Text 2 when the question asks the reverse, or puts one author's opinion in the other author's mouth.
- **One-text answer.** True about one text but silent about how the texts connect, or, in "both agree" questions, something only one author would accept (Example 3).
- **Outside knowledge.** A response that sounds smart but uses facts neither text gives.
- **Right attitude, wrong reason.** The choice disagrees, as Text 2 does, but for a reason Text 2 never gives (Example 4, choice D).
- **Overreach.** Text 2 only raises a doubt ("may not represent"), but the choice says Text 2 proves Text 1 wrong.

## Quick checklist

- Did I mark the direction: who responds to what?
- Can I state the Text 1 idea in one short line?
- Did I find the point of contact: claim, evidence, method, scope or explanation?
- Did I name the relationship and test it: can both texts be true at once?
- Does my choice match the relationship *and* the reason, at the right strength?
- For "both agree": would each author sign this sentence?
