> **Key idea:** What changes between the choices tells you the rule; then find the one word that controls it (the subject, the noun a pronoun replaces, a time clue) and match it.

## What this question looks like

A Form, Structure, and Sense question gives you a short text with one blank, and the words in the choices change, not just the punctuation. The question is the same one Boundaries uses: *Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?*

Here's how to tell the two apart:

- **Boundaries:** all four choices have the same words. Only the commas, periods, colons and so on change.
- **Form, Structure, and Sense:** the words themselves change.

Typical sets of choices:

- four versions of one verb: *reveal / reveals / revealing / to reveal*
- four pronouns: *it / its / their / they're*
- a noun with and without an apostrophe: *scientists / scientist's / scientists'*
- the same idea in four different word orders

These questions belong to **Standard English Conventions**, about a quarter of the Reading and Writing section. Only about seven rules show up, and they repeat constantly.

## The method

Your ear is easy to fool when a long phrase sits in the way, so work by rule, not by sound. Run these steps every time:

1. **Name what changes.** Before reading the passage, glance at the four choices and name the difference. The triage table below tells you which rule that is.
2. **Find the controlling word.** For a verb, it's the subject. For a pronoun, it's the noun it replaces. For tense, it's a time clue or the other verbs. For a modifier, it's the noun right after the comma.
3. **Strip the sentence down.** In your head (or with the Bluebook highlighter), cross out everything between the subject and the verb: *of, in, with, along with, such as* phrases, *who, which, that* clauses, and anything inside commas or dashes. What's left is *subject + verb*.
4. **Predict the form.** Before looking back, decide what you need: "singular verb," "a main verb," "plural possessive," "the person who did the action."
5. **Check the winner in the full sentence.** Make sure it doesn't create a new problem, like a second main verb or a fragment.

> **Tip:** If three choices share a feature and one doesn't (three plural verbs and one singular), the odd one out is often correct. Use this to double-check, not as a replacement for finding the subject.

## Rules and formulas you need

### Triage: what changes tells you what's tested

| Choices change | Rule tested | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| *is / are*, *shows / show* | Subject–verb agreement | Real subject: one or many? |
| *showed / shows / will show / had shown* | Verb tense | Time clues, nearby verbs |
| *shows / showing / to show / having shown* | Verb form | Is there already a main verb? |
| *it / its / they / their* | Pronoun agreement | The noun it replaces |
| *its / it's*, apostrophes | Plurals and possessives | Is something owned? By how many? |
| Same words, new order | Modifier placement | Who does the opening phrase describe? |
| *to study / studying / studied* in a list | Parallel structure | Form of the other items |

### Subject–verb agreement

A singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. Watch the flip: in the present tense, the singular verb usually ends in -s (*the bird sings*, *the birds sing*).

| Tricky subject | Treat it as | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Noun + *of* phrase | The first noun | The *box* of old photos **is** heavy. |
| Group word (*team, committee, collection, series, flock*) | Singular | The *committee* **has** voted. |
| *each, every, either, neither, one of the ___* | Singular | *One* of the bridges **was** closed. |
| Two nouns joined by *and* | Plural | The *map and the diary* **were** found. |
| *or / nor* | The nearer noun | Neither the coach nor the *players* **were** ready. |
| An *-ing* activity | Singular | *Measuring* glaciers **takes** years. |
| *The number of* | Singular | The number of visitors **has** grown. |
| *A number of* | Plural | A number of visitors **have** left. |
| Verb before subject | Flip it back | Along the wall **hang** three *paintings*. |

### Verb form: does the sentence need a main verb?

Every sentence needs at least one **finite verb**: a real working verb with a tense and a subject (*reports, reported, has reported, is reporting*). **Nonfinite** forms can't run a sentence on their own: *reporting* (with no helper like *is*), *to report* and *having reported*.

| Situation | Blank needs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Subject, but no main verb yet | A finite verb | The researchers, after years of fieldwork, **published** their findings. |
| Main verb done; blank adds detail after a comma | Usually an *-ing* form | The river flooded the valley, **forcing** families to move. |
| Noun *receives* the action | Past participle (-ed / -en) | a bridge **built** in 1890 |
| Noun *does* the action | Present participle (-ing) | a bridge **connecting** two towns |

After *has / have / had*, use the past participle: *had begun*, not *had began*; *has written*, not *has wrote*; *has gone*, not *has went*.

### Verb tense

Match the timeline the passage has already set. Look for time clues first; if there are none, copy the tense of the nearby verbs.

| Clue | Tense | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *in 1850, last year, ago* | Simple past | She **discovered** the fossil in 2004. |
| *since, for the past decade, so far* | Present perfect (*has/have* + participle) | Prices **have risen** since 2020. |
| One past action before another | Past perfect (*had* + participle) | By the time help arrived, the fire **had spread**. |
| A general fact, or what a book or study says | Simple present | The novel **follows** two sisters. |

### Pronoun agreement

A pronoun must match the noun it replaces in number. Find that noun, label it singular or plural, then pick.

| Noun it replaces | Use | Not |
|---|---|---|
| One thing, company, team, museum, city | *it, its, this, that* | *they, their, these, those* |
| Two or more things or people | *they, their, these, those* | *it, its* |

- **Comparisons:** "Her scores were higher than **those** of her classmates" (*those* = scores, plural).
- **Case** (*he* vs. *him*, *who* vs. *whom*) is rare on the digital test. If it shows up, drop the other person: "The award went to Maya and ___" becomes "went to ___," and you'd say *me*, not *I*.

### Plurals, possessives, and look-alike words

An apostrophe on a noun shows ownership. It never makes a plain plural.

| Meaning | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| More than one, nothing owned | -s, no apostrophe | the *scientists* agreed |
| One owner | -'s | one *scientist's* notebook |
| Several owners (plural ending in s) | -s' | three *scientists'* notebooks |
| Plural not ending in s | -'s | the *children's* drawings |

Possessive pronouns never use apostrophes. The apostrophe versions are contractions (two words squeezed into one).

| Owns something | Contraction | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| *its* | *it's* = it is / it has | Say "it is." Sounds wrong? Use *its*. |
| *their* | *they're* = they are | Say "they are." |
| *whose* | *who's* = who is | Say "who is." |
| *your* | *you're* = you are | Say "you are." |

> **Tip:** To decide on an apostrophe, look at the word right after the noun. If it's a thing being owned (*the bees' hive*), you need a possessive. If it's a verb (*the bees swarm*), you need a plain plural.

### Modifier placement

When a sentence opens with a describing phrase and a comma ("Raised in Lagos, ___"), the very next noun must be the one that phrase describes. Ask *who* was raised in Lagos; that person must come right after the comma.

### Parallel structure

Items in a list or pair must have the same form: *to plan, to build, and to test* or *planning, building, and testing*, never *planning, to build, and tested*. Pairs like *not only… but also* and *either… or* need matching forms on both sides.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Agreement with a long interrupter

The collection of handwritten letters, maps, and diaries that historian Ana Ruiz donated to the city archive ___ new details about how the town was founded.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) reveal
- (B) have revealed
- (C) reveals
- (D) are revealing

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices change between singular and plural, so this is subject–verb agreement. Step 3: strip the sentence. Cross out "of handwritten letters, maps, and diaries" and "that historian Ana Ruiz donated to the city archive." What's left: "The collection ___ new details." *Collection* is one group, so it's singular and needs a singular verb.

- (A) *reveal* is plural. It matches *diaries* or *letters*, the trap nouns.
- (B) *have revealed* is plural (singular would be *has revealed*).
- (C) *reveals* is singular. Correct.
- (D) *are revealing* is plural.

Notice that three choices are plural and one is singular, which fits the odd-one-out tip.

**Answer:** (C)

### Example 2: Missing main verb

In 2019, a team of marine biologists studying coral reefs off the coast of Belize ___ that some corals recover from bleaching faster when parrotfish are present.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) reporting
- (B) to report
- (C) having reported
- (D) reported

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices are four forms of *report*, mixing real verbs with *-ing* and *to* forms. That's a verb-form question, so ask: does this sentence have a main verb yet? The subject is "a team." The word *studying* only describes the biologists; it isn't the main verb. Nothing tells us what the team *did*, so the blank must be a finite verb. The clue "In 2019" also points to the past.

- (A) *reporting* with no helper can't be a main verb, so the sentence would be a fragment.
- (B) *to report* can't be a main verb either. Fragment.
- (C) *having reported* only describes; it can't run a sentence. Fragment.
- (D) *reported* is a finite past-tense verb, and it works for a singular subject. Correct.

**Answer:** (D)

### Example 3: Pronoun plus possessive

The Kessler Botanical Garden recently reopened ___ glass conservatory, which had been closed for repairs after a winter storm cracked several of the panes.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) it's
- (B) its
- (C) their
- (D) they're

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices mix pronouns and apostrophes, so check two things: number and possession. Step 2: the pronoun replaces "The Kessler Botanical Garden," which is one place, so it's singular. The conservatory *belongs to* the garden, so we need a possessive.

- (A) *it's* means "it is." "Reopened it is glass conservatory" makes no sense.
- (B) *its* is singular and possessive. Correct.
- (C) *their* is possessive but plural. The garden is one place, so it doesn't match.
- (D) *they're* means "they are," and it's plural. Wrong on both counts.

**Answer:** (B)

### Example 4: Who is doing the action?

Having spent three summers recording bird calls in the Andes, ___

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

- (A) the recordings that Priya Nair made were eventually turned into a free online library.
- (B) Priya Nair's recordings were eventually turned into a free online library.
- (C) Priya Nair eventually turned her recordings into a free online library.
- (D) a free online library was eventually built from Priya Nair's recordings.

**Solution**

Step 1: the choices say roughly the same thing in a different order, so this is modifier placement. Step 2: ask who "spent three summers recording bird calls." A person did, Priya Nair. So her name, as the subject, must come right after the comma.

- (A) puts *the recordings* right after the comma, which says the recordings spent three summers recording. Illogical.
- (B) is the sneaky one. *Priya Nair's* is there, but it's only describing the real subject, which is *recordings*. Recordings still didn't do the recording.
- (C) puts *Priya Nair* right after the comma. Correct.
- (D) says a library spent three summers recording bird calls. Illogical.

**Answer:** (C)

## Traps to avoid

- **Matching the nearest noun.** The test loves to park a noun of the opposite number right before the blank. In "The results of the study ___," the nearest noun is *study*, but the subject is *results*.
- **Picking the verb that "flows."** An *-ing* form often sounds smooth, but if the sentence has no other main verb, it leaves you with a fragment. Ask "what did the subject *do*?" and make sure some word answers that.
- **Adding a second main verb.** If the sentence already has its main verb, a finite verb after a comma breaks the sentence. You usually need an *-ing* form there instead.
- **Using *their* for one company, team or group.** People say "the company changed their policy," but on the SAT a single organization is *it/its*.
- **The possessive in a modifier answer.** "Born in Ohio, the *author's* first novel…" looks close, but the subject is *novel*. The person has to be the subject itself, not an owner tucked in front of it.
- **Apostrophes on plain plurals.** *The 1990's*, *the scientist's agreed*, *it's wings*: if nothing is owned, or if it's a pronoun, no apostrophe.
- **Shifting tense for no reason.** If the passage is in the past and nothing signals a change, a present or future choice is wrong.
- **Judging by length or fanciness.** The right form can be the shortest choice (*reveals*) or a longer one (*had been completed*). Only the controlling word decides.

## Quick checklist

- What changes between the four choices? Name the rule.
- Verb blank: what's the real subject, once you cross out the extras?
- Does the sentence already have a main verb, or does it still need one?
- Is there a time clue, or a tense set by nearby verbs?
- Pronoun: what exact noun does it replace, and is that one thing or many?
- Apostrophe: is something actually owned, and by one owner or several?
- Opening phrase: is the noun right after the comma the one doing that action?
