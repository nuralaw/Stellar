# Two full sentences
Period, semicolon, or comma + and/but/so.

## Two sentences, strong mark

Test both sides of the blank. **Two full sentences take a period, a semicolon, or comma + *and/but/so*.** Never a comma alone.

```example
The rain ______ the game began.
Choices: stopped, / stopped; / stopped
1. Before: "The rain stopped" :: A full sentence.
2. After: "the game began" :: Also a full sentence.
3. Answer: stopped; :: A semicolon joins two full sentences.
```

## Test both sides

```quiz
The trail was ______ we reached the summit by noon.
- steep, :: Comma alone between two sentences: a comma splice.
* steep, but
- steep but, :: The comma goes before *but*, not after it.
> "The trail was steep" and "we reached the summit by noon" are full sentences. Comma + *but* joins them.
```

# However and therefore
They can't join sentences. Semicolon first.

## However can't join sentences

*However* and *therefore* are not *and/but/so*. Between two sentences, put a semicolon or period before them.

```example
The store was ______ we waited outside.
Choices: closed, however, / closed; however, / closed however
1. "The store was closed" + "we waited outside" :: Two full sentences.
2. closed, however, :: Only commas: a comma splice.
3. Answer: closed; however, :: Semicolon before *however*, comma after.
```

## Mark before however

```quiz
The first flight ended in a ______ the second went perfectly.
- crash, however, :: Comma splice: *however* can't join two sentences.
- crash however, :: Run-on: no mark separates the two sentences.
* crash; however,
> Both sides are full sentences. Semicolon before *however*, comma after it.
```

# Colons
Full sentence before, list or explanation after.

## Colon: full sentence first

Before a colon: a full sentence. After it: a list or an explanation.

```example
She packed two ______ a map and a compass.
Choices: things: / things; / things.
1. Before: "She packed two things" :: A full sentence.
2. After: "a map and a compass" :: A list, not a sentence.
3. Answer: things: :: The colon introduces the list.
```

## Introduce the list

```quiz
The recipe needs only three ______ flour, water, and salt.
* ingredients:
- ingredients; :: A semicolon needs a full sentence after it too.
- ingredients. :: "Flour, water, and salt." has no verb: a fragment.
- ingredients :: Nothing introduces the list, so it runs into the sentence.
> The left side is a full sentence, and the colon hands over the list it promised.
```

# Matching pairs
Extra detail: two commas or two dashes.

## Close what you opened

Extra detail in mid-sentence takes a matching pair: two commas or two dashes.

```example
My sister, a nurse in ______ works nights.
Choices: Boston / Boston, / Boston—
1. Remove the detail: "My sister works nights." :: Still a full sentence.
2. The detail opened with a comma :: Close it the same way.
3. Answer: Boston, :: Two commas make the pair.
```

## Find the opening mark

```quiz
The axolotl—a salamander that keeps its gills as an ______ can regrow lost legs.
- adult, :: A dash opened the detail. A comma can't close it.
- adult :: Nothing closes the detail, so it runs into "can regrow."
* adult—
> Remove the detail: "The axolotl can regrow lost legs." It opened with a dash, so it closes with a dash.
```

# Trap: the pause comma
No mark between a subject and its verb.

## Long subject, no comma

A long subject invites a pause. Never put one mark between a subject and its verb.

```example
The books on the top ______ belong to Maya.
Choices: shelf, / shelf: / shelf
1. Subject: "The books on the top shelf" :: Long, but one subject.
2. Verb: "belong" :: It comes right after the subject.
3. Answer: shelf :: No mark between subject and verb.
```

## Spot the subject

```quiz
The stone tower at the edge of the old fishing ______ was built in 1642.
- village, :: Trap: one comma splits the subject from *was built*.
- village; :: The left side has no verb, so it isn't a sentence.
* village
> Subject: "The stone tower … village." Verb: "was built." Nothing goes between them.
```
